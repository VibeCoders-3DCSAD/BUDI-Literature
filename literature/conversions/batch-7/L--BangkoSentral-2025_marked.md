ii | 2025 BSP Annual Report
2025 was a year of shifting narratives.
The ﬁrst half carried the momentum of 2024. Inﬂation kept easing, 
hitting a record low of 0.9 percent in July. That gave the Bangko 
Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP) room to keep the key policy rate at  
4.5 percent. Growth held close to its strong 2024 pace. 
The second half told a different story. Government reforms tied to 
the ﬂood control controversy slowed public infrastructure spending. 
As uncertainty spread, business and consumer sentiment softened. 
Growth eased to 4.4 percent for the full year. Inﬂation stayed below 
target, averaging 1.7 percent, which helped support investments.  
The good news is that the banking system remained in robust health. 
Banks were well-capitalized and were ﬂush with liquidity. The banking 
system continued to support lending and economic activity. 
That resilience was no accident. Years of prudent supervision and 
regulation ensured that our banks were in a good position to absorb 
shocks. 
Even as conditions grew more challenging, the BSP pressed forward. 
Alongside our core mandates, the BSP continued to pursue initiatives 
that reﬂect our commitment to innovation, inclusion, and integrity.

Innovation
Good policy needs good and timely information. In 2025, the BSP 
stepped up its use of artiﬁcial intelligence (AI) to better track a rapidly 
changing environment. We employed large language models to 
measure sentiment and monitor trade ﬂows. These allowed us to 
formulate a more data-driven monetary policy that is ahead of the 
curve.  
In payments, the BSP advanced the adoption of ISO 20022.  
This is a global standard that makes both domestic and cross-border 
transactions more transparent and secure. We also made progress 
in cross-border payments through the incorporation of Nexus Global 
Payments in Singapore. Once fully operational, Project Nexus will 
link instant payment systems of member countries. This will enable 
seamless cross-border transactions.  
 
We also strengthened cybersecurity. The BSP developed and 
tested the Cybersecurity Incident Response Plan for PhilPaSSplus,  
our real-time gross settlement system. During the year, the system 
processed a record /uni20B1601.3 trillion in transactions at a 99.9 percent 
efﬁciency rate. Meanwhile, transactions on InstaPay, the low-value 
instant payment system, surged to 4.7 billion worth /uni20B111.6 trillion.

Inclusion
More Filipinos now have access to ﬁnancial services than ever before. 
Account ownership among Filipino households increased to  
85.0 percent. Beyond gaining access to digital ﬁnancial services,  
Filipinos also became more conﬁdent in using them in daily life.
The Paleng-QR Ph Plus Program expanded to more than 900 local 
government units. This brought the beneﬁts of digital payments to 
market vendors, transport drivers, microentrepreneurs, and informal 
workers across the country.
But access is only a starting point. The BSP elevated ﬁnancial health 
as a Priority Economic Deliverable (PED) for the 2026 ASEAN Summit, 
which is chaired by the Philippines. 
 
Integrity
Integrity remained the foundation of trust in the ﬁnancial system.  
In February, the Philippines exited the Financial Action Task Force’s 
anti-money laundering grey list. This made it easier and cheaper 
for businesses and individuals, especially overseas Filipino workers, 
to remit funds. We also exited the high-risk jurisdiction lists of the 
European Union and the United Kingdom.  
In September, to further strengthen the integrity of the ﬁnancial 
system, the BSP set new rules for withdrawals. The rules are 
designed to curb money laundering and other criminal activity while 
minimizing impact on businesses and individuals with legitimate 
and regular transactions. We supported this by strengthening the 
standards for external auditors and raising reporting requirements for  
non-bank ﬁnancial institutions such as pawnshops and money service 
businesses.

In August, responding to growing public concern, the BSP required 
banks, electronic wallet operators, and other BSP-supervised ﬁnancial 
institutions to remove links to online gambling sites. 
With these measures, we ensure that as ﬁnancial services become 
more accessible and innovative, the system remains credible and 
worthy of public trust.
Looking ahead
The economy is now navigating new headwinds. The Middle East 
conﬂict early in 2026 pushed inﬂation higher, prompting the BSP to 
raise rates. 
Fortunately, we remain on solid footing. Inﬂation is rising from a 
low base. Our banks also remain strong, ready to absorb shocks and 
sustain lending. 
None of this was achieved alone. Our stakeholders and partner 
agencies all played their part. So did the Monetary Board and every 
BSP ofﬁcer and staff. 
Our combined efforts put us in a good position to weather the current 
challenges. I am conﬁdent that continued collaboration will enable 
us to navigate these conditions and further strengthen the Philippine 
economy and the ﬁnancial system for the beneﬁt of all Filipinos.

About the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas
The BSP is the country’s independent central monetary authority mandated to promote 
price stability, help maintain stability of the financial system, and oversee the country’s 
payments and settlements system.
Section 20, Article XII, 1987 Philippine Constitution
The Congress shall establish an independent central monetary authority…  
[which] shall provide policy direction in the areas of money, banking, and credit.  
It shall have supervision over the operations of banks and exercise such regulatory 
powers as may be provided by law over the operations of finance companies and 
other institutions performing similar functions.
Section 1, Article 1, Chapter 1, Republic Act No. 7653   
(The New Central Bank Act) as amended by Republic Act No. 11211
The State shall maintain a central monetary authority that shall function and 
operate as an independent and accountable body corporate in the discharge 
of its mandated responsibilities concerning money, banking, and credit. In line 
with this policy, and considering its unique functions and responsibilities, the central 
monetary authority established under this Act, while being a government-owned 
corporation, shall enjoy fiscal and administrative autonomy.

Legal Basis of Establishment and Legal Mandate
The BSP is the central bank of the Republic of the Philippines. Section 20, 
Article XII of the 1987 Constitution mandated Congress to establish an 
independent central monetary authority tasked to provide policy direction 
in the areas of money, banking, and credit. Pursuant to this constitutionally 
mandated responsibility, Congress established the BSP through the 
enactment of Republic Act No. 7653 in 1993.
Powers and Functions
As the Philippines’ central monetary authority, the BSP is responsible,  
among others, for:
• maintaining price stability conducive to a balanced and sustainable 
growth of the economy and employment;
• promoting and maintaining monetary stability and the convertibility of 
the peso;
• promoting financial stability by closely working with the national 
government, the Department of Finance, the Securities and Exchange 
Commission, the Insurance Commission, and the Philippine Deposit 
Insurance Corporation;
• overseeing the payments and settlements system, including 
critical financial market infrastructures, to promote sound and  
prudent practices; and
• promoting broad and convenient access to high quality financial 
services for the interest of the general public.

 Liquidity management. The BSP formulates and implement monetary policy 
aimed at influencing money supply, consistent with its primary mandate of 
maintaining price stability.
 Currency issue. The BSP has the exclusive power to issue the national currency.  
All notes and coins issued by the BSP are fully guaranteed by the 
government and are considered legal tender for all private and  
public debts.
  
Lender of last resort. The BSP extends discounts, loans, and advances to 
banking institutions for liquidity purposes.
 
Financial supervision. The BSP supervises banks and exercises regulatory and 
examination powers over non-bank institutions performing quasi-banking 
functions, money service businesses, credit granting businesses, and payment 
system operators.
 Management of foreign currency reserves. The BSP seeks to maintain 
sufficient international reserves to meet any foreseeable net demands for 
foreign currencies to preserve the international stability and convertibility of 
the Philippine peso.
  
Determination of exchange rate policy. The BSP determines the exchange 
rate policy of the Philippines. Currently, it adheres to a market-oriented foreign 
exhange rate policy to ensure orderly conditions in the market.
  
Bond issue. As part of monetary policy, the BSP may issue, place, buy, and sell 
freely negotiable evidences of indebtedness.
  
Banker of the government. The BSP functions as the banker,  
financial advisor, and official depository of the government, its political 
subdivisions and instrumentalities, and government-owned and -controlled 
corporations.

Enterprise Strategy
Following the launch of the BSP Enterprise Strategy in 2024, the Bank’s priority in 2025 was 
clear: translate strategic direction into disciplined, coordinated execution across all operating 
units. This required converting strategic priorities into measurable, shared commitments; 
maintaining organizational alignment; and establishing systematic review mechanisms to 
track progress and address emerging gaps. 
 
Effective strategy orchestration keeps the organization moving as one. It strengthens 
accountability, supports timely decision-making, and advances the BSP’s objectives of 
fostering innovation, promoting financial inclusion, and upholding institutional integrity.

Strengthening strategy delivery 
through project management 
discipline
Project management discipline is fundamental to strategy delivery. It provides structure 
to complex, interdependent work by clarifying accountabilities, identifying risks early, and 
aligning project delivery with intended outcomes.
In 2025, the BSP strengthened project discipline by developing new Guidelines on 
Post-Implementation Review and Project Quality Assurance. These guidelines set clearer 
standards for assessing project outcomes and ensuring compliance with established 
processes. Management oversight was reinforced through enhanced Project Status Reports, 
which improved visibility of milestones, risks, and critical dependencies.
To build institutional capability, the BSP expanded engagement across project teams 
through the Enterprise Project Management Roadshow. The Bank also strengthened 
peer learning through the Project Managers Community of Practice and Interests Circle.  
These platforms supported knowledge sharing, coordination, and problem-solving 
across teams. With stronger standards, improved reporting, and better collaboration,  
the BSP advanced a more coherent, institution-wide approach to project delivery in support 
of its enterprise strategy.
Keeping strategy on track
Strategy remains relevant only through active management. This requires consistent 
monitoring, structured reviews, and timely course correction.
In 2025, the BSP strengthened enterprise-wide strategy monitoring through a more 
structured cadence of engagements and formal review forums. These platforms validated 
performance, identified bottlenecks and emerging risks, and confirmed corrective 
actions—ensuring that performance discussions remained timely, evidence-based, and 
results-oriented.
Reporting standards were reinforced through improved validation of performance 
submissions from Strategy Execution Teams (SETs). Consolidated insights were presented 
in the Annual and Mid-Year Strategy Performance Reports for the Monetary Board (MB), 
providing clear, decision-ready information. To maintain strategic oversight, regular briefings 
between the MB and SETs were also conducted, deepening leadership alignment and 
sustaining execution momentum.
Together, these enhanced monitoring frameworks and clearer feedback mechanisms 
position the BSP to maintain strategic focus and deliver measurable outcomes across the 
institution.

Financial market conditions 
 
The global economy remained resilient in 2025 despite heightened policy 
uncertainty, tariff-related volatility, and persistent fiscal pressures. Market sentiment 
shifted between optimism and caution, as changing trade policies, geopolitical 
developments, rapid advances in AI, and evolving central bank guidance shaped 
the outlook. 
 
The return of US President Donald Trump ushered in a new phase of US 
protectionism, with successive tariff rounds affecting Canada, Mexico, China, and 
the European Union (EU). Market pressures eased following a 90-day tariff pause 
between the US and China, along with a series of mid-year trade agreements, 
culminating in a US–China truce that helped stabilize supply chains. 
 
Although tariff measures initially raised concerns about global inﬂation and 
growth, subsequent developments—mainly in the US—helped temper inﬂationary 
pressures. These included the tariff pause, partial cost pass-through as ﬁrms 
absorbed margins, supply chain adjustments, and softening demand. As a result, 
US core inﬂation eased during the year, reducing stagﬂation risks, although growth 
concerns persisted.  
 
On the ﬁscal side, mounting strains dampened investor sentiment. The US faced  
a sovereign downgrade (Moody’s: Aaa to Aa1 on 16 May 2025), a US$4.0 trillion 
spending bill, and the longest government shutdown, underscoring rising debt 
vulnerabilities. Likewise, debt levels in Europe remained elevated, especially in the 
United Kingdom (UK) and France. 
 
US monetary policy expectations shifted throughout the year before settling into a 
gradual easing cycle. After holding rates steady for most of the year, the Federal 
Reserve (Fed) delivered three 25-basis point (bp) cuts in September, October, and 
December, and ended quantitative tightening. Meanwhile, the Bank of England 
and the European Central Bank each reduced rates four times. In contrast, the Bank 
of Japan raised its policy rate to a three-decade high of 0.75 percent.

External sector 
 
Balance of payments 
 
The Philippines recorded a full-year balance-of-payments (BOP) deﬁcit equivalent 
to 1.2 percent of GDP in 2025, reversing the surplus recorded in 2024. Softer ﬁnancial 
account inﬂows more than offset the improvement in the current account. 
The current account deficit narrowed from 4.0 percent of GDP in 2024 to 3.3 percent 
in 2025, driven largely by a smaller trade-in-goods deﬁcit. 
 
The external trade position improved due to stronger global demand for 
technology-related goods. Firms frontloaded shipments ahead of the announced  
US reciprocal tariff revisions in mid -2025, reﬂecting an intertemporal shift in  
trade activity. Meanwhile, imports expanded amid easing supply chain constraints, 
resilient industrial activity in AEs, and sustained investment in digitalization  
and electriﬁcation.  
 
Goods exports rose by 15.2 percent from US$55.1 billion in 2024 to US$63.4 billion 
in 2025. Growth was led by electronic products, reﬂecting robust global demand 
for semiconductors, electronic data processing equipment, and components  
used in AI applications and electric vehicles. Exports of other manufactured and 
mineral products also strengthened, including higher shipments of copper anodes,  
nickel ores, and copper mattes, underpinned by ﬁrm global industrial demand  
and favorable metal prices. Export growth was mainly driven by higher shipments 
to Hong Kong, the US, and Japan. 
 
Goods imports expanded by 5.0  percent from US$123.9 billion in 2024  to 
US$130.1 billion in 2025, reﬂecting resilient domestic demand. Higher imports of 
telecommunications equipment and electrical machinery mirrored continued 
investments in digital infrastructure. Increased imports of passenger cars and 
motorcycles, including electric mopeds, pointed to sustained demand for  
transport solutions. Imports of animal and vegetable oils and fats also rose.  
Import growth was largely driven by China, South Korea, and Taiwan, consistent 
with deepening regional production linkages. 
 
Net receipts from trade in services fell by 4.5 percent. This was weighed down by 
higher outbound travel and increased spending on computer and financial services. 
Nonetheless, services exports continued to beneﬁt from BPO revenues, which grew 
by 4.8 percent despite weaker travel receipts. Net primary income receipts declined 
by 6.1 percent due to higher dividend and interest payments to non-residents. 
In contrast, net secondary income receipts rose by 3.1 percent, driven by record 
remittances from overseas Filipino workers (OFWs). 
 
Financial account inﬂows moderated amid heightened global uncertainty. 
Net inﬂows declined from US$19.1 billion in 2024 to US$10.9 billion in 2025. 
This reﬂected a shift in portfolio investment to net outﬂows and reduced inﬂows  
in both direct and other investments. Net direct investment inﬂows declined by 
7.6 percent from US$6.7 billion to US$6.2 billion, as net foreign direct investments 
fell by 17.1 percent. This was partly offset by a 31.4-percent rise in net equity  
capital placements—mainly from Japan, the US, and Singapore—directed toward 
manufacturing, trade, and ﬁnancial services.

The portfolio investment account posted net outﬂows of US$1.3 billion, reversing

Meanwhile, the narrower current account deﬁcit corresponds to a smaller negative 
saving–investment gap, consistent with the standard macroeconomic identity 
linking external and domestic resource balances. From this perspective, the smaller 
gap reﬂects moderation in both gross saving and gross investment, shaping the 
observed external adjustment. The slowdown appears to have followed stronger 
activity in the ﬁrst half of the year, when consumption and investment were 
relatively ﬁrmer, before easing in the second half amid weaker conﬁdence linked to 
the ﬂood control controversy.  
 
Gross saving likely softened as household consumption remained supported by 
lower inﬂation, steady remittance inﬂows, and rising wages. At the same time, gross 
investment decelerated amid cautious business sentiment , softer external 
demand, and more conservative inventory and capital spending decisions. 
Indicators such as softening business conﬁdence, uneven PMI trends, and slower 
growth in goods imports are consistent with this moderation in private sector activity. 
 
Despite the narrowing of the current account deficit, its persistence raises questions 
about the long-run sustainability of the Philippines’ external position. An empirical 
study by the BSP using updated data for Q1 2005–Q4 2025 found that the country’s 
current account exhibits weak or conditional sustainability,28 indicating that 
external payment obligations are not fully matched by proportional export growth. 
This result reﬂects structural features of the Philippine external sector, where 
adjustment relies not only on goods exports but also on remittances, BPO and 
other services receipts, and stable capital inﬂows. In addition, recent deﬁcits have 
been largely investment-driven, associated with infrastructure development and 
capital formation rather than consumption, with medium -term repayment 
capacity supported by higher potential growth.  
 
Taken together, these conditions suggest that external adjustment is shaped not 
only by trade performance but also by the resilience of services and income ﬂows, 
the quality of investment, and the broader macroeconomic environment 
supporting external ﬁnancing and investor conﬁdence.

Strengthening systemic risk management models, metrics, and policies 
 
The BSP enhances systemic risk management through integrated models, indicators, 
and policy tools. Key measures in 2025 included: 
 
• Assessing ﬁrm-level exposures and systemic risk buildup. Amid monetary 
policy easing to support lending, the BSP developed a framework to assess 
corporate debt exposures and track systemic risk buildup. The framework 
monitors lending from a macroprudential perspective, supporting early 
risk identiﬁcation, and assesses the impact of domestic and cross-border 
shocks on ﬁrm-level risks. Enhancements are being explored to facilitate 
cross-country comparisons. 
 
• Developing systemic risk indicators. The Systemic Vulnerability Indicator 
consolidates macroﬁnancial data to monitor structural vulnerabilities across 
key risk channels, providing a broad view of systemic risk conditions. 
The Systemic Risk Sentiment Index complements this by capturing shifts in 
market perception from economic and ﬁnancial news, including risks not yet 
reﬂected in traditional indicators. Together, these tools strengthen the BSP’s 
forward-looking surveillance capabilities. 
 
• Conﬁguring macroprudential models. The BSP uses analytical tools to 
enhance systemic risk assessment by linking macroﬁnancial conditions, 
spillover channels, and bank balance sheet resilience. The Growth-at-Risk 
framework estimates the distribution of future GDP growth under prevailing 
macroﬁnancial conditions and signals the severity of potential adverse 
economic outcomes.

attributable to foreign currency movements. Stress testing applies

potential losses and capital impacts, informing assessments of system-wide 
resilience and policy responses. 
 
• Operationalizing the countercyclical capital buffer. In October 2025, 
the BSP engaged the IMF through a technical assistance mission to 
operationalize the countercyclical capital buffer (CCyB). The engagement 
reﬁned the framework’s methodology, calibration, governance, and 
implementation. This marks a critical step toward a robust, forward-looking 
CCyB framework to strengthen banking sector resilience against systemic 
risks from credit cycles. 
 
Priority areas for 2026 include adopting the positive neutral CCyB for banks,

integrating NBFI data into systemic risk assessments, and developing a macro

The BSP continues to advance its sustainability agenda across three reinforcing

Managing climate and environmental risks 
 
Sound policy and effective supervision require strong climate risk analytics  
and prudential frameworks. In 2025, the BSP further integrated climate and 
environmental risks into ﬁnancial supervision and policymaking, including 
enhancements to the regulatory relief framework for banks in calamity-affected

environmental risks. 
 
Advancing climate risk stress testing 
 
Climate risk stress testing (CRST) remains a core component of the BSP’s 
supervisory approach. It enables the assessment of banks’ resilience under severe 
yet plausible climate scenarios. As an evolving discipline, CRST requires specialized 
methods and granular data. To this end, the BSP continues to work with 
development partners and the banking sector to strengthen supervisory and 
bank-level capabilities.  
 
In partnership with the WB, the BSP conducted CRST workshops on 31 March and 
02 April 2025. The workshops enhanced participants’ understanding of how 
physical and transition risks can amplify traditional banking risks—such as credit, 
market, operational, and liquidity risks—and introduced alternative stress testing 
approaches and data requirements. These help banks proactively identify and 
manage climate- and environment-related ﬁnancial exposures. 
 
The BSP also worked with BSFIs to integrate climate risks into the stress testing 
framework. Key priorities include: (a) developing supervisory guidelines to promote 
consistency and transparency in scenario design, (b)  identifying transmission 
channels affecting banks’ risk proﬁles and portfolios, and (c) enhancing reporting 
requirements to improve the availability of granular, decision-useful data for climate 
risk assessment.

To operationalize AFASA, the BSP issued three implementing circulars in 2025:
