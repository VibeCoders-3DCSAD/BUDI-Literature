Executive Summary 
–––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––– 
 
Global economic activity continued to expand  in Q1 2026, although growth became 
increasingly uneven amid heightened geopolitical tensions. The escalation of the conflict in 
the Middle East (ME) pushed up oil prices  and increased financial market volatility , thus 
complicating the global inflation outlook . In response, major central banks  maintained a 
cautious stance, reinforcing a less supportive external environment for emerging market 
economies such as the Philippines. 
 
Against this backdrop, the Philippine economy slowed further in the first quarter of 2026 . 
Real gross domestic product (GDP) grew by 2.8 percent year-on-year (y-o-y), lower than the 
5.4 percent recorded a year earlier and the 3.0 percent growth in the previous quarter. The 
slowdown reflected weaker domestic demand, driven by subdued investment largely due 
to continued weakness in public construction amid project-related governance issues, and 
softer household spending . Rising fuel costs and heightened uncertainty associated with 
the ME conflict also weighed on economic activity. 
 
The moderation in economic growth was likewise reflected in l abor market conditions.  
Employment creation lagged behind labor force growth , resulting in  a higher 
unemployment rate. Nevertheless, labor market quality improved, as most of the gains were 
full-time, wage-and-salary, and middle - to high-skill occupations. Employment expansion 
was observed in business process outsourcing (BPO), transport and logistics, tourism-related 
activities, healthcare, and manufacturing. 
 
At the same time, inflationary pressures began to re -emerge. Headline inflation averaged  
2.8 percent in Q1 2026 , up from 1.7 percent in  the previous quarter , although it remained 
within the 3.0 ± 1.0 percent inflation target tolerance range. The increase was driven largely 
by higher food and fuel prices, particularly rice and petroleum products, as domestic supply 
conditions tightened and global oil prices rose following the escalation of the ME conflict. 
 
The evolving inflation dynamics shaped the conduct of monetary policy during the quarter. 
On 19 February 2026, the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas  (BSP) reduced the Target Reverse 
Repurchase (RRP) rate by 25 basis points (bps) to 4.25 percent to further support domestic 
demand amid a manageable inflation outlook. However, as geopolitical tensions intensified 
and inflation risks increased, the Monetary Board held an off -cycle meeting on 26 March 
2026 to assess the policy stance. The BSP kept the policy rate unch anged, while 
underscoring the need to prevent second -round effects and keep inflation expectations 
firmly anchored. 
 
Meanwhile, fiscal developments pointed to gradual consolidation . The National 
Government’s (NG) budget deficit narrowed to 5.1 percent of GDP in Q1 2026 from                      
6.8 percent a year earlier , largely reflecting  higher non -tax revenues and slower 
disbursements. The improvement in the fiscal balance was driven mainly by temporary 
revenue gains and spending restraint . At the same time, the debt -to-GDP ratio rose to         
65.2 percent, its highest level since 2006, highlighting the importance of sustaining credible 
medium-term fiscal adjustment while protecting growth-enhancing spending.  
 
Despite these economic headwinds, the Philippine financial system remained resilient and 
continued to serve as an important buffer against shocks . Banks stayed well capitalized, 
while deposits and lending  continued to expand. The payments system also processed a

higher volume and value of transactions, reflecting sustained financial intermediation and 
the continued adoption of digital payment channels.   
 
Nevertheless, domestic financial markets remained sensitive to heightened uncertainty. 
Spillovers from  the ME conflict, slower domestic growth, and narrower interest rate 
differentials contributed to weaker equity prices, wider sovereign risk spreads, and a 
depreciation of the peso during the quarter. 
 
External sector conditions were less favorable, with higher oil prices and tighter global 
financial conditions associated with a wider balance of payments  (BOP) deficit and a 
modest decline in reserves. Nonetheless, robust exports and continued inflows from 
remittances, BPO, and travel receipts provided buffers that supported a broadly 
manageable external position. 
 
Looking ahead, economic growth is expected to remain modest in the near term before 
gradually improving as investment recovers and public spending normalizes. However, the 
outlook remains clouded by uncertainty over the duration and intensity of the ME conflict, 
the risk of further oil -related inflation pressures, and the need to improve business 
sentiment. The country’s sound financial system and resilient external position provide 
important safeguards . Equally important , a firmer recovery will depend on reviving 
investment, maintaining prudent macroeconomic policies, and containing spillover 
inflation effects from recent supply shocks . In this regard, the BSP and the NG stand ready 
to implement policies that would support recovery  while promoting price stability  and 
sustainable economic growth.

II. Domestic Conditions 
–––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––––– 
A. Real Sector 
 
Domestic economic growth slowed further in Q1 2026 amid lingering governance concerns, 
with the recent ME conflict adding to headwinds. The immediate impact of these shocks is 
more evident in the softening of labor market conditions during the quarter. 
 
1. Economic Growth 
 
Philippine economic growth slowed to 2.8 percent in Q1 2026, from 5.4 percent in Q1 2025 
and 3.0 percent in Q4 2025. On a seasonally adjusted basis, real GDP grew by 0.9 percent 
quarter-on-quarter (q-o-q) (Figure 4) . The slowdown reflected soft domestic demand, 
particularly subdued investment, slower private consumption, and adverse supply -side 
pressures associated with higher fuel costs as a result of  recent ME conflict . While net 
exports improved as export growth outpaced imports, the overall contribution remained 
negative. Growth continued to be driven by services, while industry and agriculture 
contracted. 
 
Compared with  regional peers, the Philippines posted a more moderate growth 
performance in the first quarter , below that of Vietnam (7.8 percent ), Singapore                         
(6.0 percent), Indonesia (5.6 percent), Malaysia (5.4 percent), and China (5.0 percent) , and 
broadly comparable to Thailand (2.8 percent). This more measured performance may partly 
reflect the Philippines’ sensitivity to higher global oil prices, given its reliance on fuel imports 
from the ME, alongside temporary project delays linked to the flood-control controversy.

2. Labor Conditions 
 
Labor market conditions mirrored the broader moderation in economic activity in Q1 2026.5 
Employment gains remained concentrated on the services sector, while subdued activity in

Employment quality improved, as gains were mostly in wage and salary work, full -time

employment.6 However, the increase in unemployment and underemployment rates 
suggests the need for stronger and more sustained job creation to absorb the expanding

Despite the expansion of  the labor force in Q1 2026 , a slight decline in the labor force 
participation rate was still recorded given a faster increase in the working -age population.  
Moreover, even with the increase in total employment, which was driven mainly by services

3. Fiscal Spending 
 
In Q1 2026, the NG recorded a narrower fiscal deficit at 5.1 percent of GDP, from 6.8 percent 
a year earlier. The primary deficit-to-GDP ratio also improved to 1.2 percent from 3.1 percent 
in Q1 2025. The decline in deficit ratios, however, was driven by non-tax revenue collections 
and more restrained spending amid lingering governance concerns (Figure 8; Table 3).

Revenue effort rose to 16.4 percent of GDP from 15.2 percent a year earlier, largely reflecting 
higher non -tax revenues. The surge in  non-tax collections  was driven by early dividend 
remittances of Government -Owned and -Controlled Corporations (GOCC). Meanwhile, tax 
revenues grew only modestly amid a slowdown in economic activity and import growth.   
 
NG disbursements grew at a significantly slower pace at 3.2 percent y-o-y, reflecting broad 
moderation in primary spending. This was due to tighter spending controls, normalization 
from election-related spending last year, and a slightly delayed budget enactment. Despite 
the slowdown,  spending remained supported in part by higher transfers to local 
governments, increased personnel services expenditures due to the implementation of the 
3rd tranche of salary adjustments for civil servants, and initial outlays for emergency energy 
measures.  
 
Meanwhile, interest payments rose by 13.3 percent  y-o-y, increasing their share in total

issuance, and foreign exchange fluctuations.

interest rate –growth differential turning negative in Q1 2026 after two quarters of less 
favorable conditions. However, this largely reflects lower real interest rates a mid subdued 
economic growth. The margin remains fragile, as the ME conflict continues to exert supply-
side pressures, weighing on growth while raising public financing costs. Since its onset,

B.  Monetary Sector 
 
Monetary developments in Q1 2026 reflected the need to balance support for a slowing 
economy against rising inflation risks from the ME conflict and higher global oil prices. 
Inflation remained within target on average during the quarter, but price pressures became 
more pronounced by March, prompting a more cautious monetary policy stance.

(Figure 9; Table 4).  This is slightly below the NG’s target of 3.0 percent but within the 
tolerance band of ± 1.0 percentage point ( ppt) for the year. Headline inflation was largely 
driven by higher prices of food and energy prices. Rice prices, which account for a significant 
share of the consumer price index (CPI) basket, also increased due to higher farmgate prices 
following the import ban, the ongoing lean season, and the reduced domestic production. 
Domestic pump prices were also adjusted upward, reflecting the surge in international oil 
prices owing to disruptions from the geopolitical tensions in the ME.

BSP liquidity operations remained aligned with the transmission of the February policy rate 
cut during Q1 2026. As of end-Q1 2026, total outstanding placements in the BSP’s liquidity 
facilities stood at ₱1.3 trillion. Offer volumes generally declined across both term and 
overnight facilities in Q1 2026, reflecting the BSP’s calibration of monetary operations to 
support the transmission of the February policy rate cut. 
 
Liquidity absorption remained concentrated on overnight facilities, following the shift to 
single-tenor offerings for the term deposit facility (TDF) and BSP Securities Facility (BSP-SF) 
in November 2025. Placements in the overnight RRP (ON RRP) and overnight deposit facility 
(ODF) accounted for a combined 79.9 percent of total liquidity absorption in Q1 2026. 
Meanwhile, the share of TDF and BSP-SF stood at 5.3 percent and 14.9 percent, respectively.  
 
Monetary policy transmission to BSP facilities remained broadly effective during the quarter. 
Following the 25 -bp reduction in the target RRP rate on 19 February 2026, the weighted 
average ON RRP rate declined by 31.6 bps q-o-q, also reflecting ample liquidity in the 
financial system. The TDF rate declined by 27.7 bps and the BSP bill rate by 26.5 bps, from 
end-2025 levels  (Table 6) . The cumulative 225 -bp policy rate reduction under the BSP’s 
easing cycle, which started in August 2024, was also fully transm itted to the ON RRP and

and 228.1 bps, respectively. Meanwhile, the 28 -day BSPB rate declined by 204.2 bps, 
reflecting partial pass-through. 
 
The relatively slower decline in the BSP bill rate reflected overall preference for the       
shorter-term facilities following the escalation of conflict in the ME in late February. The BTr’s 
₱297.9-billion 10-year Treasury bond (T-bond) issuance in February, along with seasonal cash 
requirements ahead of the Lenten holidays and tax filing deadline, also weakened demand 
for the BSP-SF.

the previous quarter. Banks tapped the IBCL market to fund loan releases, GS purchases, and 
deposit withdrawals. Similarly, average short -term Bloomberg Evaluated Pricing Service

1. Balance of Payments 
 
The Philippines’ BOP deficit widened to 4.5  percent of GDP in Q1  2026 from 2.6  percent of 
GDP a year earlier , reflecting weaker financial inflows and a larger current account deficit. 
Financial inflows declined sharply  due to  liquidity management by local banks  through 
foreign loans repayments, alongside non-residents withdrawals from domestic instruments.  
Direct investment inflows moderated amid cautious investor sentiment. Meanwhile,  the 
current account deficit widened, driven by a larger goods trade deficit and weaker primary 
income and services receipts.  
 
The t rade-in-goods deficit widened by 4.7 percent to US$17.1 billion in Q1 2026 , as price-
driven import growth  outweighed volume-led export gains.  Despite strong  export 
performance, supported by electronics and machinery demand, elevated imports persisted, 
reflecting higher prices and sustained demand for capital and intermediate goods. 
 
Goods exports rose 13.6 percent to US$17.6 billion in Q1 2026, driven by electronics, gold, and 
machinery, with growth largely volume -led. Electronics benefited from  strong global 
demand for semiconductors and integrated circuits , particularly  from AI-related 
applications, while gold exports increased amid higher prices. M achinery and transport 
equipment exports also improved , indicating firm regional industrial demand . Export 
growth was broad -based, supported by strong demand from Hong Kong, the US, and 
Taiwan.  
 
Goods imports grew 9.0 percent to US$34.8 billion, reflecting sustained demand for 
telecommunications and electrical equipment linked to digital ization and 5G expansion. 
Fuel imports rose sharply due to higher global oil prices, while imports of materials and 
accessories for manufacturing electrical equipment increased amid elevated prices and 
supply constraints. Higher import volumes were sourced mainly  from South Korea, China 
and Japan. 
 
The services surplus narrowed by 6.0 percent to US$2.8 billion, driven by higher outbound 
travel and increased spending on technical, trade -related, and other business services. 
Nonetheless, services exports remained resilient. Travel receipts strengthened  with higher 
tourist arrivals—particularly from China following visa liberalization— reaching 2.7 percent of 
GDP. BPO revenues rose modestly by 1.3 percent to US$7.3  billion (6.2 percent of GDP), 
reflecting the sector’s continued competitiveness. 
 
Secondary income provided a stable buffer. Net remittance inflows increased 2.3 percent to

III. Conclusion 
 
Developments in Q1 2026 highlighted the Philippine economy's ability to withstand a more 
difficult global environment characterized by elevated uncertainty, rising oil prices, and 
tighter financial conditions. Although growth moderated and inflation pressures increased, 
the economy remained supported by sound macroeconomic fundamentals and resilient 
financial and external sector buffers. 
 
The BSP responded proactively to evolving conditions by calibrating monetary policy to 
balance support for domestic activity with the need to safeguard price stability. Monetary 
operations and financial sector policies continued to facilitate effective policy transmission, 
preserve financial system resilience, and promote orderly market conditions amid 
heightened volatility. 
 
At the same time, the National Government's continued commitment to fiscal discipline 
helped maintain macroeconomic stability. Going forward, efforts to strengthen revenue 
mobilization, accelerate quality public investment, and address implementation 
bottlenecks will be important in supporting growth and enhancing economic 
competitiveness. 
 
The country's strong reserve position, steady foreign exchange inflows, and well-capitalized 
banking system provide valuable protection against external shocks. These strengths, 
together with prudent macroeconomic management, position the economy to navigat e 
near-term challenges and support a gradual recovery in growth while keeping inflation 
aligned with the target over the medium term.

Extension of Regulatory Incentive Period for Financing for Eligible Green or Sustainable 
Projects and Activities (BSP Circular No. 1227 dated 5 January 202639) 
 
BSP Circular No. 1227 extends, for two years from 6 January 2026, regulatory incentives for 
financing eligible green or sustainable projects and activities, including transitional 
decarbonization initiatives. Incentives include additional headroom (up to 15 percent) above 
the standard 25 percent single -borrower limit for qualifying exposures and a zero percent 
reserve requirement on eligible sustainable bond issuances, subject to banks’ prudent risk 
management and internal capital adequacy assessments. 
 
This time‑bound measure supports the BSP ’s sustainable central banking agenda and the 
government’s climate and development commitments by encouraging BSP ‑supervised 
financial institutions to scale up financing for green, sustainable, and transition activities. It 
also reaffirms the BSP ’s commitment to adopt risk ‑based, market ‑responsive prudential 
tools that facilitate the mobilization of capital toward a low ‑carbon and climate ‑resilient 
economy. 
 
Amendments to the N-Regulations on Annual Supervision Fees for Non-Bank Financial 
Institutions with Trust Authority (BSP Circular No. 1228 dated 6 January 202640) 
BSP Circular No. 1228 amends the N‑Regulations of the Manual of Regulations for Non‑Bank 
Financial Institutions (MORNBFI) to provide guidelines on the annual supervision fees for 
NBFIs with trust authority. Under the Circular, NBFIs with trust authority shall pay to the BSP 
an annual supervision fee to help defray the cost of maintaining appropriate sup ervisory 
resources, computed at one twenty‑eighth (1/28) of one percent of their average assessable 
assets for the preceding year, based on total trust department accounts, excluding securities 
held under custodianship. 
The said fee shall be paid on or before 31 May of every year, starting with the assessment 
period for 2025, and non‑payment within the prescribed period shall subject the concerned 
NBFI to administrative sanctions in accordance with existing BSP regulations. In line with 
applicable tax laws, remittances to the BSP, including supervisory fees, are exempt from 
national in ternal revenue taxes; hence, NBFIs with trust authority are not required to 
withhold tax on their payment of the annual supervision fee to t he BSP, aligning the 
treatment of NBFIs with trust authority with that of banks with trust departments.

Risk-based Recalibration of Enhanced Due Diligence (EDD) Threshold for Large Value 
Cash Transactions (BSP Circular No. 1230 dated 27 February 202642) 
 
BSP Circular No. 1230, issued on 27 February 2026, raised the threshold for cash withdrawals 
that would trigger enhanced due diligence (EDD) to ₱1 million from ₱500,000.00. The 
increase follows consultations with banks and industry groups, which indicated that many 
legitimate cash transactions exceeded the original threshold, including payroll, loan 
releases, and project-based disbursements. The increase also follows the results of the latest 
anti-money laundering National Risk Assessment and surveillance monitoring, recognizing 
that robust risk -based safeguards over cash transactions remain essential to protecting 
financial system integrity. 
 
The EDD will be applied on a per -customer basis rather than per transaction. As such, it 
should not hamper regular transactions. Under the circular, depositors may withdraw up to 
₱1 million without EDD, while amounts above this require proof of legitimacy, and BSP -
supervised financial institutions may set lower thresholds based on their own risk 
assessments. The ₱500,000 threshold was imposed in September to curb money laundering 
and prevent illicit use of the financial system, while no threshold applies to  non-cash 
withdrawals. The new circular amends BSP Circular No. 1218 issued in September 2025. 
 
Amendments to Section 171 and relevant provisions of the Manual of Regulations for 
Banks on reporting governance (BSP Circular No. 1231 dated 3 March 202643) 
The Monetary Board, through BSP Circular No. 1231, approved amendments to Section 171 
and relevant MORB provisions on reporting governance to strengthen the integrity, 
accuracy, and timeliness of prudential and regulatory reports submitted by banks. The 
revised framework requires banks to adopt a board ‑approved reporting governance 
structure covering the entire reporting process —from data collection and validation to 
review and submission —supported by adequate data architecture, internal controls, and 
information technology systems. 
The Circular sets the accountabilities of the board of directors, senior management, 
compliance, and internal audit in overseeing reporting governance, and defines key 
attributes of an acceptable report, including completeness, accuracy, timeliness, and 
adaptability to evolving supervisory requirements. It also rationalizes existing rules by 
removing the previous categorization of reports and habituality metrics, revising the rules 
on authorized signatories, and aligning the penalty structure for reporting violations with 
banks’ asset size to promote proportionality and incentivize higher reporting quality.

The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) issued Memorandum Circular No. 1, Series

SEC Backs More Stock Market Listings with Flexible Public Float Rule46 
The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) issued Memorandum Circular No. 11, Series 
of 2026, adopting a tiered minimum public ownership (MPO) framework. This aims to 
encourage equity listings while preserving market liquidity and transparency. The revised 
structure aligns public float requirements with issuers’ expected market capitalization at 
listing, recognizing the differing liquidity and valuation dynamics of small ‑cap versus 
large‑cap companies, and calibrates thresholds to support capital formation and effective 
price discovery. Consistent with practices within the ASEAN region, the framework 
maintains higher proportional floats for smaller issuers while allowing lower, proportionate 
requirements for large companies, subject to minimum offer size safeguards. This 
strengthens the competitiveness of the domestic equities market without compromising 
governance or investor protection standards.

interest rate, specifically the overnight reverse repurchase (RRP) rate. The interest rates for the standing liquidity facilities—the overnight lending facility (OLF) and the overnight
deposit facility (ODF)—establish the corridor's upper and lower bounds, respectively. Additionally, the term deposit facility (TDF) serves as the primary tool for absorbing excess
liquidity. On 8 September 2023, the BSP transitioned to a variable-rate format with a predetermined offer volume for the overnight RRP facility auction. Two significant
changes were introduced: 1) the BSP’s monetary policy rate is now referred to as the “Target RRP Rate,” and 2) a formal operational target, known as the “Overnight (ON) RRP
Rate,” was also established.
