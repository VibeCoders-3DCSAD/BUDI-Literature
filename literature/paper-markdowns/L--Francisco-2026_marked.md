ABSTRACT  
This  study  examined  the  prevalence  and  causes  of  salary  loan  dependency  among  non-
teaching employees of the Department of Education Schools Division of Masbate Province. 
It also aimed to identify practical strategies that can reduce repeated borrowing and serve 
as a basis for strengthening a financial literacy program. The study employed a descriptive 
quantitative  research  design  using  a  survey  questionnaire.  Respondents  were  341  non-
teaching  personnel  selected  through  simple  random  sampling.  The  sample  size  was 
determined using Cochran’s formula with finite population correction, and the final size was 
increased to 341 to account for non-response and improve precision. Data were analyzed 
using  frequency,  percentage,  mean,  and  related  descriptive  statistics.  Ethical  standards 
were observed through voluntary participation, confidentiality, and informed consent. Most 
respondents were aged 36 to 45 and the majority were married, with many earning 30,000 
pesos or below and supporting several dependents. A large portion had applied for salary 
loans,  commonly  borrowing  for  daily  living  expenses  and  debt  repayment.  Respondents 
agreed  that  salary  loan  dependency  is  influenced  by  financial  literacy  gaps,  financial 
obligations,  and  pressures  such  as  insufficient  income  and  emergencies,  spending 
behavior,  and  easy  access  to 
lending  services.  Respondents  also  rated  multiple 
interventions  as  very  helpful,  particularly  financial  literacy  seminars,  financial  counseling 
and debt management support, strengthening cooperatives with better terms, and payroll 
savings schemes. The findings show that salary loan dependency among DepEd Masbate 
non-teaching personnel is driven by both economic constraints and financial behavior. A 
strengthened financial literacy program that is continuous, workplace-based and supported 
by institutional mechanisms is recommended to reduce repeated borrowing and improve 
employee financial well-being.

Keywords:  salary  loan  dependency,  financial  literacy,  debt  management,  budgeting,  DepEd 
employees

INTRODUCTION

Financial well-being has become a critical concern in contemporary workplaces, as it directly affects 
employees’ stability, productivity, and overall quality of life. Persistent financial strain can lead to stress, 
anxiety,  and  reduced  concentration,  which  may  negatively  influence  job  performance  and  decision-
making (Pomroy, 2021). For employees with fixed incomes and increasing household responsibilities, 
borrowing often becomes a common coping strategy to address financial shortfalls. While loans may 
provide  short-term  relief,  repeated  borrowing  can  result  in  long-term  financial  vulnerability  and 
dependency.

At the global level, financial literacy is widely recognized as a vital life skill that enables individuals to 
manage personal finances responsibly and avoid harmful debt cycles. The Organisation for Economic 
Co-operation and Development emphasizes that effective financial education should equip individuals 
with  the  knowledge  and  skills  necessary  for  budgeting,  saving,  debt  management,  and  long-term 
financial  planning  (OECD  INFE,  2020).  Similarly,  financial  capability  is  highlighted  in  international 
development  agendas  as  a  means  to  promote  economic  empowerment,  financial  inclusion,  and 
resilience against income shocks.

In response to these global directions, the Philippine government has institutionalized financial literacy 
initiatives  for  public  sector  employees.  Republic  Act  No.  10922,  also  known  as  the  Economic  and 
Financial  Literacy  Act,  mandated  the  promotion  of  financial  education  across  government  agencies 
through the annual observance of Economic and Financial Literacy Week. This was further reinforced 
by  Executive  Order  No.  32,  s.  2017,  which  directed  the  development  of  financial  literacy  programs 
tailored  specifically  for  government  personnel.  These  policies  underscore  the  state’s  recognition  of 
financial literacy as an essential component of employee welfare and good governance.

Within the education sector, the Department of Education has issued complementary policies to support 
employee financial wellness. DepEd Memorandum No. 121, s. 2019 encouraged the conduct of financial 
literacy seminars for personnel, while DepEd Order No. 5, s. 2021, integrated employee wellness and 
financial  education  into  the  Results-Based  Performance  Management  System.  More  recently,  DepEd 
Order  No.  12,  s.  2023  emphasized  employee  welfare  programs  that  promote  responsible  financial 
behavior and overall well-being. These directives reflect DepEd’s commitment to improving the financial 
capability of its workforce.

Despite the presence of these national and institutional initiatives, financial distress remains prevalent 
among  DepEd  personnel,  particularly  in  economically  disadvantaged  provinces  such  as  Masbate. 
Evidence  noted  in  the  study  indicates  that  while  financial  incentives  such  as  midyear  and  year-end 
bonuses provide temporary relief, many employees’ take-home pay is substantially reduced by recurring 
loan deductions (Madrona, 2017). After mandatory deductions and repayments, employees are often 
left with limited disposable income, which may be insufficient to meet basic household needs. As a

The situation is further complicated by the emergence of informal and high-risk borrowing practices. 
Reports from the DepEd Masbate Province Division Office reveal instances of unauthorized ATM card

by some employees.

Addressing salary loan dependency, therefore, requires more than policy issuance. It calls for a deeper 
understanding of the financial realities, behaviors, and constraints experienced by employees. Financial 
literacy, defined as the ability to apply knowledge and skills in budgeting, saving, and debt management, 
plays  a  crucial  role  in  promoting  responsible  borrowing  and  long-term  financial  stability  (Lusardi  & 
Tufano, 2015). In recognition of this need, the Civil Service Commission, in partnership with the Bangko 
Sentral ng Pilipinas and BDO Foundation, launched the Financial Education Program for Civil Servants 
to strengthen financial knowledge, debt management skills, and financial resilience among government 
employees (CSC, 2023).

However, there remains a lack of localized empirical studies that examine salary loan dependency and 
financial literacy within specific public sector workplaces, particularly among non-teaching personnel in 
provincial DepEd division offices. Most existing discussions focus on general employee debt patterns 
or national-level programs, leaving the lived financial experiences, causes of borrowing, and preferred 
interventions  of  employees  in  areas  like  Masbate  underexplored.  Without  such  evidence,  financial 
literacy programs risk being generic and disconnected from actual employee needs.

This  study  is  guided  by  the  Theory  of  Planned  Behavior  (Ajzen,  1991),  Financial  Capability  Theory 
(Sherraden, 2013), and the Theory of Resource Allocation. The Theory of Planned Behavior explains that 
borrowing behavior is shaped by attitudes toward loans, workplace norms, and perceived control over 
financial  decisions.  Financial  Capability  Theory  emphasizes  that  financial  well-being  depends  on 
financial  knowledge  and  the  ability  to  apply  it  through  budgeting,  saving,  and  responsible  debt 
management.  The  Theory  of  Resource  Allocation  explains  how  employees  allocate  limited  income 
across  competing  household  needs,  which  may  increase  reliance  on  loans  when  financial  demands 
exceed  available  resources.  Anchored  in  these  frameworks,  the  study  examines  how  demographic 
factors and financial capability-related causes influence salary loan dependency and informs strategies 
for strengthening workplace financial literacy programs.

This  study  addresses  this  gap  by  determining  the  demographic  and  loan  profiles  of  non-teaching 
personnel in the Department of Education, Masbate Province Division Office, identifying the underlying 
causes  of  salary  loan  dependency,  and  determining  strategies  that  employees  consider  effective  in 
reducing repeated borrowing. The findings of the study serve as a basis for strengthening a financial 
literacy  program  that  is  contextualized,  continuous,  and  responsive  to  the  financial  realities  of 
employees,  intending  to  promote  sustainable  financial  well-being  and  reduce  dependency  on  salary 
loans.

Research Questions 
This study aimed to examine the prevalence of salary loan dependency and inadequate financial literacy 
among employees of the DepEd Masbate Province Division Office. Specifically, the study investigated 
how these issues contribute to financial strain, limited resources for personal financial stability, and 
long-term financial consequences. It explored potential strategies for mitigating loan dependency and 
enhancing financial literacy to improve the overall financial well-being of employees. 
The study sought to answer the following specific questions:

1.  What is the demographic profile of the respondents as to

1.1 Age  
1.2 Civil Status 
1.3 Income/Salary 
1.4 Length of Service in DepEd (in years) 
1.5 Number of dependents (not limited to children) 
2.  What is the loan profile of the respondents in terms of:

2.1 Frequency of salary loan applications 
2.2 Amount borrowed 
2.3 Loan purpose 
2.4 Repayment behavior

3.  What are the underlying causes of salary loan dependency among non-teaching employees at

the DepEd Masbate Province Division Office?

4.  What strategies, interventions, and support mechanisms can be implemented to reduce salary 
loan dependency and enhance financial literacy among employees of the DepEd Masbate 
Province Division Office?

REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

Foreign Literature 
Financial  well-being  is  widely  recognized  as  a  multidimensional  concept  that  reflects  both  an 
individual’s  objective  financial  condition  and  their  subjective  assessment  of  financial  security, 
satisfaction, and perceived control over financial life (Grable & Joo, 2001; Brüggen et al., 2017). This 
concept goes beyond income level alone and includes a person’s capacity to meet current obligations, 
maintain savings, handle emergencies, and plan for future financial goals. Studies consistently show 
that  weak  financial  well-being  is  associated  with  increased  stress,  reduced  productivity,  and  poorer 
physical and mental health outcomes (Schreiner & Sherraden, 2007; Lichtenstein et al., 2009). These 
effects  become  particularly  evident  among  employees  who  experience  financial  strain,  as  financial 
distress often translates into workplace consequences such as reduced concentration, absenteeism, 
and lower job satisfaction (Burton, 2012; Harrison et al., 2019).

One  of  the  strongest  predictors  of  financial  well-being  is  financial  literacy.  Financial  literacy  is 
commonly defined as the knowledge, skills, and confidence necessary to manage financial resources 
effectively—particularly in relation to budgeting, saving, investing, and debt management (Atkinson & 
Messy,  2012;  OECD,  2014).  Lusardi  and  Mitchell  (2013)  emphasized  the  economic  importance  of 
financial literacy, noting that individuals who understand basic financial concepts tend to make better 
financial decisions, avoid high-risk debt, and maintain greater financial stability. Financial literacy also 
strengthens  financial  capability,  which  refers  to  the  practical  application  of  knowledge  in  real-life 
financial  situations  (Xiao  &  O’Neill,  2016).  Moreover,  research  highlights  that  subjective  financial

Inadequate financial literacy, however, has been consistently linked to financial mismanagement and

underestimate loan costs, and struggle with long-term planning, which increases reliance on credit and

through  accessible  lending  options,  particularly  salary  loans.  Salary  loans  are  frequently  used  as  a 
financial tool for meeting urgent needs, yet repeated and unregulated use can lead to dependency and 
long-term  financial  instability  (Sullivan,  2008).  When  employees  rely  on  salary  loans  for  recurring 
expenses rather than emergencies, borrowing becomes cyclical, reinforcing financial strain and limiting 
an  individual’s  capacity  to  build  savings  and  financial  resilience.  Lichtenstein  et  al.  (2009)  similarly 
reported  that  workers  caught  in  repeated  debt  cycles  often  experience  both  economic  and 
psychological strain, as debt obligations reduce financial flexibility and create persistent stress.

The  consequences  of  salary  loan  dependency  extend  beyond  economic  hardship.  Tung  (2013) 
highlighted that excessive borrowing leads to emotional distress, anxiety, and reduced sense of control, 
which can significantly undermine an employee’s overall well-being. These psychological effects are 
especially relevant in organizational settings, where employee financial distress may result in reduced 
productivity and poorer job performance. Studies on workplace well-being have long emphasized that 
employee  wellness  is  interconnected:  financial  instability  often  contributes  to  decreased  workplace 
functioning and job satisfaction, making financial wellness a legitimate organizational concern (Burton, 
2012; Harrison et al., 2019).

In response to these concerns, research supports the implementation of financial literacy and financial 
wellness  interventions  in  the  workplace.  Volpe  et  al.  (2006)  reported  that  employees  exposed  to 
financial education programs gain greater confidence and improved financial decision-making skills, 
which can result in reduced stress and better financial management behaviors. Likewise, Xiao and Porto 
(2017) found that financial education improves financial satisfaction when knowledge is translated into 
behavior and capability. Workplace-based financial wellness programs—such as seminars, counseling, 
debt management assistance, and financial planning workshops—have been associated with improved 
savings behavior and better financial outcomes (Kapoor & Duflo, 2014; Ruder & Mott, 2017). Employers 
who invest in financial wellness strategies also benefit through higher employee engagement, improved 
performance, and increased retention (Harrison et al., 2019).

However,  researchers  emphasize  that  financial  education  alone  may  not  be  sufficient  without 
institutional mechanisms that support financial resilience. Schreiner and Sherraden (2007) argued that 
savings  tools  and  asset-building  interventions  are  essential  for  helping  individuals  escape  recurring 
debt cycles. Programs such as payroll-based savings schemes, emergency funds, cooperative-based 
alternatives, and structured financial planning tools can provide employees with practical options that 
reduce the need to borrow repeatedly. Such interventions are especially relevant for employees with 
limited income and multiple financial obligations, where borrowing often becomes the default coping 
strategy.

factors,  including  low  financial  literacy,  financial  strain,  behavioral  patterns,  and  the  accessibility  of

Local Literature  
Financial well-being among Filipino employees has been widely examined due to its direct influence on 
workplace functioning and overall life satisfaction. Pascual and Banzon (2019) reported that financial 
stress  is  strongly  associated  with  lower  productivity,  reduced  engagement,  and  higher  absenteeism 
among Filipino workers, reinforcing the view that financial security is not only a personal concern but 
also a workplace issue. Similarly, Macalalag and Tria (2016) found that many Filipino employees live 
paycheck to paycheck, which increases vulnerability to financial shocks and contributes to persistent 
stress. These studies highlight how limited financial resources can significantly affect both personal 
well-being  and  organizational  performance,  particularly  among  salaried  workers  in  government 
institutions.

Within  this  context,  salary  loan  dependency  has  emerged  as  a  common  coping  mechanism  among 
Filipino workers, especially those with constrained income and limited access to sustainable financial 
resources. Noche (2015) and Delos Reyes (2018) observed that while salary loans provide immediate 
financial relief, repeated borrowing often leads to a recurring debt cycle that is difficult to escape. This 
pattern is reinforced by the increasing availability of salary loan products in the country. The Bangko 
Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP, 2017) noted that the growing demand for quick-access loans has expanded 
the salary loan market, raising concerns about long-term borrower stability—particularly when loans are 
repeatedly renewed or acquired from lenders imposing high interest rates.

A  key  factor  contributing  to  loan  dependency  is  inadequate  financial  literacy.  Teves  (2017),  citing 
findings  from  Philippine  Business  for  Social  Progress,  reported  that  a  significant  portion  of  the 
Philippine  workforce  lacks  basic  financial  skills  such  as  budgeting,  saving,  and  informed  decision-
making. Liu (2016) and Racelis (2015) further noted that financial literacy education in the Philippines 
remains  insufficient  both  in  schools  and  within  workplace  settings.  Without  practical  training, 
employees are more likely to engage in poor financial behaviors such as overspending, lack of savings, 
and excessive reliance on credit, which heightens their vulnerability to recurring debt. Manlapig et al. 
(2018) supported this observation, emphasizing that while access to loans has improved, many workers 
remain unprepared to manage debt effectively and are often unaware of healthier financial alternatives, 
thereby reinforcing dependency.

In response to this national concern, several government and institutional efforts have been launched 
to improve financial literacy and reduce financial vulnerability. Tuncel and Maglalang (2020) reported 
that  financial  literacy  programs  implemented  in  various  institutions  improved  awareness  of  basic 
financial concepts, yet many interventions lacked practical application and failed to translate knowledge 
into daily financial behavior. Similarly, the Philippine Financial Literacy Program (2019), spearheaded by 
the  BSP  and  partner  institutions,  provides  seminars  and  resources  aimed  at  improving  financial 
awareness, but Iñigo (2018) observed that participation remains low among low-income employees and 
those in rural areas. This suggests that financial literacy initiatives are often inaccessible to the groups 
who  need  them  most,  emphasizing  the  importance  of  integrating  financial  education  into  employee 
development and welfare programs.

Beyond government programs, organizations and employers have also recognized the value of financial 
wellness initiatives. Cruz (2017) documented that institutions offering financial wellness programs—
such as financial planning workshops, counseling, and savings incentives—reported higher employee 
satisfaction and retention. However, Lariosa (2019) stressed that effective financial wellness programs 
must  be  sustained  and  institutionalized,  supported  by  a  workplace  culture  that  encourages  open 
financial dialogue and provides continuous support. De Leon (2016) similarly emphasized that national 
strategies for financial literacy must prioritize reach and inclusivity, particularly in sectors affected by 
debt vulnerability and salary loan dependency.

National development frameworks further reinforce the importance of financial inclusion and consumer 
financial  education.  Llanto  (2015)  explained  that  the  Philippine  Development  Plan  emphasizes  the 
importance  of  financial  access,  alternative  financial  products,  and  consumer  protection  to  promote 
financial stability. NEDA (2014) also highlighted financial strategies such as surety programs and credit 
support mechanisms that may benefit employees and communities in provincial areas. These national 
perspectives  support  the  need  for  workplace-based  financial  education  and  sustainable  financial 
support systems, especially in provinces where financial resources and access to services may be more 
limited.

Overall,  local  literature  demonstrates  that  salary  loan  dependency  in  the  Philippines  is  shaped  by  a 
combination of limited income, financial pressures, inadequate financial literacy, and the accessibility 
of lending systems. While numerous national initiatives promote financial literacy and inclusion, gaps 
remain  in  translating  financial  knowledge  into  practice  and  in  reaching  employees  who  are  most 
financially vulnerable. This gap is particularly relevant for non-teaching personnel in the DepEd Masbate 
Province  Division  Office,  who  may  experience  fixed  income  constraints  and  household  financial 
burdens.  Thus,  localized  studies  remain  necessary  to  determine  the  prevalence  of  salary  loan 
dependency and identify context-specific strategies that can strengthen financial literacy and improve 
financial well-being among government employees in provincial settings.

Foreign Studies  
Recent global research consistently shows that financial literacy is strongly linked to employee financial 
well-being  and  workplace  performance.  Across  multiple  countries,  financially  literate  employees 
demonstrate stronger budgeting discipline, improved long-term planning, and reduced dependence on 
short-term  credit.  These  financial  behaviors  contribute  to  lower  stress  levels  and  improved  work 
engagement, highlighting financial literacy as both an individual and organizational concern (Wazdi et 
al., 2020; Frees et al., 2024; Kadoya & Khan, 2024).

Studies  in  workplace  contexts  suggest  that  financial  literacy  strengthens  employees’  capacity  to 
manage recurring obligations and cope with unexpected expenses. Employees with stronger financial 
knowledge  are  more  likely  to  plan  for  retirement,  allocate  funds  responsibly,  and  avoid  high-risk 
borrowing behaviors, which ultimately supports their long-term financial stability (Kadoya & Khan, 2024). 
This pattern is further reinforced by global evidence showing that financial literacy improves savings 
behavior  and reduces credit delinquency, especially among low-income  employees  who  are  typically 
more  vulnerable  to  debt  cycles  (Frees  et  al.,  2024).  These  findings  are  relevant  to  government 
employees  such  as  DepEd  personnel  who  often  operate  under  fixed  salaries  and  frequent  financial 
obligations.

METHODS

Research Design 
This study employed a descriptive quantitative research design to examine salary loan dependency and 
financial  literacy  among  non-teaching  personnel  of  the  Department  of  Education,  Masbate  Province 
Division Office. The design was appropriate because the primary purpose of the study was to describe 
existing conditions, patterns, and perceptions related to salary loan usage, financial behavior, and the 
underlying factors that contribute to repeated borrowing among employees. The study did not aim to 
manipulate  variables  or  establish  cause-and-effect  relationships,  but  rather  to  provide  a  clear  and 
systematic description of the current financial situation of employees and the issues surrounding salary 
loan dependency.

A survey method was used as the main data collection technique. This method was suitable because it 
allowed  the  researchers  to  gather  standardized  numerical  data  from  a  relatively  large  group  of 
respondents  within  a  limited  period  of  time.  Through  the  survey,  information  was  collected  on 
respondents’  demographic  characteristics,  loan  profiles,  financial  management  practices,  and 
perceived causes of salary loan dependency. The use of a structured questionnaire ensured consistency 
in responses and facilitated statistical analysis.

The descriptive quantitative design enabled the study to identify common trends and patterns in salary 
loan  usage,  including  the  frequency  of  loan  applications,  the  amount  borrowed,  loan  purposes,  and 
repayment behavior. It also allowed the researchers to quantify respondents’ levels of agreement with 
statements  related  to  financial  literacy,  financial  obligations  and  pressures,  spending  behavior,  and 
accessibility of lending services. By measuring these variables numerically, the study provided objective 
evidence on the extent to which each factor contributes to salary loan dependency.

In addition, the research design supported the assessment of proposed strategies aimed at reducing 
loan dependency and strengthening financial literacy among employees. Respondents were asked to 
evaluate the perceived helpfulness of various interventions, such as financial literacy seminars, financial 
counseling, payroll-based savings schemes, and cooperative reforms. The quantitative design made it 
possible  to  determine  which  strategies  were  most  strongly  supported  by  employees  and,  therefore, 
most feasible for implementation in the workplace.

Overall, the descriptive quantitative research design allowed the study to generate empirical, evidence-
based  findings  that  reflect  the  real  financial  experiences  of  non-teaching  personnel  in  the  DepEd 
Masbate Province Division Office. The results of the study serve as a sound basis for strengthening 
workplace financial literacy and financial wellness programs that are responsive to employees’ needs 
and financial realities.

random selection method through a computer-based random number generator (e.g., Microsoft Excel’s 
RAND function or an online randomizer tool) to select the required sample size of 341 respondents. The 
randomly generated numbers corresponded to the assigned identification numbers of employees in the 
master list. This process ensured that selection was free from researcher bias and that all employees 
had an equal probability of being included.

emergency response. Such a pattern is characteristic of a debt cycle, where loan repayment reduces 
net income and increases the likelihood of reborrowing to cover basic needs (Lusardi & Tufano, 2015). 
This supports the argument that repeated borrowing is a key indicator of dependency because it reflects 
persistent financial shortfalls rather than isolated crises.

In  terms  of  loan  amounts,  many  respondents  borrowed  ₱50,000  or  more,  with  a  notable  number 
borrowing  ₱100,000  or  above.  Larger  amounts  lead  to  longer  repayment  periods  and  heavier  salary

with  studies  showing  that  higher  loan  exposure  increases  financial  vulnerability,  especially  when 
borrowers  rely  on  loans  as  a  routine  source  of  support  rather  than  as  a  planned  financing  tool 
(OECD/INFE,  2018).  In  effect,  higher  borrowing  amounts  may  not  solve  financial  instability  but  may 
instead deepen dependency by limiting the employee’s capacity to save and rebuild financial buffers.

and education-related needs. Borrowing for basic necessities signals income insufficiency and lack of 
emergency savings, which indicates limited financial resilience. Notably, borrowing to pay existing debts

often  occur  when  individuals  lack  adequate  planning  and  financial  cushions,  making  them  more

emergency  expenses,  and  multiple  loans  highlights  the  long-term  financial  consequences  of 
dependency, including reduced flexibility, high stress, and increasing financial insecurity.

3. Underlying Causes of Salary Loan Dependency 
The  findings  indicate  that  salary  loan  dependency  results  from  interacting  personal,  behavioral,  and 
structural factors, rather than a single cause. One of the most prominent causes is inadequate financial 
literacy,  particularly  in  budgeting,  saving,  and  debt  management.  Respondents  admitted  difficulty  in 
creating a budget, managing priorities, and preparing for emergencies. This supports the OECD/INFE 
(2018) explanation that financial literacy is not simply awareness of concepts but the ability to apply 
financial  knowledge  through  effective  financial  behaviors.  In  other  words,  even  when  employees 
recognize financial concepts, the absence of discipline and practical money management skills can still 
lead to repeated borrowing.

Income insufficiency and financial pressure also emerged as a major cause. Many respondents strongly 
agreed that their salary is not enough to meet household needs. This indicates that dependency is not 
entirely driven by poor decision-making but by financial vulnerability. Financial well-being frameworks 
highlight that when income is limited and expenses are high, borrowing becomes an unavoidable coping 
mechanism,  particularly  for  employees  supporting  dependents  (CFPB,  2017).  This  aligns  with  the 
demographic findings showing that most respondents have multiple dependents, increasing household 
financial burdens.

Spending  behavior  was  also  identified  as  a  contributing  factor,  as  some  respondents  admitted  to 
impulsive spending and prioritizing short-term needs over long-term planning. Kim and Garman (2004) 
emphasize  that  poor  financial  self-control  and  lack  of  planning  increase  debt  reliance  because 
individuals fail to establish financial buffers or anticipate irregular expenses. Thus, even when loans are 
initially taken for emergency needs, poor financial planning and repeated exposure to financial stress 
may normalize borrowing behavior.

Finally,  accessibility  and  workplace  exposure  to  lending  services  significantly  reinforce  dependency. 
The convenience of payroll-deduction loans, simplified application processes, and frequent promotions 
creates  an  environment  where  borrowing  becomes  normalized.  The  CFPB  (2017)  emphasizes  that 
borrowing behavior is not purely personal; it is influenced by workplace systems, institutional lending 
practices,  and  social  cues.  This  explains  why  employees  who  see  coworkers  borrowing  may  be 
encouraged to do the same. The workplace environment, therefore, becomes a key factor that shapes 
financial behavior and contributes to debt dependency.

4. Strategies to Reduce Salary Loan Dependency and Enhance Financial Literacy 
The findings indicate strong support for various strategies aimed at reducing salary loans. The findings 
show  strong  support  for  strategies  that  combine  financial  education,  behavioral  support,  and 
institutional  mechanisms.  Respondents  highly  favored  regular  financial  literacy  seminars,  financial 
counseling  services,  and  the  integration  of  financial  literacy  into  employee  orientation  and  training 
programs. This reflects the literature emphasizing that financial education is most effective when it is 
continuous, applied, and directly connected to employees’ real-life financial needs (Lusardi & Mitchell, 
2014). One-time seminars are often insufficient because financial behavior change requires repetition, 
coaching, and reinforcement.

Institutional  strategies  such  as  payroll-based  savings  schemes  and  strengthening  employee 
cooperatives  were  also  strongly  supported.  These  strategies  are  significant  because  they  address 
structural  limitations  by  providing  alternatives  to  borrowing  and  by  enabling  employees  to  build 
emergency reserves. OECD/INFE (2020) emphasizes that automatic savings programs and supportive 
financial systems improve long-term stability among low- and middle-income earners. Therefore, the

CONCLUSION

Based on the findings of the study, the following conclusions were drawn:

1.  The  demographic  profile  of  DepEd  Masbate  non-teaching  personnel  reflects  a  financially 
vulnerable workforce. Most respondents are mid-career employees with relatively low salaries, 
multiple  dependents,  and  limited  years  of  service,  which  collectively  increases  household 
obligations while limiting savings capacity. These conditions heighten financial pressure and 
increase susceptibility to borrowing.

2.  Salary  loan  dependency  among  non-teaching  personnel  is  prevalent  and  recurrent. 
Respondents commonly availed of salary loans multiple times and often borrowed moderate to 
high amounts. Loans were primarily used for basic needs, medical and education expenses, 
and  repayment  of  existing  debts,  suggesting  that  borrowing  has  become  a  routine  coping 
mechanism rather than a one-time emergency response.

3.  Salary  loan  dependency  is  caused  by  interconnected  personal,  behavioral,  and  structural 
factors.  Inadequate  financial  literacy  in  budgeting,  saving,  and  debt  management  limits 
employees’  ability  to  manage  income  effectively.  Income  insufficiency  and  family-related 
financial strain compel repeated borrowing, while spending behaviors and the accessibility and 
normalization of salary loans in the workplace further reinforce dependency.

4.  Reducing salary loan dependency requires sustained and integrated interventions tailored to 
the public-sector workplace context. Respondents strongly supported practical solutions such 
as continuous financial literacy programs, financial counseling and debt support, payroll-based 
savings  schemes,  and  strengthened  employee  cooperatives.  These  strategies  indicate  that 
improving financial well-being is both an individual and institutional responsibility.

5.  This study contributes localized evidence on salary loan dependency within a DepEd division 
setting,  highlighting  the  need  for  policy  and  program  action.  By  documenting  the  patterns, 
causes, and preferred interventions among fixed-income public employees, the study provides

Priority 1: Immediate Interventions (Highest Need Based on Findings)

1.

Institutionalize a structured financial literacy program focused on budgeting, debt control, and 
emergency preparedness. 
This is recommended because respondents identified inadequate budgeting, lack of savings, 
and weak debt management practices as major contributors to salary loan dependency. Since many 
employees reported difficulty in managing income and preparing for emergencies, the division should 
prioritize a financial literacy program that is continuous and skills-based rather than one-time seminars. 
The  program  should  include  practical  modules  on  (a)  budgeting  and  expense  tracking,  (b)  building 
emergency funds, (c) responsible borrowing, (d) debt repayment planning, and (e) long-term financial 
goal setting. According to Lusardi and Mitchell (2014), sustained financial education programs improve 
financial behavior more effectively than isolated interventions.  
Action Point: The division may integrate this program into quarterly HR development sessions and make 
participation part of employee welfare initiatives.

2.  Provide financial counseling and debt management support for employees with multiple or

repeated loans. 
This is prioritized because the loan profile results show that many employees apply for loans 
repeatedly, borrow large amounts, and experience repayment difficulty due to insufficient income and 
multiple  obligations.  This  suggests  that  salary  loans  have  become  a  recurring  coping  strategy, 
potentially  forming  a  debt  cycle.  Providing  confidential  counseling  can  help  employees  restructure 
debts,  create  realistic  repayment  plans,  and  reduce  reborrowing.  Evidence  shows  that  personalized 
financial  counseling  improves  debt  management  and  reduces  financial  stress  among  employees 
(CFPB, 2017). 
Action Point: Establish a referral system through HR where employees can access scheduled financial 
counseling sessions (online or in-person), especially for high-risk borrowers.

Priority 2: Short-Term Interventions (Support Mechanisms to Reduce Borrowing)

3.  Strengthen payroll-based savings schemes to build emergency funds and reduce reliance on

salary loans. 
This recommendation directly addresses the finding that many loans were used for daily living 
expenses and emergency needs, indicating insufficient savings. Automatic savings deductions, even in 
small  amounts,  help  employees  build  financial  buffers  gradually.  OECD/INFE  (2020)  highlights  that 
structured  savings  systems  improve  financial  stability,  especially  among  low-  and  middle-income 
earners. 
Action Point: Introduce voluntary payroll savings options (e.g., ₱200–₱500 per pay period) and allow 
employees to adjust contributions based on capacity.

4.  Strengthen employee cooperatives as a safer alternative to external salary loan providers.

This is necessary because accessibility of lending services and workplace promotion of loans 
was identified as a key factor reinforcing dependency. Strengthening cooperatives provides employees 
with  lower-interest  options  and  encourages  savings  participation.  Cooperatives  can  also  serve  as  a 
platform for financial education and peer support. 
Action Point: Review cooperative policies to improve loan terms, create emergency loan products with 
lower deductions, and expand financial education services within cooperative programs.

5.  Regulate and limit aggressive salary loan promotions within the workplace.

ABSTRACT
This  study  examined  the  prevalence  and  causes  of  salary  loan  dependency  among  non-
teaching employees of the Department of Education Schools Division of Masbate Province.
It also aimed to identify practical strategies that can reduce repeated borrowing and serve
as a basis for strengthening a financial literacy program. The study employed a descriptive
quantitative  research  design  using  a  survey  questionnaire.  Respondents  were  341  non-
teaching  personnel  selected  through  simple  random  sampling.  The  sample  size  was
determined using Cochran’s formula with finite population correction, and the final size was
increased to 341 to account for non-response and improve precision. Data were analyzed
using  frequency,  percentage,  mean,  and  related  descriptive  statistics.  Ethical  standards
were observed through voluntary participation, confidentiality, and informed consent. Most
respondents were aged 36 to 45 and the majority were married, with many earning 30,000
pesos or below and supporting several dependents. A large portion had applied for salary
loans,  commonly  borrowing  for  daily  living  expenses  and  debt  repayment.  Respondents
agreed  that  salary  loan  dependency  is  influenced  by  financial  literacy  gaps,  financial
obligations,  and  pressures  such  as  insufficient  income  and  emergencies,  spending
behavior,  and  easy  access  to
lending  services.  Respondents  also  rated  multiple
interventions  as  very  helpful,  particularly  financial  literacy  seminars,  financial  counseling
and debt management support, strengthening cooperatives with better terms, and payroll

INTRODUCTION

Financial well-being has become a critical concern in contemporary workplaces, as it directly affects
employees’ stability, productivity, and overall quality of life. Persistent financial strain can lead to stress,
anxiety,  and  reduced  concentration,  which  may  negatively  influence  job  performance  and  decision-
making (Pomroy, 2021). For employees with fixed incomes and increasing household responsibilities,
borrowing often becomes a common coping strategy to address financial shortfalls. While loans may
provide  short-term  relief,  repeated  borrowing  can  result  in  long-term  financial  vulnerability  and
dependency.

At the global level, financial literacy is widely recognized as a vital life skill that enables individuals to
manage personal finances responsibly and avoid harmful debt cycles. The Organisation for Economic
Co-operation and Development emphasizes that effective financial education should equip individuals
with  the  knowledge  and  skills  necessary  for  budgeting,  saving,  debt  management,  and  long-term
financial  planning  (OECD  INFE,  2020).  Similarly,  financial  capability  is  highlighted  in  international
development  agendas  as  a  means  to  promote  economic  empowerment,  financial  inclusion,  and
resilience against income shocks.

In response to these global directions, the Philippine government has institutionalized financial literacy
initiatives  for  public  sector  employees.  Republic  Act  No.  10922,  also  known  as  the  Economic  and
Financial  Literacy  Act,  mandated  the  promotion  of  financial  education  across  government  agencies
through the annual observance of Economic and Financial Literacy Week. This was further reinforced
by  Executive  Order  No.  32,  s.  2017,  which  directed  the  development  of  financial  literacy  programs
tailored  specifically  for  government  personnel.  These  policies  underscore  the  state’s  recognition  of
financial literacy as an essential component of employee welfare and good governance.

Within the education sector, the Department of Education has issued complementary policies to support
employee financial wellness. DepEd Memorandum No. 121, s. 2019 encouraged the conduct of financial
literacy seminars for personnel, while DepEd Order No. 5, s. 2021, integrated employee wellness and
financial  education  into  the  Results-Based  Performance  Management  System.  More  recently,  DepEd
Order  No.  12,  s.  2023  emphasized  employee  welfare  programs  that  promote  responsible  financial
behavior and overall well-being. These directives reflect DepEd’s commitment to improving the financial
capability of its workforce.

Despite the presence of these national and institutional initiatives, financial distress remains prevalent
among  DepEd  personnel,  particularly  in  economically  disadvantaged  provinces  such  as  Masbate.
Evidence  noted  in  the  study  indicates  that  while  financial  incentives  such  as  midyear  and  year-end
bonuses provide temporary relief, many employees’ take-home pay is substantially reduced by recurring
loan deductions (Madrona, 2017). After mandatory deductions and repayments, employees are often
left with limited disposable income, which may be insufficient to meet basic household needs. As a

Addressing salary loan dependency, therefore, requires more than policy issuance. It calls for a deeper
understanding of the financial realities, behaviors, and constraints experienced by employees. Financial
literacy, defined as the ability to apply knowledge and skills in budgeting, saving, and debt management,
plays  a  crucial  role  in  promoting  responsible  borrowing  and  long-term  financial  stability  (Lusardi  &
Tufano, 2015). In recognition of this need, the Civil Service Commission, in partnership with the Bangko
Sentral ng Pilipinas and BDO Foundation, launched the Financial Education Program for Civil Servants
to strengthen financial knowledge, debt management skills, and financial resilience among government
employees (CSC, 2023).

However, there remains a lack of localized empirical studies that examine salary loan dependency and
financial literacy within specific public sector workplaces, particularly among non-teaching personnel in
provincial DepEd division offices. Most existing discussions focus on general employee debt patterns
or national-level programs, leaving the lived financial experiences, causes of borrowing, and preferred
interventions  of  employees  in  areas  like  Masbate  underexplored.  Without  such  evidence,  financial
literacy programs risk being generic and disconnected from actual employee needs.

This  study  is  guided  by  the  Theory  of  Planned  Behavior  (Ajzen,  1991),  Financial  Capability  Theory
(Sherraden, 2013), and the Theory of Resource Allocation. The Theory of Planned Behavior explains that
borrowing behavior is shaped by attitudes toward loans, workplace norms, and perceived control over
financial  decisions.  Financial  Capability  Theory  emphasizes  that  financial  well-being  depends  on
financial  knowledge  and  the  ability  to  apply  it  through  budgeting,  saving,  and  responsible  debt
management.  The  Theory  of  Resource  Allocation  explains  how  employees  allocate  limited  income
across  competing  household  needs,  which  may  increase  reliance  on  loans  when  financial  demands
exceed  available  resources.  Anchored  in  these  frameworks,  the  study  examines  how  demographic
factors and financial capability-related causes influence salary loan dependency and informs strategies
for strengthening workplace financial literacy programs.

This  study  addresses  this  gap  by  determining  the  demographic  and  loan  profiles  of  non-teaching
personnel in the Department of Education, Masbate Province Division Office, identifying the underlying
causes  of  salary  loan  dependency,  and  determining  strategies  that  employees  consider  effective  in
reducing repeated borrowing. The findings of the study serve as a basis for strengthening a financial
literacy  program  that  is  contextualized,  continuous,  and  responsive  to  the  financial  realities  of
employees,  intending  to  promote  sustainable  financial  well-being  and  reduce  dependency  on  salary
loans.

REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE

Foreign Literature
Financial  well-being  is  widely  recognized  as  a  multidimensional  concept  that  reflects  both  an
individual’s  objective  financial  condition  and  their  subjective  assessment  of  financial  security,
satisfaction, and perceived control over financial life (Grable & Joo, 2001; Brüggen et al., 2017). This
concept goes beyond income level alone and includes a person’s capacity to meet current obligations,
maintain savings, handle emergencies, and plan for future financial goals. Studies consistently show
that  weak  financial  well-being  is  associated  with  increased  stress,  reduced  productivity,  and  poorer
physical and mental health outcomes (Schreiner & Sherraden, 2007; Lichtenstein et al., 2009). These
effects  become  particularly  evident  among  employees  who  experience  financial  strain,  as  financial
distress often translates into workplace consequences such as reduced concentration, absenteeism,
and lower job satisfaction (Burton, 2012; Harrison et al., 2019).

One  of  the  strongest  predictors  of  financial  well-being  is  financial  literacy.  Financial  literacy  is
commonly defined as the knowledge, skills, and confidence necessary to manage financial resources
effectively—particularly in relation to budgeting, saving, investing, and debt management (Atkinson &
Messy,  2012;  OECD,  2014).  Lusardi  and  Mitchell  (2013)  emphasized  the  economic  importance  of
financial literacy, noting that individuals who understand basic financial concepts tend to make better
financial decisions, avoid high-risk debt, and maintain greater financial stability. Financial literacy also
strengthens  financial  capability,  which  refers  to  the  practical  application  of  knowledge  in  real-life
financial  situations  (Xiao  &  O’Neill,  2016).  Moreover,  research  highlights  that  subjective  financial

Inadequate financial literacy, however, has been consistently linked to financial mismanagement and

reported  that  workers  caught  in  repeated  debt  cycles  often  experience  both  economic  and

The  consequences  of  salary  loan  dependency  extend  beyond  economic  hardship.  Tung  (2013)
highlighted that excessive borrowing leads to emotional distress, anxiety, and reduced sense of control,
which can significantly undermine an employee’s overall well-being. These psychological effects are
especially relevant in organizational settings, where employee financial distress may result in reduced
productivity and poorer job performance. Studies on workplace well-being have long emphasized that
employee  wellness  is  interconnected:  financial  instability  often  contributes  to  decreased  workplace
functioning and job satisfaction, making financial wellness a legitimate organizational concern (Burton,
2012; Harrison et al., 2019).

In response to these concerns, research supports the implementation of financial literacy and financial
wellness  interventions  in  the  workplace.  Volpe  et  al.  (2006)  reported  that  employees  exposed  to
financial education programs gain greater confidence and improved financial decision-making skills,
which can result in reduced stress and better financial management behaviors. Likewise, Xiao and Porto
(2017) found that financial education improves financial satisfaction when knowledge is translated into
behavior and capability. Workplace-based financial wellness programs—such as seminars, counseling,
debt management assistance, and financial planning workshops—have been associated with improved
savings behavior and better financial outcomes (Kapoor & Duflo, 2014; Ruder & Mott, 2017). Employers
who invest in financial wellness strategies also benefit through higher employee engagement, improved
performance, and increased retention (Harrison et al., 2019).

However,  researchers  emphasize  that  financial  education  alone  may  not  be  sufficient  without
institutional mechanisms that support financial resilience. Schreiner and Sherraden (2007) argued that
savings  tools  and  asset-building  interventions  are  essential  for  helping  individuals  escape  recurring
debt cycles. Programs such as payroll-based savings schemes, emergency funds, cooperative-based
alternatives, and structured financial planning tools can provide employees with practical options that
reduce the need to borrow repeatedly. Such interventions are especially relevant for employees with
limited income and multiple financial obligations, where borrowing often becomes the default coping
strategy.

Local Literature
Financial well-being among Filipino employees has been widely examined due to its direct influence on
workplace functioning and overall life satisfaction. Pascual and Banzon (2019) reported that financial
stress  is  strongly  associated  with  lower  productivity,  reduced  engagement,  and  higher  absenteeism
among Filipino workers, reinforcing the view that financial security is not only a personal concern but
also a workplace issue. Similarly, Macalalag and Tria (2016) found that many Filipino employees live
paycheck to paycheck, which increases vulnerability to financial shocks and contributes to persistent
stress. These studies highlight how limited financial resources can significantly affect both personal
well-being  and  organizational  performance,  particularly  among  salaried  workers  in  government
institutions.

Within  this  context,  salary  loan  dependency  has  emerged  as  a  common  coping  mechanism  among
Filipino workers, especially those with constrained income and limited access to sustainable financial
resources. Noche (2015) and Delos Reyes (2018) observed that while salary loans provide immediate
financial relief, repeated borrowing often leads to a recurring debt cycle that is difficult to escape. This
pattern is reinforced by the increasing availability of salary loan products in the country. The Bangko
Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP, 2017) noted that the growing demand for quick-access loans has expanded
the salary loan market, raising concerns about long-term borrower stability—particularly when loans are
repeatedly renewed or acquired from lenders imposing high interest rates.

A  key  factor  contributing  to  loan  dependency  is  inadequate  financial  literacy.  Teves  (2017),  citing
findings  from  Philippine  Business  for  Social  Progress,  reported  that  a  significant  portion  of  the
Philippine  workforce  lacks  basic  financial  skills  such  as  budgeting,  saving,  and  informed  decision-
making. Liu (2016) and Racelis (2015) further noted that financial literacy education in the Philippines
remains  insufficient  both  in  schools  and  within  workplace  settings.  Without  practical  training,
employees are more likely to engage in poor financial behaviors such as overspending, lack of savings,
and excessive reliance on credit, which heightens their vulnerability to recurring debt. Manlapig et al.
(2018) supported this observation, emphasizing that while access to loans has improved, many workers
remain unprepared to manage debt effectively and are often unaware of healthier financial alternatives,
thereby reinforcing dependency.

In response to this national concern, several government and institutional efforts have been launched
to improve financial literacy and reduce financial vulnerability. Tuncel and Maglalang (2020) reported
that  financial  literacy  programs  implemented  in  various  institutions  improved  awareness  of  basic
financial concepts, yet many interventions lacked practical application and failed to translate knowledge
into daily financial behavior. Similarly, the Philippine Financial Literacy Program (2019), spearheaded by
the  BSP  and  partner  institutions,  provides  seminars  and  resources  aimed  at  improving  financial
awareness, but Iñigo (2018) observed that participation remains low among low-income employees and
those in rural areas. This suggests that financial literacy initiatives are often inaccessible to the groups
who  need  them  most,  emphasizing  the  importance  of  integrating  financial  education  into  employee
development and welfare programs.

Foreign Studies
Recent global research consistently shows that financial literacy is strongly linked to employee financial

METHODS

Research Design
This study employed a descriptive quantitative research design to examine salary loan dependency and
financial  literacy  among  non-teaching  personnel  of  the  Department  of  Education,  Masbate  Province
Division Office. The design was appropriate because the primary purpose of the study was to describe
existing conditions, patterns, and perceptions related to salary loan usage, financial behavior, and the
underlying factors that contribute to repeated borrowing among employees. The study did not aim to
manipulate  variables  or  establish  cause-and-effect  relationships,  but  rather  to  provide  a  clear  and
systematic description of the current financial situation of employees and the issues surrounding salary
loan dependency.

A survey method was used as the main data collection technique. This method was suitable because it
allowed  the  researchers  to  gather  standardized  numerical  data  from  a  relatively  large  group  of
respondents  within  a  limited  period  of  time.  Through  the  survey,  information  was  collected  on
respondents’  demographic  characteristics,  loan  profiles,  financial  management  practices,  and
perceived causes of salary loan dependency. The use of a structured questionnaire ensured consistency
in responses and facilitated statistical analysis.

The descriptive quantitative design enabled the study to identify common trends and patterns in salary
loan  usage,  including  the  frequency  of  loan  applications,  the  amount  borrowed,  loan  purposes,  and
repayment behavior. It also allowed the researchers to quantify respondents’ levels of agreement with
statements  related  to  financial  literacy,  financial  obligations  and  pressures,  spending  behavior,  and
accessibility of lending services. By measuring these variables numerically, the study provided objective
evidence on the extent to which each factor contributes to salary loan dependency.

In addition, the research design supported the assessment of proposed strategies aimed at reducing
loan dependency and strengthening financial literacy among employees. Respondents were asked to
evaluate the perceived helpfulness of various interventions, such as financial literacy seminars, financial
counseling, payroll-based savings schemes, and cooperative reforms. The quantitative design made it
possible  to  determine  which  strategies  were  most  strongly  supported  by  employees  and,  therefore,
most feasible for implementation in the workplace.

Overall, the descriptive quantitative research design allowed the study to generate empirical, evidence-
based  findings  that  reflect  the  real  financial  experiences  of  non-teaching  personnel  in  the  DepEd
Masbate Province Division Office. The results of the study serve as a sound basis for strengthening
workplace financial literacy and financial wellness programs that are responsive to employees’ needs
and financial realities.

(Lusardi  &  Mitchell,  2014).  In  this  context,  salary  loan  dependency  may  be  interpreted  not  only  as  a
personal financial weakness but also as a coping response to life-stage financial demands.

2. Loan Profile of the Respondents
The  loan  profile  findings  show  that  salary  loan  dependency  is  widespread  and  often  repetitive,  with
many respondents reporting multiple loan applications within the past three years. The recurrence of
borrowing indicates that salary loans have become a routine financial strategy rather than a one-time
emergency response. Such a pattern is characteristic of a debt cycle, where loan repayment reduces
net income and increases the likelihood of reborrowing to cover basic needs (Lusardi & Tufano, 2015).
This supports the argument that repeated borrowing is a key indicator of dependency because it reflects
persistent financial shortfalls rather than isolated crises.

In  terms  of  loan  amounts,  many  respondents  borrowed  ₱50,000  or  more,  with  a  notable  number
borrowing  ₱100,000  or  above.  Larger  amounts  lead  to  longer  repayment  periods  and  heavier  salary
deductions, which can significantly reduce disposable income and worsen financial strain. This aligns
with  studies  showing  that  higher  loan  exposure  increases  financial  vulnerability,  especially  when
borrowers  rely  on  loans  as  a  routine  source  of  support  rather  than  as  a  planned  financing  tool
(OECD/INFE,  2018).  In  effect,  higher  borrowing  amounts  may  not  solve  financial  instability  but  may
instead deepen dependency by limiting the employee’s capacity to save and rebuild financial buffers.

The most common loan purposes included daily living expenses, payment of existing debts, and medical
and education-related needs. Borrowing for basic necessities signals income insufficiency and lack of
emergency savings, which indicates limited financial resilience. Notably, borrowing to pay existing debts
suggests  revolving  credit  behavior  and  highlights  the  possible  presence  of  long-term  debt  burden
among employees. Lusardi and Tufano (2015) argue that debt-related stress and repeated borrowing
often  occur  when  individuals  lack  adequate  planning  and  financial  cushions,  making  them  more
susceptible to cycles of repayment and reborrowing.
While  salary  deduction  repayment  promotes  consistent  payment,  it  can  also  be  a  structural  factor
reinforcing dependency, because employees receive lower take-home pay and may be forced to borrow
again to meet household expenses. This is consistent with debt behavior literature emphasizing that
automatic deductions can create hidden financial strain if employees fail to budget around reduced net
income (CFPB, 2017). Moreover, the reported missed or delayed payments due to insufficient income,
emergency  expenses,  and  multiple  loans  highlights  the  long-term  financial  consequences  of
dependency, including reduced flexibility, high stress, and increasing financial insecurity.

3. Underlying Causes of Salary Loan Dependency
The  findings  indicate  that  salary  loan  dependency  results  from  interacting  personal,  behavioral,  and
structural factors, rather than a single cause. One of the most prominent causes is inadequate financial
literacy,  particularly  in  budgeting,  saving,  and  debt  management.  Respondents  admitted  difficulty  in
creating a budget, managing priorities, and preparing for emergencies. This supports the OECD/INFE
(2018) explanation that financial literacy is not simply awareness of concepts but the ability to apply
financial  knowledge  through  effective  financial  behaviors.  In  other  words,  even  when  employees
recognize financial concepts, the absence of discipline and practical money management skills can still
lead to repeated borrowing.

Income insufficiency and financial pressure also emerged as a major cause. Many respondents strongly
agreed that their salary is not enough to meet household needs. This indicates that dependency is not
entirely driven by poor decision-making but by financial vulnerability. Financial well-being frameworks
highlight that when income is limited and expenses are high, borrowing becomes an unavoidable coping
mechanism,  particularly  for  employees  supporting  dependents  (CFPB,  2017).  This  aligns  with  the
demographic findings showing that most respondents have multiple dependents, increasing household
financial burdens.

Spending  behavior  was  also  identified  as  a  contributing  factor,  as  some  respondents  admitted  to
impulsive spending and prioritizing short-term needs over long-term planning. Kim and Garman (2004)
emphasize  that  poor  financial  self-control  and  lack  of  planning  increase  debt  reliance  because
individuals fail to establish financial buffers or anticipate irregular expenses. Thus, even when loans are
initially taken for emergency needs, poor financial planning and repeated exposure to financial stress
may normalize borrowing behavior.

Finally,  accessibility  and  workplace  exposure  to  lending  services  significantly  reinforce  dependency.
The convenience of payroll-deduction loans, simplified application processes, and frequent promotions
creates  an  environment  where  borrowing  becomes  normalized.  The  CFPB  (2017)  emphasizes  that
borrowing behavior is not purely personal; it is influenced by workplace systems, institutional lending
practices,  and  social  cues.  This  explains  why  employees  who  see  coworkers  borrowing  may  be
encouraged to do the same. The workplace environment, therefore, becomes a key factor that shapes
financial behavior and contributes to debt dependency.

4. Strategies to Reduce Salary Loan Dependency and Enhance Financial Literacy
The findings indicate strong support for various strategies aimed at reducing salary loans. The findings
show  strong  support  for  strategies  that  combine  financial  education,  behavioral  support,  and
institutional  mechanisms.  Respondents  highly  favored  regular  financial  literacy  seminars,  financial
counseling  services,  and  the  integration  of  financial  literacy  into  employee  orientation  and  training
programs. This reflects the literature emphasizing that financial education is most effective when it is
continuous, applied, and directly connected to employees’ real-life financial needs (Lusardi & Mitchell,
2014). One-time seminars are often insufficient because financial behavior change requires repetition,
coaching, and reinforcement.

cooperatives  were  also  strongly  supported.  These  strategies  are  significant  because  they  address
structural  limitations  by  providing  alternatives  to  borrowing  and  by  enabling  employees  to  build
emergency reserves. OECD/INFE (2020) emphasizes that automatic savings programs and supportive

1.  The  demographic  profile  of  DepEd  Masbate  non-teaching  personnel  reflects  a  financially
vulnerable workforce. Most respondents are mid-career employees with relatively low salaries,
multiple  dependents,  and  limited  years  of  service,  which  collectively  increases  household
obligations while limiting savings capacity. These conditions heighten financial pressure and
increase susceptibility to borrowing.

2.  Salary  loan  dependency  among  non-teaching  personnel  is  prevalent  and  recurrent.
Respondents commonly availed of salary loans multiple times and often borrowed moderate to
high amounts. Loans were primarily used for basic needs, medical and education expenses,
and  repayment  of  existing  debts,  suggesting  that  borrowing  has  become  a  routine  coping
mechanism rather than a one-time emergency response.

3.  Salary  loan  dependency  is  caused  by  interconnected  personal,  behavioral,  and  structural
factors.  Inadequate  financial  literacy  in  budgeting,  saving,  and  debt  management  limits
employees’  ability  to  manage  income  effectively.  Income  insufficiency  and  family-related
financial strain compel repeated borrowing, while spending behaviors and the accessibility and
normalization of salary loans in the workplace further reinforce dependency.

4.  Reducing salary loan dependency requires sustained and integrated interventions tailored to
the public-sector workplace context. Respondents strongly supported practical solutions such
as continuous financial literacy programs, financial counseling and debt support, payroll-based
savings  schemes,  and  strengthened  employee  cooperatives.  These  strategies  indicate  that
improving financial well-being is both an individual and institutional responsibility.

5.  This study contributes localized evidence on salary loan dependency within a DepEd division
setting,  highlighting  the  need  for  policy  and  program  action.  By  documenting  the  patterns,
causes, and preferred interventions among fixed-income public employees, the study provides
initiatives  and  workplace  support
empirical  basis  for  strengthening  financial

These recommendations are prioritized according to the top-rated causes of salary loan dependency
and the most strongly supported strategies identified by the respondents. Prioritization ensures that
interventions address the most urgent needs and provide actionable direction for the Department of
Education, Masbate Province Division Office.

Priority 1: Immediate Interventions (Highest Need Based on Findings)

1.

Institutionalize a structured financial literacy program focused on budgeting, debt control, and
emergency preparedness.
This is recommended because respondents identified inadequate budgeting, lack of savings,
and weak debt management practices as major contributors to salary loan dependency. Since many
employees reported difficulty in managing income and preparing for emergencies, the division should
prioritize a financial literacy program that is continuous and skills-based rather than one-time seminars.
The  program  should  include  practical  modules  on  (a)  budgeting  and  expense  tracking,  (b)  building
emergency funds, (c) responsible borrowing, (d) debt repayment planning, and (e) long-term financial
goal setting. According to Lusardi and Mitchell (2014), sustained financial education programs improve
financial behavior more effectively than isolated interventions.
Action Point: The division may integrate this program into quarterly HR development sessions and make
participation part of employee welfare initiatives.

2.  Provide financial counseling and debt management support for employees with multiple or

repeated loans.
This is prioritized because the loan profile results show that many employees apply for loans
repeatedly, borrow large amounts, and experience repayment difficulty due to insufficient income and
multiple  obligations.  This  suggests  that  salary  loans  have  become  a  recurring  coping  strategy,
potentially  forming  a  debt  cycle.  Providing  confidential  counseling  can  help  employees  restructure
debts,  create  realistic  repayment  plans,  and  reduce  reborrowing.  Evidence  shows  that  personalized
financial  counseling  improves  debt  management  and  reduces  financial  stress  among  employees
(CFPB, 2017).
Action Point: Establish a referral system through HR where employees can access scheduled financial
counseling sessions (online or in-person), especially for high-risk borrowers.

Priority 2: Short-Term Interventions (Support Mechanisms to Reduce Borrowing)

3.  Strengthen payroll-based savings schemes to build emergency funds and reduce reliance on

salary loans.
This recommendation directly addresses the finding that many loans were used for daily living
expenses and emergency needs, indicating insufficient savings. Automatic savings deductions, even in
small  amounts,  help  employees  build  financial  buffers  gradually.  OECD/INFE  (2020)  highlights  that
structured  savings  systems  improve  financial  stability,  especially  among  low-  and  middle-income
earners.
Action Point: Introduce voluntary payroll savings options (e.g., ₱200–₱500 per pay period) and allow
employees to adjust contributions based on capacity.

4.  Strengthen employee cooperatives as a safer alternative to external salary loan providers.

This is necessary because accessibility of lending services and workplace promotion of loans
was identified as a key factor reinforcing dependency. Strengthening cooperatives provides employees
with  lower-interest  options  and  encourages  savings  participation.  Cooperatives  can  also  serve  as  a
platform for financial education and peer support.
Action Point: Review cooperative policies to improve loan terms, create emergency loan products with
lower deductions, and expand financial education services within cooperative programs.

Priority 3: Medium-Term Workplace Policy Reforms (Reduce Institutional Drivers of Dependency)

5.  Regulate and limit aggressive salary loan promotions within the workplace.

Priority 4: Long-Term Research and Policy Development

6.  Conduct follow-up and longitudinal studies to monitor changes in financial behavior and loan

dependency.
This is recommended to evaluate whether interventions reduce dependency over time. Future
studies may include regression or correlation analysis to determine predictors of loan dependency and
examine long-term impact of financial literacy programs. Qualitative research may also be conducted
to  explore  employee  decision-making,  lived  experiences,  and  financial  coping  behaviors  in  deeper
context.

Implementing  these  prioritized  recommendations  may  strengthen  financial  resilience  among  DepEd
non-teaching  personnel  by  addressing  the  most  pressing  causes  of  salary  loan  dependency—
particularly  weak  financial  management  practices,  income-related  strain,  and  reliance  on  accessible
lending  systems.  A  combined  approach  of  continuous  financial  literacy  training,  counseling  support,
structured savings mechanisms, and workplace policy reforms is essential to reduce dependency and
promote sustainable financial well-being.
