# A--Sireesha-2026

## Summary

The paper presents an AI-Based Personal Finance Manager, a system that combines supervised machine learning, deep learning, rule-based logic and reinforcement learning to automate personal financial management. The stated purpose is to relieve users of manual monitoring of the large volumes of transaction data generated by online banking, e-commerce, UPI payments and digital wallets (Introduction, p. 305). The system is described as having four primary modules: data acquisition, AI-driven analytics, a prediction and recommendation engine, and a user interface layer (Abstract, p. 304). Users upload or link financial transaction data, which is cleaned, tokenized and classified using models such as Random Forest, Gradient Boosting Machines or LSTM-based sequence models (Abstract, pp. 304-305).

The reported capabilities are expense classification, budget planning, financial forecasting, savings optimisation, bill reminders and anomaly detection for unusual transactions (Abstract, p. 304). Machine learning models such as Random Forest, SVM and neural networks classify transactions into categories including food, transportation, entertainment, rent and utilities, while an LSTM model predicts future expenses and revenue streams from historical patterns, and clustering algorithms such as K-Means identify unusual spending patterns and assign behavioural profiles (Introduction, p. 306). Anomaly detection uses Isolation Forest to alert users to suspicious transactions or overspending, and final recommendations come from a hybrid model combining rule-based logic, supervised ML and reinforcement learning (Introduction, p. 306).

The evaluation reports a Random Forest-based expense classifier at 93% to 96% accuracy, against a stated 75-80% plateau for conventional rule-based categorisation systems; an LSTM forecasting Mean Absolute Error of 4.7%; a usability study with 50 participants in which 84% reported better spending awareness and 78% reported increased control over savings after one month; and anomaly-detection precision of 92% (Results & Discussion, pp. 308-309). The paper argues that AI-driven personalisation is the key differentiator from static budgeting tools because the system recalibrates recommendations in response to user compliance (Results & Discussion, p. 309).

## Problem and Motivation

The paper frames the problem as an overload of personal financial data: rapid growth in digital financial services has produced an "overwhelming increase in the volume, variety, and complexity of personal financial data", and users struggle to monitor spending, manage budgets, forecast savings and make informed decisions because of limited time, financial literacy or analytical tools (Abstract, p. 304). Manual monitoring is described as impractical given that users generate thousands of transactions monthly through online banking, e-commerce, UPI payments and digital wallets (Introduction, p. 305).

The paper's stated motivation is that traditional finance-management tools rely heavily on static rules and offer limited personalisation and insight, whereas AI can bring dynamic, adaptive and intelligent capabilities to personal finance management (Introduction, p. 305). Existing systems are said to lack advanced predictive capabilities and personalised insights; supervised models for expense prediction depend heavily on data quality and may not adapt to dynamic user behaviour; deep-learning models require large datasets and computational resources; and personalisation systems based on collaborative and content-based filtering may suffer cold-start problems when user data is limited (Related Work, pp. 307-308). The paper further states that existing systems face data privacy concerns, lack of explainability and limited integration of multiple AI techniques into a unified platform, and that the proposed system aims to address these gaps by combining machine learning, deep learning and intelligent recommendation techniques (Related Work, p. 308).

## Method

**Design.** The paper states no formal design label. It describes itself as presenting "the design, development, and evaluation of an AI-Based Personal Finance Manager" (Abstract, p. 304) and reports a built-system evaluation with a small usability study; no controlled comparison, ablation or held-out benchmark protocol is described.

**Sample.** No N is reported for the transaction dataset, which is described only as "a diverse dataset of transactional logs from digital wallets, online banking statements, and categorized receipts" (Results & Discussion, p. 308); the usability study reports n = 50 participants (Results & Discussion, p. 308). Unit of analysis: the individual user and their financial transactions.

**Context.** geography: Not reported (no user, dataset or deployment geography is stated; the author affiliation is Chalapathi Institute of Technology, Guntur, Andhra Pradesh, India); population: users of personal finance management applications and their transaction records; setting: computational — an AI-based personal finance system evaluated on transaction logs plus a one-month usability study with 50 participants.

Procedure as described by the paper:

- Collect financial transaction data from banking statements, e-commerce receipts and user-provided inputs (Methodology, p. 306).
- Preprocess by cleaning data, normalising, tokenizing merchant names and mapping transaction labels to standardised categories (Methodology, p. 306).
- Classify transactions into categories such as food, transportation, entertainment, rent or utilities using Random Forest, SVM or neural networks (Introduction, p. 306).
- Predict future expenses and revenue streams from historical patterns with an LSTM model (Introduction, p. 306).
- Identify unusual spending patterns and categorise user behavioural profiles with clustering algorithms such as K-Means (Introduction, p. 306).
- Detect anomalies with Isolation Forest to alert users about suspicious transactions or overspending (Introduction, p. 306).
- Generate final recommendations with a hybrid model combining rule-based logic, supervised ML and reinforcement learning (Introduction, p. 306).
- Refine recommendations continuously with reinforcement learning based on user behaviour and historical response patterns (Abstract, p. 304).
- Structure the system as four primary modules: data acquisition, AI-driven analytics, prediction and recommendation engine, and user interface layer (Abstract, p. 304).
- Evaluate the built system on the transaction dataset and via a usability study with 50 participants over one month (Results & Discussion, p. 308).

## Key Findings

- num: The Random Forest-based expense classifier is reported at an accuracy range of 93% to 96%, against conventional rule-based categorisation systems that "typically plateau at around 75–80% accuracy" (Results & Discussion, p. 308).
- num: The LSTM forecasting model is reported at a Mean Absolute Error (MAE) of 4.7% for predicting monthly expenditures, and is said to estimate recurring payments such as rent, subscriptions and utility bills (Results & Discussion, p. 308).
- num: The usability study involved 50 participants; 84% reported better awareness of their spending and 78% reported increased control over savings after one month of use (Results & Discussion, p. 308).
- num: Anomaly detection is reported at a precision of 92% for flagging unusual transactions (Results & Discussion, p. 308).
- num: K-Means clustering is said to label users into financial personas such as "budget-conscious", "impulsive spender" or "balanced planner" (Results & Discussion, p. 308).
- The recommendation engine's personalisation is said to increase user satisfaction, and the system is described as adapting continuously via reinforcement learning, recalibrating budget goals when a user consistently ignores warnings (Results & Discussion, p. 309).
- Visualisation tools (heat maps of spending, monthly trend lines, category-wise pie charts) are reported as usable, with users said to prefer visual data-driven insights over textual summaries (Results & Discussion, p. 309).
- The paper reports that younger users benefited from automated budgeting while older users valued predictive features more (Results & Discussion, p. 309).
- The paper concludes that the system is a proactive financial assistant rather than merely a tracking tool (Results & Discussion, p. 309).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The paper acknowledges that existing systems — and by extension the design space it works in — face data privacy concerns, lack of explainability in AI models, and limited integration of multiple AI techniques into a unified platform (Related Work, p. 308). It claims to address these but reports no privacy or explainability measurement.
- The paper acknowledges that personalisation systems based on collaborative and content-based filtering "may suffer from cold-start problems when user data is limited" (Related Work, p. 308), and that supervised models "may not adapt well to dynamic user behavior" (Related Work, p. 307).
- The paper acknowledges that deep-learning techniques "require large datasets and computational resources" (Related Work, p. 307).
- The paper lists future work rather than resolving present gaps: blockchain for tamper-proof financial logs, investment portfolio analysis using reinforcement learning, voice-driven interaction, and federated learning for privacy-preserving training (Conclusion, p. 310).
- [unacknowledged] No N is reported for the transaction dataset used in the evaluation; the paper says only that "a diverse dataset of transactional logs" was used (Results & Discussion, p. 308), so no sampling frame, size, period or source is recoverable.
- [unacknowledged] The "System Architecture" section is headed "III. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE" but contains no body text between the heading and "IV. RESULTS & DISCUSSION" (p. 308); the four-module architecture is asserted in the abstract but never described.
- [unacknowledged] The 75-80% rule-based baseline is a literature claim ("typically plateau at around 75–80% accuracy"), not a baseline measured in this study; no comparison model was trained or evaluated on the same data (Results & Discussion, p. 308).
- [unacknowledged] No train/test split, cross-validation, confidence interval, p-value, precision, recall, F1 or confusion matrix is reported for the classifier; the accuracy is given only as a range (93-96%) with no point estimate (Results & Discussion, p. 308).
- [unacknowledged] The LSTM MAE of 4.7% is not defined against a unit, scale or normalisation, and no baseline forecasting model is compared (Results & Discussion, p. 308).
- [unacknowledged] The abstract claims Gradient Boosting Machines and reinforcement learning as components, but the results section reports results only for Random Forest, LSTM and Isolation Forest; no result is reported for Gradient Boosting, SVM, NLP, the chatbot, or the reinforcement-learning recommender (Abstract, p. 304; Results & Discussion, pp. 308-309).
- [unacknowledged] The usability study is self-reported with no instrument named (no SUS, no ISO/IEC 25010), no sampling procedure, no control condition and no statistical test (Results & Discussion, p. 308).
- [unacknowledged] No software, library, framework, hyperparameter or model-version detail is reported, so none of the reported results are reproducible.
- [unacknowledged] The paper reports no geographic or population context for the evaluation data or the usability participants, so external validity is untested (Results & Discussion, p. 308).
- [unacknowledged] The section numbering is internally inconsistent: "III. Related Work" and "III. SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE" both carry the number III (pp. 306, 308).

## Definitions

- **AI-Based Personal Finance Manager** — The paper's proposed system; automates expense classification, budget planning, financial forecasting, savings optimisation, bill reminders and anomaly detection (Abstract, p. 304).
- **Data acquisition module** — First of four primary modules; handles upload or linking of financial transaction data (Abstract, p. 304).
- **AI-driven analytics module** — Second of four modules; cleans, tokenizes and classifies transaction data (Abstract, pp. 304-305).
- **Prediction and recommendation engine** — Third of four modules; generates saving, budget-adherence, expense-reduction, investment and goal-planning suggestions (Abstract, p. 304).
- **User interface layer** — Fourth of four modules; presents dashboards, alerts and visualisations (Abstract, p. 304).
- **Financial persona** — User label produced by K-Means clustering; examples given are "budget-conscious", "impulsive spender" and "balanced planner" (Results & Discussion, p. 308).
- **Isolation Forest** — Anomaly-detection method used to alert users about suspicious transactions or overspending (Introduction, p. 306).
- **MAE** — Mean Absolute Error, the metric by which the LSTM forecasting model is reported (Results & Discussion, p. 308).
- **Mint / YNAB** — Named existing personal finance applications that the paper says focus on expense tracking, budgeting and financial reporting but lack advanced predictive capabilities (Related Work, p. 307).
