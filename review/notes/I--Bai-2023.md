## Summary

Bai (2023) examines how three cognitive factors — financial literacy, mental budgeting, and self-control — affect subjective financial wellbeing among Chinese university students, and tests whether investment decision-making behaviour mediates those relationships. Using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) on 449 survey responses, the study finds positive and significant direct effects of financial literacy, mental budgeting and self-control on financial wellbeing (H1–H3), a positive direct effect of investment decision making on financial wellbeing (H4), and partial mediation through investment decision making for all three independent-variable paths (H5–H7). The paper frames these relationships with cognitive dissonance theory and the behavioural life-cycle hypothesis, and draws practical implications for financial education, financial counselling, and the development of digital tools that support mental budgeting and self-control. The measurement model reports composite reliabilities from 0.714 to 0.868, Cronbach alphas from 0.710 to 0.841, and AVE values from 0.692 to 0.811, all within conventional thresholds. The study is cross-sectional, self-reported, and limited to university students, and the authors acknowledge those limitations.

## Problem and Motivation

The paper opens by linking financial wellbeing to public health. It cites the Global Health Data Exchange to report that mental health issues affect 84 million Europeans, or 17.3% of the population (Sect. 1, p. 2), and cites Fiksenbaum et al. (2017) that 4.4 percent of people worldwide have anxiety disorders, with 3.6 percent exhibiting symptoms (Sect. 1, p. 2). Financial hardship is rising, and UK and US surveys — including PwC's Employee Financial Wellness Survey — reached similar conclusions, with employees reporting financial stress higher than any other type of stress (Sect. 1, p. 2). HSBC data are cited to show that 64% of Chinese were satisfied with their finances in 2019, up from 57% in 2016, while 36% worried about saving for retirement and 29% about paying unexpected costs (Sect. 1, p. 2).

The authors argue that a lack of financial wellbeing causes financial distress, which lowers physical and mental health and workplace productivity (Sect. 1, p. 2, citing Bagwell and Kim 2003; Garman et al. 1999; Joo and Garman 1998; Strömbäck et al. 2020). The paper's stated gap is that while multiple determinants of financial wellbeing exist, the interaction of financial literacy, mental budgeting, self-control and investment decision making has not been examined together. The study makes three claimed contributions: examining the complex interaction of these constructs; connecting psychological and economic aspects by studying mental budgeting and self-control in financial decision making; and positioning investment decision making as a mediator between the three cognitive factors and financial health (Sect. 1, p. 3).

The theoretical framing is cognitive dissonance theory, which "suggests that individuals strive for consistency in their beliefs and behaviors" (Sect. 2, p. 3). The paper argues that financial literacy, mental budgeting and self-control can influence the alignment of an individual's financial decisions with their overall financial goals and values, reducing cognitive dissonance and enhancing financial wellbeing (Sect. 2, p. 3).

## Method

**Design.** Cross-sectional survey study analysed with Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM); the authors state no formal design label beyond this (Sect. 3.4, p. 9; Sect. 4, p. 9).
**Sample.** n = 449 usable observations; unit of analysis is the individual Chinese university student (Sect. 3.1, p. 7).
**Context.** geography: China (data collected at Henan Institute of Economics and Trade, Zhengzhou, Henan); population: Chinese university students — 245 male (55%) and 204 female (45%), of whom 270 (60%) were business majors; setting: survey administered through both physical and electronic channels (Sect. 3.1, p. 7; Table 1, p. 8).

The sample was chosen "using the criteria based on number of items" and convenience sampling was used to collect the data (Sect. 3.1, p. 7). Respondents included 245 male (55%) and 204 female (45%) students; 270 (60%) belonged to a business major (Sect. 3.1, p. 7). Table 1 reports the distribution: Male 245 (54.6), Female 204 (45.4); Undergraduate 239 (53.2), Masters 112 (24.9), Doctor 98 (21.8); Business 270 (60.1), Non-Business 179 (39.9).

Measures were assembled from prior instruments (Sect. 3.2, p. 7):
- Financial wellbeing was captured with two separate measures — financial anxiety (four items from [88]) and financial security (three factors from [14]), for seven FWB items in total.
- Financial literacy was measured with seven items adopted from [89].
- Mental budgeting was measured with four items adopted from a study of mental budgeting and management of household finance [59].
- Self-control was quantified through a smaller version of the Brief Self-Control Scale [78] (five items) plus four items from the Short-Term Future Orientation Scale [59], for nine SC items in total.
- Investment decision-making behaviour was measured with a scale adopted from [90] containing four components: overall financial management or decision-making behaviour, savings and investment, cash management, and credit management.

Respondents rated agreement on a scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) (Sect. 3.2, p. 7). The list of items is given in S1 File.

Ethical clearance was obtained from the research council of Henan Institute of Economics and Trade; participants were informed of the study purpose, benefits and risks, and gave verbal informed consent (Sect. 3.3, p. 8).

Analysis used PLS-SEM because "it is more flexible with the sample sizes and is also less vulnerable to the violations of the multivariate data assumptions, such as normality of data" (Sect. 3.4, p. 9).

Seven hypotheses were tested:
- H1: financial literacy has a significant direct impact on financial wellbeing.
- H2: mental budgeting has a significant direct impact on financial wellbeing.
- H3: self-control has a significant direct impact on financial wellbeing.
- H4: investment decision making has a significant direct impact on financial well-being.
- H5: investment decision making has a significant mediating effect between mental budgeting and financial well-being.
- H6: investment decision making has a significant mediating effect between financial literacy and financial well-being.
- H7: investment decision making has a significant mediating effect between self-control and financial well-being.

The conceptual model is shown in Fig 1 (p. 8).

## Key Findings

- The measurement model met conventional thresholds: all indicator loadings were above 0.50 (range 0.637–1.000), composite reliability ranged from 0.714 to 0.845, Cronbach's alpha ranged from 0.743 to 0.821, and AVE values were all above 0.50 (Sect. 4.1, p. 9; Table 2, p. 10).
- Financial literacy, mental budgeting and self-control all positively affect financial wellbeing (Sect. 4.2, p. 9; Table 3, p. 10).
- The path from investment decision making to financial wellbeing (DMB → FWB) is reported as Beta 0.126 with p = 0.059 and is nonetheless marked "Supported" (Table 3, p. 10).
- Investment decision making partially mediates the relationship between each of the three independent variables and financial wellbeing; indirect effects are 0.017 (FL), 0.016 (MB) and 0.019 (SC), all with p-values below 0.05 (Table 4, p. 11).
- The authors conclude that "individuals with elevated levels of financial literacy, proficient mental budgeting skills, and self-regulatory abilities are inclined towards demonstrating favorable investment decision-making conduct" (Abstract, p. 1).
- The direct effect for FL → FWB is reported as 0.299 in Table 3 but as 0.136 in Table 4; MB → FWB is 0.102 in Table 3 but 0.128 in Table 4; SC → FWB is 0.182 in Table 3 but 0.152 in Table 4 (Table 3, p. 10; Table 4, p. 11).
- The study reports no effect sizes, no confidence intervals for path coefficients, and no model fit indices for the structural model.

## Definitions

- **Financial literacy** — "the knowledge of basic financial concepts, the ability to apply financial knowledge and skills in managing financial resources effectively, and the ability to make informed financial decisions to achieve financial welfare over a lifetime" (Sect. 2.1, p. 4).
- **Mental budgeting** — "the cognitive process that people use to organize, evaluate, and keep track of financial activities" (Sect. 2.2, p. 4); "a financial management technique that involves categorizing and monitoring expenses and income on a mental level" (Sect. 2.2, p. 4).
- **Self-control** — "the ability to control one's conduct and reduce impulsivity" (Sect. 2.3, p. 5); "the ability to manage ideas, emotions, and actions to attain long-term goals and avoid temptation" (Sect. 1, p. 2).
- **Investment decision-making behaviour** — "the process of making decisions related to finances and investments by individuals, which are influenced by various factors such as financial knowledge, financial attitude, financial behavior, self-control, psychological biases, and external environment" (Sect. 2.4, p. 6).
- **Financial wellbeing** — treated as "a subjective evaluation of one's present and future financial situation" (Sect. 2, p. 3), focused on "the consumer's self-assessment of his or her disposition, attitude, belief, and behaviors linked to money management" (Sect. 2, p. 3).
- **PLS-SEM** — Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling, chosen because it is "more flexible with the sample sizes" and "less vulnerable to the violations of the multivariate data assumptions, such as normality of data" (Sect. 3.4, p. 9).
- **Cognitive Dissonance Theory** — the study's stated theoretical frame; "suggests that individuals strive for consistency in their beliefs and behaviors" (Sect. 2, p. 3).
- **Behavioural Life-Cycle (BLC)** — Shefrin and Thaler's theory, cited to explain that "the majority of individuals are preoccupied with the challenges and rewards of the now rather than the advantages of the long term" (Sect. 2.3, p. 5).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The authors acknowledge that the sample is a specific demographic and geographic group (students), which "could limit the generalizability of the results to a broader population" (Sect. 5.3, p. 12).
- The authors acknowledge that the cross-sectional design "prevents establishing causal relationships between the variables investigated" (Sect. 5.3, p. 12).
- The authors acknowledge that self-reported measures "may introduce response biases and social desirability effects" and that participants "might have provided answers that they believed were expected or socially acceptable" (Sect. 5.3, p. 12).
- [unacknowledged] Table 3 and Table 4 report conflicting direct effects for the same paths: FL → FWB is 0.299 in Table 3 but 0.136 in Table 4; MB → FWB is 0.102 in Table 3 but 0.128 in Table 4; SC → FWB is 0.182 in Table 3 but 0.152 in Table 4. The paper does not reconcile these values.
- [unacknowledged] The DMB → FWB path has p = 0.059 (above the conventional 0.05 threshold) yet is marked "Supported" in Table 3 and counted as supporting H4. No effect size or confidence interval is reported for any path.
- [unacknowledged] The text reports 245 male (55%) and 204 female (45%) while Table 1 reports 54.6 and 45.4, and the text reports "270 (60%)" business majors while Table 1 reports 60.1.
- [unacknowledged] No model fit indices for the structural model, no discriminant validity statistics (e.g. HTMT, Fornell–Larcker), and no full item wording are reported in the main text; the item list is deferred to S1 File.
- [unacknowledged] The sample is described as convenience-sampled from a single institution (Henan Institute of Economics and Trade), so the sampling frame and response rate are not reported and non-response bias cannot be assessed.

## Remember This

- Bai (2023) tests a PLS-SEM model in which financial literacy, mental budgeting and self-control predict financial wellbeing, with investment decision making as a partial mediator.
- The sample is 449 Chinese university students, convenience-sampled from a single institution.
- All seven hypotheses are reported as supported, but the DMB → FWB path has p = 0.059 and is still marked "Supported."
- Table 3 and Table 4 report different direct-effect values for the same paths (FL → FWB 0.299 vs 0.136; MB → FWB 0.102 vs 0.128; SC → FWB 0.182 vs 0.152).
- Composite reliabilities range 0.714–0.868, Cronbach alphas 0.710–0.841, and AVE 0.692–0.811.
- The study is cross-sectional and self-reported; the authors acknowledge these limits.
