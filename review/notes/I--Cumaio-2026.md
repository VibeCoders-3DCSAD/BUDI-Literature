# Linking Financial Literacy and Behavioural Finance to Saving and Debt Behaviours: A Literature Review of Global and Developing Economy Contexts

`I--Cumaio-2026` — Cumaio (2026), *Journal of Risk and Financial Management, 19(6), 425* \[10.3390/jrfm19060425]

## Summary

A semi-systematic review of 109 studies reports mixed evidence that financial literacy and behavioural factors shape saving and debt behaviour, maps the field to saving rather than debt as its dominant theme, and finds the behavioural work concentrated in developed economies while developing-economy studies remain largely literacy-training interventions.

## Problem and Motivation

Financial literacy and behavioural finance developed largely independently and were treated as mutually exclusive rather than complementary, and the reviews linking them have concentrated on investment decisions rather than on savings and debt (Sec. 1, pp. 1-2). Household financial vulnerability is more pronounced in developing economies, so the decisions that obtain resources for present needs and future goals matter most where they are most constrained (Sec. 1, p. 2).

## Method

**Design.** Semi-systematic literature review (S-SLR) after Snyder (2019), using the PRISMA 2020 model for the document search and predominantly narrative, qualitative analysis of coded articles; the authors state this is a review, not an empirical study (Sec. 2 Materials and Methods, pp. 3-4).
**Sample.** n = 109 articles published 2011-2024, selected from 502 records retrieved on 6 January 2025; one article per study, of which 40 are classified as developing-economy studies (Abstract, p. 1; Sec. 2.2-2.3, pp. 4-6; Sec. 3, p. 6).
**Context.** geography: reviewed studies span Europe (27 articles), America (35), Asia (26), Africa (12) and Oceania (5); 40 articles are set in developing economies under the World Bank (2024) classification of 2023 per capita income, and the Philippines contributes one article (Table 1, p. 8; Sec. 3.3, pp. 14-15); population: individuals and households making saving and debt decisions — the paper reports no pooled participant count across the 109 studies; setting: bibliographic search of Scopus and Web of Science plus narrative coding by the three authors, in Portuguese academic institutions (Sec. 2.2, p. 4).

- Establish a guiding conceptual framework in which financial behaviour is both input and output of financial literacy and behavioural finance (Sec. 2.1, pp. 3-4; Figure 1).
- Review existing reviews first, and record that none addresses the topic set by the conceptual framework (Sec. 2.2, p. 4).
- Search Scopus and Web of Science on 6 January 2025 with keywords derived from the framework, most marked with an asterisk to capture terminology variants (Sec. 2.2, pp. 4-5; Figure 2).
- Restrict the search to articles and review papers, to English, Spanish and Portuguese, and to economics and business (Sec. 2.2, p. 5).
- Screen by abstract and, where necessary, full text, retaining studies that address financial literacy or education together with financial behaviour, emotions or biases in relation to saving or debt (Sec. 2.2, p. 5; Figure 3).
- Add two previously identified articles (Choi, 2015; Shen, 2014) that analyse literacy and biases separately and do not use the systematic review method (Sec. 2.2, p. 5).
- Code each article under three categories: thematic approach (A), methodological approach and method (B), and geographical incidence (C) (Sec. 2.3, pp. 5-6).
- Separate the global and developing-economy analyses using the World Bank (2024) income classification, and exclude four literature reviews that lacked a defined geographical incidence (Sec. 2.3, p. 6).
- Present findings in Table 1 for the first three research questions, interpret them descriptively through Figures 4-10, and draw a future research agenda from the sample's own suggestions (Sect. 3, pp. 6-16).

## Key Findings

- num: The corpus is 109 articles published between 2011 and 2024, selected from 502 records retrieved from Scopus and Web of Science on 6 January 2025 (Abstract, p. 1; Sec. 3, p. 6).
- num: 40 of the 109 articles are set in developing economies under the World Bank (2024) classification; four further literature reviews were excluded for lacking a defined geographical incidence (Sec. 2.3, p. 6).
- num: Saving behaviour leads the themes at 38.53% (42 articles), against 29.36% (32) for debt, 13.76% (15) for both, 14.68% (16) for daily financial management, and 1.83% (2) each for insurance decisions and financial well-being (Table 1 codes A1-A6, p. 8).
- num: In developing economies the same ordering holds at 42.50% (17), 22.50% (9), 20.00% (8), 10.00% (4), 0.00% and 5.00% respectively (Table 1 codes A1-A6, p. 8).
- num: 89.91% of all articles and 95% of developing-economy articles use questionnaire-based quantitative methods; 3.67% are interview-based and 0.92% hybrid (Sec. 3.2, p. 12; Table 1 codes B1-B4, p. 8).
- num: Longitudinal and intervention designs account for 14.68% of all articles and 17.50% of developing-economy articles (Table 1 code B2, p. 8; Sec. 3.2, p. 12).
- num: The USA hosts 30 articles, 27.52% of the sample, the largest single-country share; America totals 35, Europe 27, Asia 26, Africa 12 and Oceania 5, with 17 of the 26 Asian articles from Indonesia, India, Malaysia and Thailand, all five Oceanian articles Australian, and one article from the Philippines (Sect. 3.3, pp. 14-15; Table 1 codes C, C3 and C5, p. 8).
- num: 13 articles met the inclusion criteria on their abstracts but could not be accessed because of restrictions (Contribution and Limitations, p. 17).
- The Abstract and Conclusion report mixed rather than consistent evidence for the influence of financial literacy and behavioural factors on saving and debt behaviour (Abstract, p. 1; Sect. 4, pp. 16-17).
- Most studies did not rely explicitly on consumer behaviour theories; the theory of planned behaviour and the theory of bounded rationality together account for 12 applications, and Figure 5 records theory frequency rather than study counts (Sect. 3, p. 8).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The South Africa count is internally inconsistent: Table 1 (p. 8) lists South Africa—5 within the 12 African articles, while the geographical-incidence discussion (p. 15) states that half of the 12 African studies, "South Africa (six studies)", were conducted there. Both figures are kept above with their locators; the paper does not reconcile them.
- The authors acknowledge that the S-SLR was restricted to two databases, Scopus and Web of Science, without accounting for journal quality metrics when selecting articles (Contribution and Limitations, pp. 17-18).
- The authors acknowledge that the final selection rested on their own content assessment, introducing a degree of subjectivity (Contribution and Limitations, p. 17).
- The authors acknowledge that 13 articles met the inclusion criteria but could not be accessed because of restrictions, which may have excluded relevant studies (Contribution and Limitations, pp. 17-18).
- [unacknowledged] The Abstract reports mixed evidence for the influence of financial literacy and behavioural factors, yet the review's coding counts studies by theme rather than by the direction or strength of their findings, so no pooled estimate of literacy's effect is available.
- [unacknowledged] The corpus rests on English, Spanish and Portuguese sources only, and the language criterion is justified by author proficiency rather than by coverage (p. 5).
- [unacknowledged] The Philippines contributes one article to the corpus, and Table 1's developing-economy analysis is reported only in aggregate, so no single-country evidence can be read from the paper.

## Definitions

- **S-SLR (semi-systematic literature review)** — the review design adopted from Snyder (2019), suited to topics with multiple conceptualisations spanning disciplines, mapping dominant themes and gaps through predominantly narrative and qualitative analysis rather than quantitative pooling (Sec. 2, p. 3).
- **Financial literacy (FL)** — one of the three core components of financial behaviour as an input, together with knowledge and attitudes, in the paper's guiding framework (Sec. 2.1, pp. 3-4).
- **Behavioural finance (BF)** — the second input component of the framework, comprising biases and emotions (Sec. 2.1, pp. 3-4).
- **Financial behaviour** — the review's dependent construct, positioned as both input and output: as input it covers budgeting, purchase management, saving for emergencies and retirement, and debt management; as output it is how literacy and behavioural mechanisms shape intentions, attitudes and decisions about saving and debt (Sec. 2.1, p. 4).
- **Behaviours** — the paper's scope term, encompassing intentions, attitudes and decision-making rather than actions alone (Sec. 1, p. 2).
- **Financing decisions** — decisions about savings and debt that sustain the ordinary course of life, that is, how individuals obtain resources to meet present needs and future financial goals (Sec. 1, p. 2).
- **Developing economies** — countries classified on 2023 per capita income as low-, lower-middle- or upper-middle-income by the World Bank (2024); multi-country studies count as developed if at least one country is high-income, and countries that changed band during 2011-2024 are assigned to the band they held longest (Sec. 2.3, p. 6).
