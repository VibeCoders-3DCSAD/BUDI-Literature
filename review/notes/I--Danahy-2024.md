# I--Danahy-2024

## Summary

This short "Trends and Applications" article provides updated estimates of the association between money-related stressors and financial stress among US college students, and tests whether social and personal resources moderate those associations. Using the February 2020 administration of the Study on Collegiate Financial Wellness (N = 25,310; borrower-only robustness subsample n = 12,519), the authors estimate ordered probit models of a four-item financial stress index on student loan debt amount, the likelihood of coming up with $400 in a financial emergency, financial socialization, financial self-efficacy, and positive and negative financial management behaviours, with sociodemographic controls. Students report higher financial stress when they hold more student loan debt and when they are less likely to have emergency savings; these associations hold in the full sample and in the borrower-only subsample and survive the addition of social and personal resources. Financial socialization and financial self-efficacy are associated with lower financial stress, while both positive and negative financial management behaviours are associated with higher financial stress — a result the authors report they did not expect. Among student-borrowers the moderating role of social and personal resources is weakened. The authors describe the evidence as associational and call for longitudinal research.

## Problem and Motivation

Student loan debt occupies roughly the same share of non-mortgage household debt as auto, credit card and other debt types combined until mid-life, and students who borrow delay marriage, childbearing and homeownership, are less likely to take low-paid public-interest jobs, and more often report personal financial distress and overextended family finances into retirement (Sect. 1, p. 693). Students who experience financial stress more often report negative mental health outcomes, food and housing insecurity, forgone medical care and negative physical health outcomes (Sect. 1, p. 693). The paper's stated contribution is new data from a robust collection effort and the application of the stress process model to the college student population, in order to provide updated estimates of the association between student loan debt, liquidity constraints and financial stress, and to test whether social and personal resources moderate that association (Sect. 1, p. 693). Two research questions are posed: whether exposure to money-related stressors is associated with college students' financial stress, and whether financial stress differs for students with fewer and greater levels of social and personal resources (Sect. 1, p. 693). Figure 1 illustrates the proposed relationships (Fig. 1, p. 694).

## Method

**Design.** Cross-sectional survey study with ordered probit regression models; the authors give no formal design label beyond describing the data as a "cross-sectional study design" and the article as a short "Trends and Applications" piece (Abstract, p. 692; Sect. 2.1, p. 694).
**Sample.** N = 25,310 college students in the analytical sample, unit of analysis the individual student respondent, drawn from 29,883 respondents at 60 institutions; borrower-only robustness subsample n = 12,519 (48% of sample); Table 1 splits the full sample into 12,245 low-stress and 13,065 high-stress respondents.
**Context.** geography: United States — 60 two- and four-year US higher education institutions, which the authors state do not represent the national distribution of institution types; population: undergraduate college students at the participating institutions; setting: February 2020 administration of the Study on Collegiate Financial Wellness, a self-report cross-sectional survey; no intervention, no software system and no experimental manipulation.

Procedure as printed:

- Dependent variable is a composite index of opinions about four statements about personal finances, example "I feel stressed about my personal finances in general"; four response options from 1 = strongly disagree to 4 = strongly agree; summed measure ranges 4 to 16; higher values represent greater financial stress; alpha = 0.832 (Sect. 2.1, p. 694).
- Sample split descriptively: scores 4 to 10 = low financial stress; scores 11 to 16 = high financial stress (Sect. 2.1, p. 694).
- Focal predictor 1: student loan debt amount, reported as any debt and in seven categories of $10,000 each ($0, $1–9999 through $60,000 or higher), with "Don't know" treated as missing (Sect. 2.1, p. 694; Appendix 1, p. 706).
- Focal predictor 2: lack of emergency savings, from "How likely is it that you could come up with $400 in cash in the event of a financial emergency during the school year?", four categories from 1 = very likely to 4 = very unlikely (Sect. 2.1, p. 694; Appendix 1, p. 706).
- Moderator 1: financial socialization, four items about parents and guardians providing financial advice, having conversations about money, telling the respondent what they needed to know about money management, and modelling sound financial management; alpha = 0.832 (Sect. 2.1, pp. 694–695; Appendix 1, p. 706).
- Moderator 2: financial self-efficacy, four items including "I am confident that I can manage my finances"; alpha = 0.876 (Sect. 2.1, p. 695; Appendix 1, p. 706).
- Behaviour indices: positive financial behaviors, three items including "I tracked my spending", alpha = 0.662; negative financial behaviors, three items including "I overdrew my bank account", alpha = 0.667; index values range 4 to 16 for four-item scales and 3 to 12 for three-item scales (Sect. 2.1, p. 695; Appendix 1, pp. 706–707).
- Controls: gender, race/ethnicity, employment status, year in college, and whether the respondent met with a financial aid counselor in high school or college (Sect. 2.1, p. 695).
- Estimation: "We use STATA 17.0 to estimate all models. Because our dependent variable is an ordered categorical variable, our main model specifies an ordered probit" (Sect. 3, p. 695).
- Three nested models per sample: Column 1 baseline with money-related stressors and controls; Column 2 adds financial socialization, self-efficacy and financial behaviours; Column 3 adds interactions of those resources and behaviours with student loan debt amount and with lack of emergency savings (Table 3, pp. 699–700; Table 4, pp. 701–702).
- Robustness test repeats the analysis for the subsample of student loan borrowers only, excluding students with a zero student loan debt amount (Sect. 3.1, p. 700).
- Appendix Table A1 re-estimates the full-sample models using a single combined financial behaviour variable, with negative items reverse coded; range min = 6, max = 24; mean = 21.10; SD = 2.63; alpha = 0.594 (Table A1 note, p. 709).
- Missing data handled by listwise dropping of 4,573 of 29,883 respondents (15.3%) (Sect. 2.1, p. 695).

- STATA 17.0 (version stated; used for all models) — Sect. 3, p. 695.

## Key Findings

- Greater financial stress is correlated with higher student loan debt (r = 0.31***) and with lack of cash on hand to pay unforeseen expenses (r = 0.58***) (Sect. 3, p. 695; Table 2, p. 697).
- Controlling for sociodemographic characteristics, students experience more financial stress when they hold more debt and are less likely to have emergency savings, and this applies across student loan amounts (Sect. 3, p. 698; Table 3, p. 699).
- Students who borrow up to $10,000 report a 0.4 higher likelihood of being under financial stress, and students who borrow $60,000 and more a 0.8 higher likelihood, compared with students who do not borrow; the coefficient on the highest loan amount is twice as large as the smallest loan amount category (Sect. 3, p. 698; Table 3, p. 699).
- Students who say it is very unlikely they could come up with $400 report a 1.7 times greater likelihood of financial stress relative to those who say it is very likely (Sect. 3, p. 698; Table 3, p. 699). The borrower-only text states "1.7 higher log odds" for the same quantity (Sect. 3.1, p. 702).
- A unit increase in financial socialization is associated with a 0.05 lower likelihood of financial stress and a unit increase in self-efficacy with a 0.1 lower likelihood (Sect. 3, p. 698; Table 3, p. 699).
- A unit increase in positive or negative financial behaviours is associated with 0.12 and 0.10 higher likelihood of financial stress (Sect. 3, p. 698; Table 3, p. 699).
- Adding resources and behaviours reduces the focal coefficients to 80 to 90% of the baseline specification while remaining significant at p < 0.001 (Sect. 3, p. 698).
- In the borrower-only subsample, student-borrowers report higher financial stress with increasingly higher debt amounts; log odds increase from 0.137 among students who borrow $10,000–19,999 to 0.424 among those who borrow $60,000 or more compared with students who borrow up to $10,000 (Sect. 3.1, p. 702; Table 4, p. 701).
- Taken together, the borrower-only findings "indicate a weakened role of social and personal resources for the association of student loan debt and emergency savings with financial stress among student-borrowers" (Sect. 3.1, p. 703).
- The authors state they were somewhat surprised to observe higher financial stress among students with both positive and negative financial management behaviours, and offer no obvious explanation (Sect. 4, p. 703).
- Male students are less likely to report high financial stress; Hispanic/Latinx and multiracial/multiethnic students are more likely to report higher financial stress than White students; employed students are more likely to report higher financial stress; third- and fourth-year students report lower predicted probability of higher financial stress than first-year students; students who never met a financial aid counselor are more likely to report higher financial stress (Sect. 3, p. 698; Table 3, p. 700).

## Definitions

- **Financial stress** — as defined by the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and adopted by the authors, "a negative emotional response people experience when their financial situation exposes them to uncertainty and (severely) constrains their current and future choices" (Sect. 1, p. 693).
- **Financial stress index** — composite index of four statements about personal finances, four response options from strongly disagree to strongly agree, summed 4–16, higher values greater stress, alpha = 0.832 (Sect. 2.1, p. 694).
- **Stress process model** — framework of Pearlin (1989) as extended by Drentea and Reynolds (2015), used by the authors to inform the empirical models and to allow for social and personal resources moderating the association between financial stress and money-related stressors (Sect. 1, p. 693).
- **Student loan debt amount** — self-reported borrowing in seven categories of $10,000 each, from $0 to $60,000 or higher (Sect. 2.1, p. 694; Appendix 1, p. 706).
- **Lack of emergency savings** — self-reported likelihood of coming up with $400 in cash in a financial emergency during the school year, 1 = very likely to 4 = very unlikely (Sect. 2.1, p. 694).
- **Financial socialization** — four-item index of parental and guardian financial advice, money conversations, money-management information and modelled sound financial management; alpha = 0.832 (Sect. 2.1, pp. 694–695).
- **Financial self-efficacy** — four-item index of confidence in managing finances, feeling in control, confidence in planning for the financial future, and ability to solve a financial challenge; alpha = 0.876 (Sect. 2.1, p. 695).
- **Positive financial behaviors** — three-item index (tracked spending, planned ahead for major purchases, monitored account balances); alpha = 0.662 (Sect. 2.1, p. 695).
- **Negative financial behaviors** — three-item index (overdrew bank account, purchased things could not afford, made late payments); alpha = 0.667 (Sect. 2.1, p. 695).
- **Low / high financial stress groups** — descriptive split of the index at 4–10 and 11–16 respectively (Sect. 2.1, p. 694).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The authors acknowledge that the 60 participating institutions do not represent the national distribution of institution types, and that respondents from four-year public universities are overrepresented while public and private two-year institutions and four-year private institutions are underrepresented (Sect. 2.1, p. 694).
- The authors report a low response rate: 12.7% overall, 7% at 2-year institutions (n = 9), 13% at 4-year public institutions (n = 38) and 17% at 4-year private institutions (n = 13) (Sect. 2.1, p. 694).
- The authors report dropping 4,573 of 29,883 respondents (15.3%) for missing key data, with financial stress (6.5%), employment (6.3%), student loan debt (3.8%) and financial socialization (3.5%) the predictors with most missing values (Sect. 2.1, p. 695).
- The authors describe the literature they build on as not identifying a causal link ("While none of these studies identify a causal link, the underpinnings of these associations merit further study") and close by calling for future research exploring "longitudinal changes in students' self-efficacy, social support, and the experience of stress" (Sect. 1, p. 693; Sect. 4, p. 703).
- The authors state they were somewhat surprised by the positive-behaviour result and that they "have no obvious explanation for the observation of higher financial stress among students with more positive financial behaviors"; they say the finding merits more attention (Sect. 4, p. 703).
- The authors raise self-selection as a possibility: students with parents who are good role models may not need to take on additional student loans in the first place and may receive more financial assistance from their parents (Sect. 4, p. 703).
- The survey questionnaire is not publicly available and can only be requested from the Center for the Study of Student Life at The Ohio State University (Appendix 1, p. 705).
- [unacknowledged] The Section 3 narrative and Table 3 disagree on the significance of the positive-behaviour interactions. The text states that "a higher student loan debt amount is associated with lower financial stress for both those who report more positive or more negative financial behaviors, p < 0.001" (p. 700), but Table 3 prints the positive-behaviours × debt interaction as −0.005* (0.002), i.e. p < 0.05, and the negative-behaviours × debt interaction as −0.007*** (0.002). The text also states that "a greater lack of emergency savings is associated with lower financial stress for students who report more both more positive and negative financial behaviors, p < 0.001" (p. 700), but Table 3 prints the positive-behaviours × lack-of-emergency-savings interaction as 0.005 (0.004), unsigned and non-significant. Both the printed coefficients and the narrative magnitudes are recorded in the Statistical Evidence table and are not reconciled here.
- [unacknowledged] The same quantity is described two ways in the paper: for students very unlikely to come up with $400 the full-sample text reports "a 1.7 times greater likelihood of financial stress (p < 0.001)" (Sect. 3, p. 698), while the borrower-only text reports "1.7 higher log odds of being under financial stress, p < 0.001" (Sect. 3.1, p. 702). Both statements carry the same number; the corresponding Table 3 coefficient is 1.740 and the Table 4 coefficient is 1.70.
- [unacknowledged] The paper states "Of students with debt, most report debt of less than $10,000 (33% of students with some debt)" (Sect. 2.2, p. 695); the printed share of 33% is not consistent with the word "most".
- [unacknowledged] The paper states that "more than half (58%) have had not met with a financial aid counselor" (Sect. 2.2, p. 695), while Table 1 prints the indicator "Met With Financial Aid Counselor in High School or College (No = 1)" at 0.415 (0.003) for the full sample; under the Appendix coding (0 = Yes, 1 = Never) these two figures describe the same quantity in opposite directions.
- [unacknowledged] No confidence interval is reported for any coefficient, correlation or group mean; precision is conveyed only by standard errors in parentheses, and the narrative magnitudes (0.4, 0.8, 1.7, 0.05, 0.1, 0.12, 0.10) are printed without standard errors or intervals.
- [unacknowledged] The average student loan debt of $12,926 is not an observed mean: it is derived "using midpoint values of the debt ranges and an assumption of a uniform distribution in each range" (Sect. 2.2, p. 695), and no uncertainty is reported for it.
- [unacknowledged] The ordered probit models are reported with coefficients, standard errors, LR chi2 and pseudo R2 only (0.104 to 0.134 in the full sample; 0.076 to 0.100 in the borrower-only subsample); no marginal effects, no proportional-odds or parallel-lines test, and no other fit or specification diagnostic is reported.
- [unacknowledged] No adjustment for multiple comparisons and no power analysis are reported, despite three nested models per sample, many interaction terms and three parallel model sets (Tables 3, 4 and A1).
- [unacknowledged] The cross-sectional, self-report design admits no causal identification strategy; the paper reports associations only and never claims otherwise, but also never bounds the direction of the debt–stress association.
- [unacknowledged] Tables 3 and 4 use different reference categories: the full-sample models omit $0 and the borrower-only models omit $1–$9999, so the two tables do not estimate the same debt contrasts and the "student-borrowers" comparison in Table 4 is not against non-borrowers.
- [unacknowledged] Debt is measured only by borrowed amount category; no loan type, interest rate, repayment status, delinquency or servicer information is collected or reported, so "debt exposure" is not characterised beyond the amount band.
- [unacknowledged] The financial stress index sums four items, one of which is worded in the opposite direction from the others ("I have enough money to participate in most of the same activities as my peers", Appendix 1, p. 705); the paper does not state that this item is reverse coded before summing.

## Remember This

- The paper is a cross-sectional, self-report survey study of US college students (N = 25,310 from the February 2020 Study on Collegiate Financial Wellness) using ordered probit regressions; it establishes association, not causation.
- The two focal stressors are student loan debt amount and the self-reported likelihood of raising $400 in an emergency; both are associated with higher financial stress in the full sample and in the borrower-only subsample (n = 12,519).
- Table 2 correlations: financial stress with debt r = 0.31***, with lack of emergency savings r = 0.58***, with financial socialization r = −0.30***, with financial self-efficacy r = −0.42***.
- Both positive and negative financial management behaviours are associated with higher financial stress in the full sample — the authors state they have no obvious explanation for the positive-behaviour result.
- The narrative and the tables disagree on two interactions and on the direction of the financial aid counselor share; the 58% text figure and Table 1's 0.415 (No = 1) are recorded side by side and not reconciled.
- Social and personal resources weaken as moderators among student-borrowers; no confidence interval is reported anywhere in the paper.
