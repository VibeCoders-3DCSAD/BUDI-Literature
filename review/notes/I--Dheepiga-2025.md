# I--Dheepiga-2025 — How Financial Literacy Influences Budgeting, Investment, and Savings Behaviors

## Summary

A quantitative cross-sectional survey of 100 respondents, analysed with Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) in Smart PLS, examining how financial literacy relates to budgeting behavior, investment behavior, savings behavior and financial confidence. Financial literacy has a strong, statistically significant positive effect on both budgeting behavior (β = 0.783) and investment behavior (β = 0.786). Budgeting behavior's relationship with savings behavior is negative and statistically insignificant (β = -0.231, p = 0.081), while savings behavior's relationship with financial confidence is negative but significant (β = -0.266, p = 0.007). Investment behavior and budgeting behavior emerge as the highest-importance constructs in the IPMA. The authors conclude that financial education programs should address both practical and emotional aspects of financial management, particularly fostering financial confidence.

## Problem and Motivation

Financial literacy is treated as more critical than ever in a complex financial landscape, because it empowers people to make planned decisions about budgeting, saving and investing—components of personal financial health and economic stability (Introduction, p. 102). The paper asks whether the current generation, particularly college students as future investors and economic leaders, possesses adequate financial knowledge (Kumari, D. A. T. 2020). It seeks to examine the connection among financial literacy, budgeting behavior and investment confidence, and to check the level to which financial literacy drives budgeting practices and how these practices influence investment confidence (p. 102). The study aims to enhance understanding of the role financial education plays in shaping sound financial behaviors and enhancing overall financial health.

## Method

**Design.** Quantitative cross-sectional study; the authors state no formal design label beyond "a cross-sectional study method was utilized to gather information from participants" (p. 105). Data were analysed using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) with Smart-PLS.
**Sample.** n = 100 respondents (individuals aged 18 and older), unit of analysis is the individual respondent.
**Context.** geography: Not reported (respondents "drawn from diverse demographic backgrounds", recruited through social media platforms, community forums and local organizations); population: individuals aged 18 and older; setting: online—the questionnaire "was distributed electronically" and respondents rated statements on a 1–5 Likert scale (p. 105).

Additional method detail:
- A formal questionnaire measured Financial Literacy, Budgeting Behavior, Investment Behavior, Savings Behavior and Financial Confidence using validated scales and items (p. 105).
- Non-probability sampling was used to collect data, enabling quick access to a variety of respondents (p. 105).
- Four hypotheses were formulated: H1 direct connection between budgeting behavior and savings behavior; H2 financial literacy positively impacts budgeting behavior; H3 financial literacy positively affects investment behavior; H4 savings behavior positively influences financial confidence (p. 105).
- Data were assessed with SEM in Smart-PLS to check connections among latent variables and evaluate overall model fit; techniques included reliability analysis, validity test, path analysis and IPMA (p. 105).
- The measurement model was assessed with confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to ensure constructs are valid and reliable (p. 105).
- The structural model used 5,000 bootstrap samples (p. 109).
- Hypotheses were tested through path coefficients from SEM, with significance assessed using p-values and t-statistics (p. 105).

## Key Findings

- num: Financial literacy has a strong direct positive effect on budgeting behavior (β = 0.783, t = 19.965, p = 0.000) — H2 accepted (Table 4, p. 109).
- num: Financial literacy has a strong direct positive effect on investment behavior (β = 0.786, t = 18.781, p = 0.000) — H3 accepted (Table 4, p. 109).
- num: Budgeting behavior's relationship with savings behavior is negative and statistically insignificant (β = -0.231, t = 1.743, p = 0.081) — H1 rejected (Table 4, p. 109).
- num: Savings behavior's relationship with financial confidence is negative but statistically significant (β = -0.266, t = 2.695, p = 0.007) — H4 accepted (Table 4, p. 109).
- num: In IPMA, Investment Behavior is the most important construct (importance 0.786, LV performance 39.585), followed closely by Budgeting Behavior (0.783, 151.364), then Savings Behavior (0.181, 15.863) and Financial Confidence (0.048, 57.542) (Table 5, p. 110).
- Financial Literacy and Financial Confidence exhibit good reliability (Cronbach's Alpha 0.795 and 0.901); the other three constructs fall below 0.70 (p. 108).
- All constructs have Composite Reliability above 0.70; AVE exceeds 0.50 for Financial Literacy, Financial Confidence and Savings Behavior, but Budgeting Behavior (0.483) and Investment Behavior (0.484) fall below 0.50 (p. 108).
- All indicators have VIF values below 5, indicating no significant multicollinearity (p. 108).
- The paper concludes that budgeting alone does not directly impact savings behavior, and in some cases an increase in savings behavior can lead to a decrease in financial confidence (Conclusion, p. 111).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The authors acknowledge that the unexpected indirect relationship between savings behavior and financial confidence warrants further exploration, suggesting diligent savers may still feel uncertain about their financial future due to broader economic or personal concerns (Discussion, p. 111).
- The authors acknowledge that the lack of a meaningful statistical relationship between budgeting behavior and savings behavior challenges the conventional assumption that better budgeting directly leads to increased savings, and that other factors such as income level or financial goals may play a more prominent role (Discussion, p. 111).
- The authors call for a more nuanced approach to financial education and advisory services addressing both practical and emotional aspects of financial management, and for future research into the factors that shape financial confidence (Conclusion, p. 111).
- [unacknowledged] The paper contradicts itself on the t-statistic for the SB → FC path: Table 4 (p. 109) prints 2.695, while the results text (p. 110) prints "a t-value of 2.659". Both are recorded in the Statistical Evidence table.
- [unacknowledged] The Budgeting Behavior construct contains two negative factor loadings ("Maintaining a personal or household budget" -0.516; "Frequency of updating of your budget" -0.329) and the Investment Behavior construct contains two negative loadings ("Investment in any financial products" -0.513; "Type of investments do you hold" -0.034), which the paper notes may indicate reverse-coded questions or a problem with the model (p. 108).
- [unacknowledged] Budgeting Behavior (0.483) and Investment Behavior (0.484) have AVE values below the 0.50 threshold, and Cronbach's Alpha for Budgeting Behavior (0.158), Savings Behavior (0.191) and Investment Behavior (0.293) are far below the 0.70 threshold, indicating weak internal consistency the authors do not flag as disqualifying.
- [unacknowledged] In Table 3 the Investment Behavior diagonal value (0.695) is lower than its off-diagonal correlation with Financial Confidence (0.883), a Fornell-Larcker violation of discriminant validity the paper does not acknowledge despite claiming "strong discriminant validity".
- [unacknowledged] The sample of 100 respondents is small for SEM with five latent constructs, and no power analysis, response rate, or demographic breakdown by count is reported; the paper also reports no confidence intervals for any path estimate.
- [unacknowledged] Non-probability sampling through social media, community forums and local organizations limits generalizability; the geographic setting and country are never stated.
- [unacknowledged] The paper reports no model fit statistics (e.g., SRMR, R², Q²) and no effect sizes, so the overall explanatory quality of the structural model cannot be assessed.
- [unacknowledged] Table 1 item labels for BB2 (mean 3.088) and IB2 (mean 3.269) are reported to three decimals while every other mean is reported to three decimals as well, but the descriptive table is presented without the item wording, so what each item code represents is only recoverable from Table 2.

## Definitions

- **Financial Literacy (FL)** — The knowledge and qualities wanted to make well-planned decisions regarding budgeting, saving and investing; measured by four indicators (FL1–FL4).
- **Budgeting Behavior (BB)** — Creating a financial plan that balances income and expenses to meet financial obligations and prepare for future needs; measured by five indicators (BB1–BB5).
- **Investment Behavior (IB)** — Allocation of resources to assets like stocks, bonds or real estate with the expectation of returns; measured by five indicators (IB1–IB5).
- **Savings Behavior (SB)** — Reserving a part of income to meet future needs, whether for emergencies, short-term goals or long-term objectives; measured by two indicators (SB1–SB2).
- **Financial Confidence (FC)** — Confidence about one's financial future and related attitudes; measured by five indicators (FC1–FC5).
- **Cronbach's Alpha** — Internal coherence measure; values above 0.70 indicate acceptable reliability, below 0.70 weaker consistency (p. 108).
- **Composite Reliability (CR)** — Reliability measure like Cronbach's Alpha, with values above 0.70 acceptable (p. 108).
- **Average Variance Extracted (AVE)** — The extent of variance collected by a construct compared to variance attributed to measurement error; values above 0.50 considered acceptable (p. 108).
- **Variance Inflation Factor (VIF)** — Multicollinearity measure among indicators; a value above 5 suggests problematic collinearity (p. 108).
- **Fornell-Larcker Criterion** — Discriminant validity approach; the square root of AVE (diagonal) should exceed correlations between constructs (off-diagonal) (p. 108).
- **IPMA (Importance-Performance Map Analysis)** — Assesses the significance and effectiveness of latent variables in relation to the target construct (p. 110).

## Remember This

- Financial literacy has a strong, significant positive effect on budgeting behavior (β = 0.783, p = 0.000) and investment behavior (β = 0.786, p = 0.000).
- Budgeting behavior does not significantly predict savings behavior (β = -0.231, p = 0.081); the paper rejects H1.
- Savings behavior has a significant but negative relationship with financial confidence (β = -0.266, p = 0.007).
- The paper internally contradicts itself on the SB → FC t-statistic: 2.695 in Table 4 vs 2.659 in the results text.
- Several constructs fail conventional reliability/validity thresholds (BB alpha = 0.158, SB alpha = 0.191, IB alpha = 0.293; BB AVE = 0.483, IB AVE = 0.484) and the paper's discriminant-validity claim is contradicted by the IB–FC off-diagonal (0.883) exceeding the IB diagonal (0.695).
- Investment Behavior is the highest-importance construct in the IPMA, followed closely by Budgeting Behavior.
