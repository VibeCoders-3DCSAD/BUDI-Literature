## Summary

This is a convergent mixed-methods study of how artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are being taken up in financial markets and what that uptake is perceived to do. The quantitative arm is an online questionnaire administered through Google Forms between March and April 2023 to 144 financial professionals — traders, portfolio managers and analysts — drawn purposively from the United States and European regions, yielding 120 responses and a response rate of 83% (Sect. 2.2, p. 7). The qualitative arm is a content analysis, coded in NVivo, of academic papers, reports and industry articles, organised into five themes: adoption trends, barriers to adoption, the role of regulation, workforce transformation, and ethical and social considerations (Sect. 3.3, pp. 10-11).

The quantitative results report that algorithmic trading (70.8%) and risk management (58.3%) are the most prevalent application areas among respondents (Table 2, p. 8), that improved efficiency and increased accuracy are each endorsed as benefits by 83.3% agreeing or strongly agreeing (Sect. 3.1, p. 8), and that a negative correlation of −0.41 (p < 0.01) links years of professional experience to AI/ML adoption (Table 4, p. 9). A multiple regression with the perceived impact of AI/ML on financial markets as dependent variable reports R2 = 0.55, Adjusted R2 = 0.53, F(4, 115) = 35.60, p < 0.001, with level of AI/ML adoption carrying the largest standardised weight (Table 5, p. 9).

The paper's own framing is normative as well as descriptive: it argues that a comprehensive approach coupling regulation, workforce transformation and ethical considerations is what turns AI/ML adoption into enhanced operational efficiency, improved decision making and increased public trust (Hypothesis 1, p. 6), and it concludes that the findings "strongly support" that hypothesis (p. 12). It offers no formal test of the hypothesis beyond narrative synthesis of the survey and literature.

The paper is not a software-development study and reports no system, no algorithm implementation, and no model-training pipeline of its own; its quantitative contribution is the survey of perceptions and the regression on perceived impact, and its qualitative contribution is a thematic synthesis of the secondary literature. Credit risk assessment and credit scoring appear throughout as the leading illustrative domain for AI/ML in finance (Sect. 1.3, p. 4), and fairness in credit-scoring and risk-assessment decisions is singled out among the ethical challenges (Sect. 1.4, p. 5).

## Problem and Motivation

The authors state that AI and ML have already reshaped trading procedures and risk management (p. 1), that their applications in finance date back to the 1980s and now include price forecasting and fraud detection (Boukherouaa et al. 2021, p. 1), and that a World Economic Forum study projects that financial-sector integration of these technologies "could lead to an added value of a trillion dollars by 2025" (p. 1). Against that, they argue that "there is a limited body of research that has thoroughly explored how AI and ML have enhanced the financial sector's performance and stability" (p. 1), and that a further examination of these technologies' influence on financial markets is required alongside an understanding of how they could be efficiently integrated within existing financial infrastructures (p. 1).

The stated aim is therefore to fill that knowledge gap by exploring the impact and contributions of AI and ML on the financial sector's performance and stability, using a two-step empirical approach drawing on case studies, surveys and data analysis, and to formulate a hypothesis to guide the analysis (pp. 1-2). The paper also sets out to review principal use cases (trading, risk management, other financial services), to highlight core benefits and potential drawbacks including efficiency gains, minimised human error, and ethical and regulatory issues, and to introduce a conceptual model scrutinising the influence of AI and ML on financial markets across aspects of industry performance and stability (p. 2).

The motivating problems that recur across the paper are: imprecision and potential unfairness in traditional credit decisioning built on predefined parameters and thresholds (Sect. 1.3, p. 4); opacity and limited explainability of AI/ML models relative to what regulators require (Sect. 1.4, p. 5); systemic risk from herding when AI/ML systems are trained on the same data or adopt similar strategies (Sect. 1.4, p. 5); and the possibility of large-scale job displacement in the financial workforce through automation (Sect. 3.3, p. 11). No research question is stated in interrogative form; the paper's explicit formal statement is Hypothesis 1 (p. 6).

## Method

**Design.** Convergent mixed-methods approach integrating qualitative and quantitative data (Sect. 2.1, p. 6); the authors state no other design label. The rationale given is triangulation to bolster validity and reliability (Sect. 2.1, p. 6).
**Sample.** n = 120 financial professionals (traders, portfolio managers and analysts) responding to an online questionnaire issued to 144 invitees; the unit of analysis is the individual survey respondent. A second, unbounded corpus of academic papers, reports and articles supplies the qualitative arm (Sect. 2.2, pp. 6-7).
**Context.** geography: the United States and the European regions (Sect. 2.2, p. 7); population: financial professionals in firms classified as small (1–50 employees), medium (51–200 employees) and large (200+/201+ employees) (Sect. 2.3, p. 7; Table 1, p. 8); setting: online questionnaire hosted on Google Forms, administered March 2023 to April 2023 (Sect. 2.2, p. 7).

Procedures as reported:

- Draw primary data from an online questionnaire and secondary data from extant literature, finance databases and industry reports (Sect. 2.2, p. 6).
- Recruit through purposive sampling "to ensure that the respondents possess the necessary expertise and experience to offer meaningful insights" (Sect. 2.2, p. 6).
- Retrieve secondary data from JSTOR, ScienceDirect and Google Scholar, and from journals named as the Journal of Finance, IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems, and the Journal of Machine Learning Research (Sect. 2.2, p. 7).
- Organise the questionnaire into four sections: participant demographics; degree of AI and ML usage in their firms; views on the influence of AI and ML on financial markets; and perspectives on future trends (Sect. 2.3, p. 7).
- Classify firms by size as small (1–50), medium (51–200) or large (200+) in the demographics section to allow analysis by firm scale (Sect. 2.3, p. 7).
- Analyse quantitative data in SPSS with descriptive and inferential statistics presented in tabular form (Sect. 2.3, p. 7).
- Run a multiple regression of the perceived influence of AI and ML on financial markets on level of AI/ML implementation, organisation size, and years of experience in the finance sector (Sect. 2.3, p. 7).
- Code and examine qualitative data in NVivo, executing content analysis on academic papers, reports and articles to spot themes and patterns, then deliberate those themes against the hypothesis (Sect. 2.3, p. 7).
- Report ethics: the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Holy Spirit University of Kaslik, protocol code HCR/EC 2023-058, on 01-03-2023; informed consent was obtained from all subjects (p. 14).

The regression table (Table 5, p. 9) reports a constant of 2.35, standardised weights of 0.18 (years of experience), 0.20 (company size), 0.40 (level of AI/ML adoption) and 0.26 (attitude toward AI/ML), with R2 = 0.55, Adjusted R2 = 0.53 and F(4, 115) = 35.60, p < 0.001. The narrative discussion reports the same model differently: it states that "the model accounted for 55 percent of the variation in the perceived impact of AI and ML on financial markets (Adjusted R2 = 0.55)" (Sect. 3.2, p. 10), and it describes the predictor weights using the unstandardised B values while labelling them "Beta" (Sect. 3.2, p. 10). Both versions are recorded below without reconciliation.

No confirmatory factor analysis is reported, although the paper's outline of its own structure promises "descriptive statistics and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) for the survey results on the impact of AI and ML in the financial markets" (p. 2).

## Key Findings

- num: Algorithmic trading (85 respondents, 70.8%) and risk management (70, 58.3%) are the most prevalent AI/ML application areas; portfolio optimization is 65 (54.2%) and fraud detection 60 (50.0%) (Table 2, p. 8).
- num: Large firms with 201 or more employees show a higher adoption rate of 54.2% than smaller companies (Sect. 3.1, p. 8; Table 1, p. 8).
- num: Improved efficiency and increased accuracy are each endorsed by 83.3% agreeing or strongly agreeing (Sect. 3.1, p. 8).
- num: Job loss is disputed rather than endorsed: 45.8% disagree or strongly disagree, and ethical and privacy concerns are disputed by 41.7% (Sect. 3.1, p. 8).
- num: Years of experience correlates with AI/ML adoption at −0.41, significant at p < 0.01, which the authors read as less-experienced professionals being more predisposed to use AI/ML tools (Table 4, p. 9).
- num: The regression explains 55% of the variation in perceived impact per the narrative and reports Adjusted R2 = 0.53 in Table 5; the constant is 2.35 and the largest standardised predictor is level of AI/ML adoption (Beta = 0.40) (Table 5, p. 9; Sect. 3.2, p. 10).
- num: Survey demographics: 66.7% male, modal age band 30–39 years (37.5%), modal experience band 5–10 years (41.7%), and 54.2% employed by large firms (Table 1, p. 8).
- The qualitative arm identifies five themes: adoption trends; challenges and barriers including implementation costs, data and infrastructure deficits, privacy issues and regulatory compliance; the role of regulation; workforce transformation; and ethical and social considerations (Sect. 3.3, pp. 10-11).
- The paper reports that "algorithmic bias, fairness, and transparency" were widely discussed in the literature and that public trust depends on the industry's ability to address them (Sect. 3.3, p. 11).
- The paper concludes that "the findings strongly support our hypothesis" without reporting a statistical test of the hypothesis itself (p. 12).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The stated structure promises that "conclusions, limitations and recommendations for future research are presented" (p. 2), but Sect. 5 contains no limitations statement; the reader is given no author-flagged account of what the study cannot support. [unacknowledged]
- The Introduction advertises "descriptive statistics and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) for the survey results" (p. 2). No confirmatory factor analysis appears anywhere in Sect. 3; the reported analyses stop at cross-tabulations, one correlation and one multiple regression. [unacknowledged]
- The paper contradicts itself on the fit of its own regression model: the narrative says the model accounted for "55 percent of the variation ... (Adjusted R2 = 0.55)" (Sect. 3.2, p. 10), while Table 5 prints "Adjusted R2 = 0.53" (p. 9). Both values are recorded below; the paper does not flag or resolve the discrepancy. [unacknowledged]
- The narrative reports the regression predictors using the unstandardised B column while labelling them "Beta" — "(Beta = 0.45, p < 0.001)" for level of AI/ML adoption, "(Beta = 0.30, p < 0.001)" for attitude, "(Beta = 0.15, p < 0.001)" for firm size, and "(Beta = 0.12, p < 0.001)" for years of experience (Sect. 3.2, p. 10). Table 5 gives B = 0.45/0.30/0.15/0.12 and Beta = 0.40/0.26/0.20/0.18 for the same rows. Both sets are recorded below; the paper does not note the mismatch. [unacknowledged]
- The bivariate correlation between years of experience and AI/ML adoption is negative (−0.41, Table 4, p. 9) and is interpreted as less-experienced professionals being more predisposed to use AI/ML tools (Sect. 3.1, p. 9), while the regression reports a positive coefficient for years of experience (B = 0.12; Beta = 0.18, Table 5, p. 9) and interprets it as experience positively influencing perceived impact (Sect. 3.2, p. 10). The two analyses use different dependent variables (adoption versus perceived impact), but the paper never states this and never reconciles the two readings. [unacknowledged]
- No CI, no confidence interval, no standard error and no significance test is reported for any of the descriptive percentages in Tables 1, 2 or 3, and no reliability statistic for the questionnaire instrument (for example a Cronbach's alpha or a pilot validation) is reported anywhere. [unacknowledged]
- The Likert data in Table 3 are printed as raw counts only; no aggregate score, no test of difference and no factor structure is reported for the six attitude items. [unacknowledged]
- The regression reports no confidence intervals, no variance inflation factors, no residual diagnostics and no assumption checks. [unacknowledged]
- The paper's own regression narrative claims significance for the collective model on the basis of F(4, 115) = 35.60, p < 0.001 (Sect. 3.2, p. 10), but gives no cross-validation, no out-of-sample check and no sensitivity analysis; the finding is a single in-sample fit on 120 responses. [unacknowledged]
- Sampling is purposive with no sampling frame, no stated inclusion criteria beyond occupation, and no non-response analysis; with 144 invitations and 120 responses the paper cannot describe who declined. [unacknowledged]
- The authors state that the majority of surveyed financial experts came from the USA and Europe, "two regions that are frequently in the forefront of technology developments in the financial sector" (p. 13). This is the closest the paper comes to acknowledging a scope limit; it is presented as a strength of coverage rather than as a constraint on generalisability.
- The qualitative arm is described as content analysis coded in NVivo (Sect. 2.3, p. 7), but the paper reports no corpus size, no search strings, no coding scheme, no coder agreement and no audit trail for theme derivation; the five themes (Sect. 3.3, pp. 10-11) cannot be reproduced from what is printed. [unacknowledged]
- The paper asserts that "the findings strongly support our hypothesis" (p. 12) but Hypothesis 1 (p. 6) is a compound claim about regulation, workforce transformation and ethical considerations producing efficiency, better decisions and public trust; none of those three outcome terms is measured. [unacknowledged]
- The referenced "conceptual model" that the Introduction says will be introduced to scrutinise the influence of AI and ML on financial markets (p. 2) is never presented as a model, a diagram or a set of estimated paths. [unacknowledged]
- The paper reports a "54.2%" adoption rate for large firms (Sect. 3.1, p. 8) but gives no comparable adoption figure for small or medium firms, so the stated comparison "compared to smaller companies" cannot be checked against the printed tables. [unacknowledged]
- Several quantitative claims in the Introduction and Sect. 1 are attributed to other authors (for example the 70 percent HFT share of US equity trade volume attributed to Miller and Shorter 2016, p. 2) and are not part of this study's evidence base; they are not treated as the paper's results here.

## Definitions

- **Artificial intelligence (AI)** — used throughout as the umbrella term for computational systems performing tasks associated with trading, risk management, fraud detection, credit scoring and customer service in finance (Abstract, p. 1).
- **Machine learning (ML)** — used alongside AI as the learning-from-data component of those systems; the paper treats neural networks and deep learning as the leading ML methods in trading and investment (Sect. 1.2, p. 3).
- **High-frequency trading (HFT)** — "an investment approach that leverages advanced computers and intricate algorithms to initiate rapid and automated buy and sell orders", capitalising on minute price shifts often within milliseconds (Sect. 1.1, p. 2).
- **Robo-advisors** — described as a cost-effective and readily accessible alternative to traditional financial advisors, enabling more individuals to benefit from personalised investment advice (Sect. 1.2, p. 4).
- **Credit risk assessment** — the domain in which traditional models built on predefined parameters and thresholds are contrasted with AI/ML models that digest transaction histories, spending habits and online behaviour (Sect. 1.3, p. 4).
- **Deep neural networks (DNNs)** — described as utilising multiple interconnected layers to process data hierarchically into increasingly abstract representations, in contrast to traditional linear models (Sect. 1.2, p. 3).
- **Convergent mixed-methods approach** — the study's design, integrating qualitative and quantitative data for triangulation and for capturing the complexity of the financial industry (Sect. 2.1, p. 6).
- **Purposive sampling** — the recruitment method used to ensure respondents possess the expertise and experience to offer meaningful insights (Sect. 2.2, p. 6).
- **Hypothesis 1** — "The integration of AI and ML in financial markets, guided by comprehensive regulation, adaptive workforce transformation, and ethical considerations, leads to enhanced operational efficiency, improved decision making, and increased public trust" (p. 6).
- **Explainability** — the paper's term for the demand that AI/ML models provide insights into their decision-making processes, which regulators push for (Sect. 1.4, p. 5).

## Remember This

- The paper is a convergent mixed-methods study — an online survey of 120 financial professionals in the US and Europe plus an NVivo-coded review of secondary literature — not a system or algorithm development paper.
- Its own numbers are Tables 1 to 5 on pages 8 and 9: demographics, application areas, Likert counts, one correlation (−0.41, p < 0.01) and one multiple regression (R2 = 0.55; Adjusted R2 = 0.53 per the table).
- The narrative and the table disagree twice: Adjusted R2 is printed as 0.55 in the text (p. 10) and 0.53 in Table 5 (p. 9); and the text's "Beta" values (0.45, 0.30, 0.15, 0.12) are the table's unstandardised B column, not its Beta column (0.40, 0.26, 0.20, 0.18).
- A promised confirmatory factor analysis (p. 2) never appears, and the promised "limitations" section is absent from the conclusions.
