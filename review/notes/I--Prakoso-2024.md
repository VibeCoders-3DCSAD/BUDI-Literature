## Summary

This note extracts a single paper: a narrative literature review on personal financial management, published in the Indonesian Journal of Islamic Economics and Finance (Vol. 4, No. 2, 2024, pp. 257–272). The paper's stated aim is "to review the current literature on personal financial management, focusing on effective budgeting and saving techniques" (p. 259). Its thesis is stated in the title and repeated in the abstract: budgeting and saving are the two pillars that hold up sustainable personal financial management, and they "complement each other in achieving sustainable personal financial stability" (Abstract, p. 257).

The paper is not an empirical study. It reports no sample of its own, no instrument, no data collection, no analysis, and no result table or figure anywhere in its sixteen pages. Everything quantitative it prints is a restatement of someone else's published figure. The note's Statistical Evidence section below is therefore short, and that shortness is a finding about the paper, not an omission by the extractor.

**What the paper argues, section by section.**

The Introduction (pp. 257–259) opens with the claim that the ability to manage personal finances is "a very important yet often overlooked skill," and that "many individuals face financial difficulties not because of low income but because of the inability to manage finances effectively" (p. 257). It attributes this to a pattern visible across countries: high consumer debt, low savings rates, and unpreparedness for retirement (p. 257).

The Introduction then sets out two named personal-finance frameworks. The first is Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi's allocation, cited as (Warren, 2006): 50% for necessities covering rent, utilities, food, transport and insurance; 30% for wishes covering entertainment, eating out and holidays; and 20% for savings and debt repayment including investments, emergency funds and overpayments to reduce debt (p. 257). The second is Robert Kiyosaki's, cited as (Kiyosaki, 2017), which "emphasizes the importance of setting aside money for savings and investments before using money for other expenses" (p. 258). These are the paper's only two named normative frameworks, and both are introduced without data.

Financial management is then defined at the personal and household level as "the process of planning, organizing, directing, and controlling financial resources to achieve predetermined financial goals," and said to include "budgeting, managing income and expenses, saving, investing, managing debt, and planning for the financial future" (p. 258, citing Low, 2024). Its main objectives are given as maximising the use of available resources, minimising financial risk, and achieving long-term stability and security (p. 258, citing Deventer, 2020).

The paper then presents three external statistics to establish that practice lags knowledge: an OJK survey from 2019 putting Indonesian financial literacy at 38.03% (p. 258); a Banco de México finding from 2021 that only 33% of respondents regularly prepare and follow a monthly budget (p. 258); and World Bank data from 2022 showing that only 42% of adults in developing countries save in formal financial institutions (p. 259). From these it concludes that many individuals are "trapped in a cycle of debt, struggle to fulfill basic needs, or do not have adequate emergency funds" (p. 258).

A further strand of the Introduction concerns financial technology. The paper notes that fintech "has opened up new opportunities in personal finance management," but that these technologies "can only be optimally utilized with a basic understanding and skills in budgeting and saving" (p. 259, citing BEKTAŞ, 2021). It also warns that fintech brings risk: fraud and cybercrime targeting the unwary, and easy access to credit through online platforms that "can encourage excessive consumptive behavior, making individuals more vulnerable to debt traps" (p. 259, citing Noor, 2020; Susan, 2020).

The Methods section (p. 259) states the paper uses "the literature research method," described as an approach "used to collect information through various existing written sources, such as books, academic journals, articles, and other documents" (p. 259, citing Firman, 2018). The method is described in four procedural stages: identification and selection of relevant sources with defined inclusion and exclusion criteria; classification and organisation of information by theme; analysis and synthesis including critical review of content, comparison of similarities, differences and patterns, and evaluation of methodological quality and validity; and formulation of implications, recommendations and future research questions (pp. 259–260).

The Findings and Discussion section is organised into six subsections, but only five are distinct: **3.1 Personal Finance Management** (pp. 260–261), **3.2 Budget Preparation** (p. 261), **3.3 Effective Saving** (pp. 262–263), **3.4 Strategies for Effective Budgeting** (p. 263), **3.5 Challenges in Personal Finance Management** (pp. 264–265), and **3.6 Personal Finance Management** (pp. 265–267). Subsections 3.1 and 3.6 carry the identical heading; the second is a partially overlapping restatement that adds debt management, investment, financial education, SMART goal-setting and periodic review.

Section 3.1 defines personal financial management as "the process of planning, organizing, controlling, and supervising the financial resources of an individual or family," encompassing "managing one's income, expenses, savings, investments, and protection of financial assets" (p. 260, citing Madakkatel, 2022). Its objectives are to balance income and expenditure, maximise wealth, and achieve short- and long-term financial goals (p. 260, citing Lovita, 2023). In practice it covers budgeting, tax planning, debt management, retirement planning and risk management through insurance, plus understanding bank accounts, credit cards, loans and investment instruments (p. 260, citing Kumar, 2024). The paper states that effective personal financial management "requires knowledge, skills, and discipline" and that applying it increases control, reduces financial stress and raises the chances of long-term financial freedom (p. 260, citing Likhonosova, 2024). It then argues for its importance: optimising resources, avoiding unnecessary debt, building assets, preparing for emergencies, planning retirement, and financing goals such as a home or a child's education (pp. 260–261, citing Ostrovska, 2020; Aduda, 2020).

Section 3.2 defines a budget as "a systematically prepared and detailed financial plan for a specific period, usually one year," including estimates of expected income and expenditure and the allocation of resources to goals and activities (p. 261, citing Aduda, 2020). It positions the budget as "a planning and control tool" that lets an individual "map cash flow, prioritize spending, identify areas of potential savings, and ensure that both short-term and long-term financial goals are achieved" (p. 261). The section then enumerates seven personal budget types: traditional budgets dividing income into fixed and variable categories; the 50/30/20 budget; the envelope budget using physical envelopes per spending category; values-based budgets prioritising spending by personal values; zero budgets requiring every expense to be considered and justified each period; pay-yourself-first budgets prioritising savings before other expenses; and flexible budgets adjustable as income or circumstances change (p. 261, citing Asadullah, 2020). It adds that specialised budgets exist for travel, holidays or projects, and that the choice depends on financial situation, lifestyle and goals, with combinations often used (p. 261, citing Tripathi, 2023).

Section 3.3 defines saving as "setting aside a portion of one's income or money to be saved and not used for current consumption, aiming to meet future needs or wants," achieved by reducing current expenditure to accumulate funds for later use, in forms ranging from a bank account to a piggy bank to financial instruments (p. 262, citing Hendrati, 2021). Purposes named include emergency funds, education, valuable assets and retirement; benefits named include financial discipline, increased security and peace of mind (p. 262, citing Salam, 2022). The paper then lists a nine-item effective savings strategy: set clear and realistic goals; apply "pay yourself first"; automate transfers from salary to savings accounts; reduce unnecessary expenses and redirect the funds; use financial apps to track spending and progress; diversify across deposits, mutual funds and bonds by risk profile; increase income through side jobs or investments and allocate the extra to savings; stay motivated by celebrating achieved savings targets; and continuously educate oneself about personal finance and investment (p. 262, citing Challoumis, 2024; Ajija, 2021). It closes with an eight-item numbered list of the benefits of saving, running from emergency-fund security and goal achievement through discipline, passive income, reduced stress, flexibility and freedom, debt avoidance, and early-instilled frugality (pp. 262–263).

Section 3.4 sets out a four-step budgeting strategy. First, track all income and expenses for several months, recording every transaction including regular and unexpected or seasonal expenses, to identify where spending may be excessive (p. 263, citing Jiang, 2024). Second, set clear and realistic financial goals that are "specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound (SMART)" (p. 263, citing Naji, 2024; Anyika, 2020). Third, allocate income to spending categories by priority, with the 50/30/20 rule given as the popular method, adjusted to individual situations and including an emergency fund, while looking for ways to cut unnecessary expenses (p. 263, citing Wang, 2023; Kurshan, 2022). Fourth, implement and evaluate regularly — using financial apps or spreadsheets, reviewing the budget monthly against actual expenditure, and keeping the budget flexible enough to accommodate changing circumstances (p. 263, citing Puspita, 2022; ILKHOMOV, 2024).

Section 3.5 names five challenges. The first is lack of financial knowledge and education, particularly around compound interest, investments and retirement planning, leading to uninformed or harmful decisions (p. 264, citing Lapika, 2020; Trent, 2021). The second is discipline and consistency: impulse purchases, a lifestyle exceeding means, and procrastination on saving and investing, compounded by cognitive biases and entrenched habits (p. 264, citing Romenska, 2024; Telnov, 2024). The third is the complexity and uncertainty of the modern financial environment: globalised markets, rapid technological change, unpredictable economic fluctuations, diverse investment options, fiscal and monetary policy shifts, cybersecurity risk, and demographic change such as increased life expectancy (p. 264, citing Mostova, 2024; Zhydovska, 2024). The fourth is social pressure and lifestyle expectations — "fear of missing out" (FOMO), comparison with others, overspending, debt accumulation and neglected long-term priorities (p. 264, citing Jung, 2022; Nikolaienko, 2024). The fifth is income uncertainty and job security under the gig economy, requiring larger emergency funds and diversified income (p. 265, citing Bhattacharjee, 2024; Andrushchak, 2024).

Section 3.6 restates personal financial management as planning, organising, directing and controlling financial activities to achieve personal financial goals, with the fundamental aspects being budget planning, spending control, debt management and investment (p. 265, citing Warren, 2006). It presents a budget as the first step, categorising expenses into basic needs (housing, food, transportation) and secondary needs (entertainment, holidays) (p. 265, citing Detweiler, 2013). Debt management is described as strategies to pay off existing debt, manage interest payments and avoid new unnecessary debt, distinguishing consumptive debt such as credit cards from productive debt such as student loans or mortgages, and naming the snowball and avalanche methods (pp. 265–266, citing Bernheim, 2001). Savings and investment are described as protection against unexpected situations and preparation for a stable future, requiring understanding of stocks, bonds, mutual funds and property with their risks and returns (p. 266, citing Yates, 2015). Financial education is called "the foundation of effective personal finance management," delivered through books, online courses, seminars or professional planners (p. 266, citing Lusardi, 2011). SMART goal-setting is restated, as is periodic review and adjustment of the financial plan when life changes (p. 266, citing Stolper, 2017; Mandell, 2008). The section closes by noting that managing personal finances is challenging, that not all individuals have sufficient knowledge or skills, that social pressure and consumptive lifestyles obstruct budget discipline and saving, and that inflation and volatile economic change complicate long-term planning (pp. 266–267, citing Garman, 2014) — while holding that success is possible through continuous learning, budget-management apps, financial planners, discipline and flexibility.

The Conclusion (p. 267) repeats the thesis without adding evidence: budgeting lets individuals "better plan and control their cash flow," prioritise important expenses and cut unnecessary ones, and monitor and evaluate their financial position regularly; saving provides security and emergency preparedness, builds a reserve fund, prevents debt and creates opportunities to invest; and "these two practices-budgeting and saving-should go hand-in-hand to ensure sustainable financial stability and health."

## Problem and Motivation

The paper's problem statement is that personal financial management is important but poorly practised. The stated phenomenon is that "many individuals face financial difficulties not because of low income but because of the inability to manage finances effectively," evidenced by "high levels of consumer debt, low savings rates, and financial unpreparedness for retirement" (p. 257). The Indonesian framing is financial illiteracy: the OJK 2019 figure of 38.03% financial literacy is used to argue that "many people still need to properly understand basic financial concepts, including the importance of budgeting and saving effectively" (p. 258). The paper states that "currently, Indonesians need help managing their finances" and that one main challenge "is the low level of financial literacy," leading to "unwise financial decisions, such as uncontrolled spending or the utilization of high-interest loan services" (p. 259). A second motivation is technological: fintech offers access but also risk, so "research on effective strategies for budgeting and saving is highly relevant and important" (p. 259). The stated gap is simply that the literature needs reviewing; the paper does not identify a contested finding, an untested mechanism or a measurement problem it intends to resolve.

## Method

**Design.** Literature research method (the authors' own label); described as a qualitative review of previously published written sources — books, academic journals, articles and other documents — with no stated design beyond that label.
**Sample.** N not reported; unit of analysis is previously published written sources (books, academic journals, articles and other documents) selected by researcher-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria (Sect. 2, p. 259). The paper never states how many sources were selected, searched, screened or excluded.
**Context.** geography: Indonesia, with international literature cited (the only country-specific statistics are Indonesian, Mexican and World Bank developing-country figures); population: individuals and families managing personal finances, with a stated focus on Indonesian individuals; setting: desk-based, non-empirical literature review with no fieldwork, no participants and no site.

The procedure as described runs: (1) identification and selection of relevant sources with inclusion and exclusion criteria, searched through academic databases, library catalogues and other online search tools (p. 259); (2) classification and organisation of information by theme or category (p. 260); (3) analysis and synthesis — critical review of source content, examination of similarities, differences and patterns between studies, and evaluation of "the methodological quality and validity of the findings from each source" (p. 260); and (4) discussion of implications and recommendations, identification of gaps, and formulation of new research questions, with results intended to support policy decisions and practical exercises or to develop new theories (p. 260). The paper adds the caveat that the method "still requires scientific integrity and analytical skills to produce a valuable literature review" (p. 260). No search strings, databases, date ranges, screening counts or quality-appraisal instrument are printed, so the procedure cannot be reproduced.

## Key Findings

- The paper's headline claim is normative rather than empirical: budgeting and saving are "two key pillars that complement each other in achieving sustainable personal financial stability" (Abstract, p. 257).
- Budgeting is argued to work by giving "a clear framework for managing income and expenditure," letting individuals monitor cash flow, allocate funds to essential needs, and identify where spending can be reduced (Abstract, p. 257).
- Budgeting is also argued to help "set short-term and long-term goals, encouraging financial discipline and responsibility," and to be associated with better financial health and less debt entrapment — "studies indicate that individuals who consistently follow a budget tend to have better financial health and are less trapped in debt" (Abstract, p. 257), though no such study is named or located.
- Saving is argued to serve two functions: dealing with financial emergencies and achieving future goals such as home purchase, education or retirement (Abstract, p. 257).
- Saving is argued to reduce reliance on loans or credit and to increase financial well-being and reduce money-related stress — "studies have also found that saving regularly can increase financial well-being and reduce money-related stress" (Abstract, p. 257), again without naming a study.
- The paper adopts the 50/30/20 allocation as its recurring reference framework, stating it twice: as Warren and Tyagi's rule at p. 257 and as a "popular method" for allocation at p. 263.
- Seven personal budget types are enumerated (traditional, 50/30/20, envelope, values-based, zero, pay-yourself-first, flexible), with the choice said to depend on financial situation, lifestyle and goals, and combinations often used (p. 261).
- Nine savings strategies and eight benefits of saving are enumerated, with no evidence cited for the effect of any individual strategy (pp. 262–263).
- Five challenges to personal financial management are named: financial illiteracy, weak discipline and consistency, complexity and uncertainty of modern financial markets, social pressure and lifestyle expectations, and income uncertainty under the gig economy (pp. 264–265).
- The claimed benefits of financial management are framed as life outcomes, not measured ones: financial stability, reduced stress, emergency readiness, better retirement planning, and "a sense of financial security and freedom that positively affects various aspects of life" (p. 258).

## Limitations and Gaps

- **The paper is a narrative review with no primary evidence.** It reports no sample, no instrument, no data collection, no analysis and no results table or figure. Its only numeric content is three externally sourced statistics and the 50/30/20 allocation. Nothing in the paper measures whether budgeting or saving actually produces the outcomes it attributes to them. [unacknowledged]
- **Sources named in the abstract are never identified.** The abstract asserts that "studies indicate that individuals who consistently follow a budget tend to have better financial health and are less trapped in debt" and that "studies have also found that saving regularly can increase financial well-being and reduce money-related stress" (p. 257), but no study is cited for either claim anywhere in the paper. [unacknowledged]
- **The method section is procedural description, not a report.** No databases, search strings, date ranges, inclusion/exclusion criteria, screening counts or quality-appraisal tool are printed (Sect. 2, pp. 259–260), so the review cannot be reproduced or assessed for coverage or selection bias. [unacknowledged]
- **The paper contradicts itself editorially.** Two subsections share the identical heading "Personal Finance Management" — Sect. 3.1 (p. 260) and Sect. 3.6 (p. 265) — and their content overlaps (both define personal financial management, both cover budgeting and saving). The structure does not distinguish what the second treatment adds. [unacknowledged]
- **The reference base is partly unrelated to personal finance.** A substantial share of the cited works concern public budgeting and state finance rather than household finance — for example ILKHOMOV (2024) on programme-based budgeting of state budget funds in Uzbekistan, Jung (2022) on budget transparency, Tripathi (2023) on "Economies of fiscal budget," and Makarov (2024) on coal supply forecast scenarios (reference list, pp. 268–272). These are attached to personal-finance claims in the body text. [unacknowledged]
- **Several in-text citations appear mismatched to their claims.** For example, the 50/30/20 allocation rule and the World Bank savings statistic are both supported by "(Wang, 2023)," a reference whose listed title concerns intelligent analysis in regional economic development decision-making (p. 263; p. 259; reference list, p. 271). The debt-repayment snowball/avalanche passage is supported by "(Bernheim, 2001)," a listed study of high-school financial curriculum mandates (p. 266; reference list, p. 268). [unacknowledged]
- **The reference list contains citation errors.** "(Samoshkina, 2024)" in the text (p. 258) appears as "amoshkina, I. (2024)" in the list; "(oha, 2022)" (p. 258) appears as "oha, M. &. (2022)"; and one entry, "Ginavičienė, J. &. (n.d.)", has no year and a truncated author list (reference list, pp. 268–271). [unacknowledged]
- **The Indonesian focus is asserted but not evidenced.** The paper states that "currently, Indonesians need help managing their finances" (p. 259) and that Indonesian financial literacy is low, but the only Indonesian evidence given is the OJK 2019 literacy figure; the budgeting-adherence and saving statistics come from Mexico and from World Bank developing-country aggregates (pp. 258–259). [unacknowledged]
- **The three quantitative figures are second-hand, dated and unverifiable from this paper.** The OJK 2019 figure, the Banco de México 2021 figure and the World Bank 2022 figure are each cited through a secondary reference (MacNeil, 2022; Bryans, 2020; Wang, 2023 respectively) rather than the primary survey, and none is accompanied by a definition of the construct measured, the sample, or a margin of error. [unacknowledged]
- **Recency is claimed but not demonstrated.** The paper argues that fintech development makes research on budgeting and saving "highly relevant and important" (p. 259), yet the review contains no section on digital budgeting tools, no evaluation of any budgeting application, and no evidence on whether software-supported budgeting changes outcomes. [unacknowledged]
- The authors acknowledge no limitations of their own. There is no limitations paragraph anywhere in the paper, and the Conclusion (p. 267) restates the thesis without qualification. [unacknowledged]
- The paper's own caution about method — that literature research "still requires scientific integrity and analytical skills to produce a valuable literature review" (p. 260) — is the nearest thing to a self-critique, and it is a general statement about the method rather than about this review.

## Definitions

- **Personal financial management** — "the process of planning, organizing, controlling, and supervising the financial resources of an individual or family," encompassing income, expenses, savings, investments and protection of financial assets (Sect. 3.1, p. 260).
- **Financial management (general)** — "the process of planning, organizing, directing, and controlling financial resources to achieve predetermined financial goals" (Sect. 1, p. 258).
- **Budget** — "a systematically prepared and detailed financial plan for a specific period, usually one year," including estimates of expected income and expenditure and allocation of resources to goals and activities (Sect. 3.2, p. 261).
- **50/30/20 budget** — a budget allocating 50% of income to needs, 30% to wants, and 20% to savings or debt repayment (Sect. 3.2, p. 261; restated Sect. 3.4, p. 263).
- **Envelope budget** — a budget in which cash is allocated into physical envelopes for different spending categories (Sect. 3.2, p. 261).
- **Values-based budget** — a budget that prioritises spending based on personal values (Sect. 3.2, p. 261).
- **Zero budget** — a budget that requires every expense to be considered and justified each period (Sect. 3.2, p. 261).
- **Pay-yourself-first budget** — a budget that prioritises savings before other expenses (Sect. 3.2, p. 261).
- **Flexible budget** — a budget that can be adjusted as income or circumstances change (Sect. 3.2, p. 261).
- **Saving** — "setting aside a portion of one's income or money to be saved and not used for current consumption, aiming to meet future needs or wants" (Sect. 3.3, p. 262).
- **SMART goals** — goals that are "specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound," applied to both budgeting and personal financial goal-setting (Sect. 3.4, p. 263; Sect. 3.6, p. 266).
- **Consumptive debt vs productive debt** — consumptive debt such as credit cards, contrasted with productive debt that generates assets, such as student loans or home mortgages (Sect. 3.6, p. 265).
- **Snowball and avalanche methods** — the two named debt repayment strategies, given without further definition or comparison (Sect. 3.6, p. 265).
- **Emergency fund** — a reserve built to deal with unexpected situations such as job loss or health problems (Sect. 1, p. 258; Sect. 3.3, pp. 262–263).

## Remember This

- This is a narrative literature review, not an empirical study: no sample, no data, no results table, no figure, and no limitations paragraph.
- Its only printed numbers are three second-hand statistics — 38.03% Indonesian financial literacy (OJK 2019), 33% monthly budget adherence (Banco de México 2021), 42% of adults in developing countries saving formally (World Bank 2022) — plus the 50/30/20 allocation.
- The central claim, that budgeting and saving are the two complementary pillars of sustainable personal financial management, is asserted throughout and tested nowhere.
- The abstract cites "studies" for the budget–financial-health and saving–wellbeing links, but neither study is named anywhere in the paper.
- Two subsections carry the identical heading "Personal Finance Management" (Sect. 3.1 and Sect. 3.6), and the reference list leans heavily on public-budgeting literature unrelated to household finance.
