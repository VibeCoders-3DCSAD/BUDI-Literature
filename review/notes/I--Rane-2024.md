# I--Rane-2024

## Summary

A narrative literature review of the acceptance of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies in three sectors — business management, finance, and e-commerce — organised around the factors that drive adoption, the obstacles encountered, and the strategies organisations use to overcome those obstacles. The review is built from academic papers, conference papers and industry publications from the last decade, retrieved from Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, SpringerLink, ScienceDirect and JSTOR, and analysed through keyword extraction, keyword co-occurrence and cluster analysis with VOSviewer, hierarchical clustering and k-means clustering. The paper argues that acceptance is shaped by technological, organisational, environmental and individual factors; that the principal barriers are cost, data privacy and security, legacy-system integration, algorithmic bias, skills shortages, regulatory uncertainty and workforce resistance; and that acceptance improves when organisations build trust through transparency and explainable AI, invest in data governance and continuous AI training, adopt ethical guidelines, engage stakeholders, demonstrate ROI, and align AI initiatives with business strategy. The paper reports no empirical data, no sample size and no outcome statistics of its own; its two numbered tables are qualitative catalogues of barriers and strategies.

## Problem and Motivation

AI has become a transformative force across business management, finance and e-commerce, delivering predictive analytics, automation, personalised customer experiences and improved decision-making, yet its acceptance is uneven and is mediated by technological readiness, organisational culture, perceived benefits, regulation and trust. The paper sets out to map this acceptance landscape comprehensively: to identify which factors drive adoption, which challenges impede it, and which strategies organisations can adopt to enhance it. The motivating gap is that AI's potential is widely asserted while its acceptance remains contested, and the paper positions itself as a roadmap that consolidates factors, challenges and strategies into a single view for practitioners and researchers. The review is explicitly cross-sectoral — business management, finance and e-commerce are treated together because AI applications, challenges and strategies recur across all three — and it uses bibliometric-style keyword analysis to surface the thematic structure of the literature rather than relying on a purely narrative synthesis.

## Method

**Design.** Structured literature review with keyword extraction, keyword co-occurrence analysis and cluster analysis; the authors state no formal design label beyond describing "a structured approach."
**Sample.** N Not reported — the paper never states how many documents were reviewed, how many were screened, or how many were included; the unit of analysis is the published academic paper, conference paper, or industry publication from the last decade indexed in the five named databases.
**Context.** geography: Not reported — no study geography is stated; the authors are affiliated with the University of Mumbai, Mumbai, India, and the databases searched are international; population: Not applicable — no human participants, no survey, no interview, no field site; setting: desk-based literature review and bibliometric/keyword analysis carried out with VOSviewer, hierarchical clustering and k-means clustering, with no stated search date, protocol registration or PRISMA-style flow.

- Define selection criteria as peer-reviewed journals, conference papers and reputable industry publications from the last decade (Sect. 2, p. 24).
- Search Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, SpringerLink, ScienceDirect and JSTOR (Sect. 2, p. 24).
- Extract and analyse the most frequently occurring keywords from the selected literature, categorising them into business management, finance and e-commerce (Sect. 2, p. 24).
- Use VOSviewer for keyword extraction and categorisation (Sect. 2, p. 24).
- Perform keyword co-occurrence analysis to identify how often keywords appear together in the same documents, and visualise co-occurrence networks in VOSviewer to identify clusters (Sect. 2, pp. 24-25).
- Conduct a cluster analysis to group the keyword clusters into broader themes, using hierarchical clustering methods and k-means clustering (Sect. 2, p. 25).
- Present the resulting clusters as four thematic concentrations: decision making and machine learning (green), technology adoption and acceptance (yellow), e-commerce and consumer behaviour (blue), and innovation and Industry 4.0 (red) (Sect. 3.1, pp. 25-27).
- Structure the remainder of the paper as a thematic discussion of factors (Sect. 3.2), challenges and barriers (Sect. 3.3, Table 1), strategies (Sect. 3.4, Table 2), and sector-specific acceptance and integration (Sect. 3.5), followed by a restatement of applications (Sect. 3.6) and conclusions (Sect. 3.7).
- Report no search strings, no inclusion/exclusion counts, no inter-coder agreement, no VOSviewer version, no cluster resolution parameters, no k value, and no date of search.

## Key Findings

- The keyword co-occurrence network places "artificial intelligence" at its centre with extensive links across domains, indicating AI's foundational role in the reviewed literature (Sect. 3.1, p. 25).
- Four thematic clusters emerge: decision making, machine learning and big data; technology adoption, acceptance tests and user acceptance; e-commerce, consumer behaviour and electronic commerce; and innovation, Industry 4.0 and digital transformation (Sect. 3.1, pp. 25-27).
- Factors influencing acceptance are grouped into four categories in the Sankey diagram: technological (AI capability, data quality, system integration), organisational (leadership support, organisational culture, change management), environmental (market trends, regulatory environment, competitive pressure) and individual (user attitudes, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness) (Sect. 3.2, p. 29, Fig. 2).
- Twelve named factors are discussed: technological readiness and infrastructure; perceived benefits and ROI; organisational culture and leadership; employee attitudes and skills; ethical considerations and trust; regulatory environment; cost and resource allocation; data quality and availability; customer expectations and market trends; competitive pressure; integration with existing systems; and strategic alignment (Sect. 3.2, pp. 27-30).
- Table 1 catalogues challenges and barriers under nine categories — Technical, Economic, Organizational, Ethical and Legal, Strategic, Operational, User-Centric, Regulatory, Cultural and Environmental — with rows numbered 1 to 18 (Table 1, pp. 31-32).
- Table 2 catalogues strategies under rows numbered 1 to 15, each with a description, key stakeholders involved, implementation steps and potential challenges (Table 2, pp. 34-37).
- The paper claims that in business management AI improves decision-making through predictive analytics, NLP, chatbots and RPA; that in finance it supports risk management, fraud detection, algorithmic trading, robo-advisors and financial forecasting; and that in e-commerce it drives personalised recommendations, chatbots, supply-chain optimisation, dynamic pricing, visual search and fraud prevention (Sect. 3.5, pp. 38-39; Sect. 3.6, pp. 39-40).
- Emerging technologies named as likely to further reshape acceptance are Explainable AI (XAI), AI combined with blockchain, Edge AI, and AI-driven augmented and virtual reality (Sect. 3.6, p. 40).
- The paper asserts repeatedly that AI delivers efficiency, accuracy, security, personalisation and ROI, but attaches no measurement, dataset or study to any of these claims.

## Limitations and Gaps

- The paper reports no sample size, no number of documents screened or included, no search strings, no search date, no inclusion/exclusion counts and no PRISMA-style flow, so the review's corpus cannot be reconstructed or replicated (Sect. 2, pp. 24-25). [unacknowledged]
- The methodology promises keyword co-occurrence and cluster analysis with VOSviewer, hierarchical clustering and k-means clustering (Sect. 2, pp. 24-25), but the results section reports only a qualitative description of the network diagram and its four clusters — no cluster counts, no link strengths, no k value, no clustering statistics and no VOSviewer version appear anywhere (Sect. 3.1, pp. 25-27). [unacknowledged]
- The paper reports no outcome statistics of its own: no frequencies, percentages, means, confidence intervals, test statistics or p-values for any claim about AI acceptance, adoption, benefits or barriers. [unacknowledged]
- The claim that AI enhances accuracy, efficiency, security and ROI in finance and e-commerce (Sect. 3.5, pp. 38-39; Sect. 3.6, pp. 39-40) is asserted without any cited measurement, dataset or evaluation, so the paper cannot support a causal or quantitative claim about AI's effects. [unacknowledged]
- The paper states no study-level limitations section of its own: Sect. 3.7 Conclusions discusses challenges to AI acceptance in the field but never acknowledges the review's own methodological constraints. [unacknowledged]
- Because the design is a narrative review with keyword analysis and no empirical component, the paper cannot support claims about which factors cause acceptance, which strategies work, or for whom — it can only report what the reviewed literature discusses. [unacknowledged]
- The paper gives no operational definition of "acceptance" and no measurement instrument, so the construct is used loosely across the review and is not comparable to instrument-based acceptance research. [unacknowledged]
- The paper treats business management, finance and e-commerce as parallel domains and draws cross-domain conclusions without testing whether factors, challenges or strategies differ by sector; the four clusters in Fig. 1 are not tested for sector-specific structure. [unacknowledged]
- The author list and the paper's own reference list contain a substantial number of self-citations to the same author team (e.g. references 28, 29, 30, 38, 41, 43, 47, 50, 53, 54, 55, 61, 62, 63, 64), which the paper does not acknowledge as a potential source of framing bias. [unacknowledged]
- The paper names Table 1 and Table 2 as catalogues but provides no empirical basis for the categories, no expert validation and no inter-rater reliability, so the taxonomies are the authors' synthesis rather than a tested classification. [unacknowledged]
- The paper's sector conclusions rely on narrative assertion rather than on the keyword analysis: the clusters in Fig. 1 are described in four paragraphs and then the paper moves to unclustered thematic discussion, so the bibliometric evidence does not carry the argument. [unacknowledged]
- No country, region or market is specified for the review's evidence base, so the paper cannot speak to any particular national context or regulatory regime, including the Philippine context. [unacknowledged]

## Definitions

- **AI acceptance** — the adoption and integration of AI technologies by organisations and their users; the paper's central construct, used without an operational definition or measurement instrument.
- **Explainable AI (XAI)** — techniques that make AI decisions more understandable to humans, named as crucial for trust and compliance in regulated industries such as finance (Sect. 3.4, p. 33; Sect. 3.6, p. 40).
- **Robotic Process Automation (RPA)** — AI-powered automation of routine tasks such as data entry, invoice processing and payroll management (Sect. 3.5, p. 38; Sect. 3.6, p. 39).
- **Natural Language Processing (NLP)** — AI that enables computers to understand, interpret and respond to human language, used for sentiment analysis, chatbots and virtual assistants (Sect. 3.5, p. 38).
- **Robo-advisors** — AI platforms that provide personalised investment advice and portfolio management services based on individual financial goals, risk tolerance and market conditions (Sect. 3.5, p. 38; Sect. 3.6, p. 39).
- **General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)** — the data privacy law named repeatedly as a compliance constraint on AI adoption in finance and e-commerce (Sect. 3.2, p. 28; Sect. 3.3, p. 31; Sect. 3.4, p. 37).
- **AML** — anti-money-laundering compliance, named alongside GDPR as a regulatory expectation in finance (Sect. 3.4, p. 37).
- **VOSviewer** — the text-mining and visualisation tool used for keyword extraction, categorisation and co-occurrence network construction (Sect. 2, pp. 24-25).
- **Co-occurrence analysis** — counting how often keywords appear together within the same documents to identify links between topics, visualised as a network (Sect. 2, p. 25).
- **Cluster analysis** — grouping keyword clusters into broader themes using hierarchical clustering and k-means (Sect. 2, p. 25).
- **Sankey diagram** — the flow-diagram form used in Fig. 2 to depict factors flowing into AI acceptance (Sect. 3.2, p. 29).
- **Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs)** — firms named as finding AI implementation costs potentially prohibitive (Sect. 3.3, p. 31).
- **Edge AI** — processing data locally on devices rather than on centralised cloud servers, reducing latency and enhancing privacy (Sect. 3.6, p. 40).
- **AI–blockchain integration** — combining AI with blockchain for transaction security, transparency and fraud reduction in finance and supply chains (Sect. 3.6, p. 40).
- **Augmented reality (AR) / virtual reality (VR)** — immersive technologies named as future e-commerce shopping interfaces that can reduce return rates (Sect. 3.6, p. 40).

## Remember This

- This is a narrative literature review of AI acceptance in business management, finance and e-commerce, not an empirical study; it reports no sample, no outcome statistics and no measurements.
- Method is keyword extraction, keyword co-occurrence and cluster analysis with VOSviewer, hierarchical clustering and k-means, but the results report only four qualitative clusters and no clustering statistics.
- The organising model is Fig. 2's four factor categories: technological, organisational, environmental and individual.
- Table 1 catalogues barriers in rows numbered 1–18; Table 2 catalogues strategies in rows numbered 1–15; both are qualitative and unevaluated.
- The closest fit to the project's taxonomy is the paper's description of AI-driven personal financial services — robo-advisors, personalised investment advice and financial forecasting — which supports `pfm_apps_overview` and `pfm_apps_features`, not the algorithms, methodology or evaluation modules.
- The paper cannot support any quantitative claim about AI acceptance, adoption, benefits or strategy effectiveness.
