# A Bibliometric Analysis of How Financial Behaviour Drives Effective Debt Management

`I--Samli-2026` — Samli (2026), *Labuan Bulletin of International Business & Finance, 24(1)* \[doi unavailable]

## Summary

A bibliometric synthesis of 136 Scopus documents (1987-May 2026) reports output peaking at 12 papers in 2024 and 2025, keyword co-occurrence centred on personal finance and financial literacy, and collaboration concentrated in the United States and United Kingdom.

## Problem and Motivation

Rising household indebtedness, wider access to credit and increasingly complex financial products have made the relationship between financial behaviour and effective debt management a focal point of contemporary personal finance research (Sec. 1 Introduction, p. 115). The literature is nevertheless fragmented, with limited synthesis of the field's intellectual foundations and of the progression of emerging research trajectories (Abstract, p. 114). Effective debt management is argued not to be a function of income or access to resources alone but to be shaped by behavioural determinants, including financial literacy, self-efficacy, attitudes toward money and indebtedness, behavioural biases, social pressures and the ability to regulate emotions (Sec. 1, pp. 115-117).

## Method

**Design.** Bibliometric analysis (systematic mapping) paired with a narrative literature synthesis in the introduction; the authors name no formal design label beyond "bibliometric analysis" and describe the opening section itself as a literature review.
**Sample.** 136 peer-reviewed journal articles retrieved from Scopus for 1987 through May 2026 — 140 records obtained first, 136 retained after screening — drawn from one TITLE-ABS-KEY query on "personal debt management", restricted to English-language works in econometrics and finance, economics, psychology and decision sciences; unit: one scholarly publication. The abstract names "personal debt management", "financial behaviour" and "effective measures" as three core keywords, but only the first appears in the Table 1 search string.
**Context.** geography: global scholarly output, mapped by country-level co-authorship and dominated by the United States and United Kingdom (Sec. 6.4, pp. 124-125); population: Not applicable — the unit of analysis is the published article, not a human sample; setting: bibliometric computation on Scopus records exported in Plaintext format and analysed in VOSviewer 1.6.20 alongside Scopus Analyzer.

- Frame four research questions covering the publication trajectory from 1987 to May 2026, the ten most cited articles, keyword thematic clusters and country-level co-authorship structure (Sec. 2 Research Questions, p. 117).
- Restrict inclusion to peer-reviewed journal articles, deliberately omitting books and lecture-based materials, and give priority to high-impact works for shaping the field's theoretical foundation (Sec. 3 Methodology, p. 118).
- Query Scopus through the TITLE-ABS-KEY field on "personal debt management" and apply subject-area filters covering economics, psychology, decision sciences, business, sociology and multidisciplinary domains (Table 1, p. 119; Sec. 4, p. 118).
- Screen the 140 retrieved records on language (English only) and on disciplinary area (econometrics and finance, economics, psychology, decision sciences), excluding everything else (Sec. 4, pp. 118-119; Table 2, p. 119).
- Export publication year, article title, author information, source journals, citation frequencies and keywords in Plaintext format from Scopus for the 1987 to May 2026 window (Sec. 5 Data Analysis, p. 119).
- Build bibliometric networks in VOSviewer 1.6.20 using VOS mapping and clustering, which locates items in reduced-dimensional space so that inter-item distances correspond to their level of relatedness (Sec. 5, pp. 119-120).
- Normalise co-occurrence data with the Association Strength measure AS_ij = C_ij/(w_i w_j), which the paper contrasts with Multidimensional Scaling (Sec. 5, p. 120; Eq. 1).
- Compute standard descriptive indicators from Scopus Analyzer — publication venues, chronological patterns and prominent contributors — alongside co-citation analysis of documents (Sec. 3 Methodology, p. 118).
- Tabulate annual output as counts and percentages from 1987 to 2026 to answer RQ1, then rank the ten most cited articles by author, title, source journal, year and citation count to answer RQ2 (Table 3, pp. 120-121; Table 4, pp. 121-122).
- Map keyword co-occurrence and country-level co-authorship networks to answer RQ3 and RQ4, interpreting clusters as evidence of a micro-level, capability-driven view of the field (Figures 2-3, pp. 123-124; Sec. 6.3-6.4, pp. 123-125).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The authors acknowledge that the analysis was confined to a single database and relied exclusively on a limited set of keywords, which may have constrained the breadth of relevant literature captured, and they call for multiple databases, expanded keyword combinations and additional analytical techniques (Sec. 7 Conclusion, p. 125).
- [unacknowledged] The keyword set described in the abstract — "personal debt management", "financial behaviour" and "effective measures" — does not match the documented strategy: Table 1's TITLE-ABS-KEY query contains only "personal debt management", so two of the three advertised core keywords never enter the retrieval (Abstract, p. 114; Table 1, p. 119).
- [unacknowledged] Sec. 3 (p. 118) states that the methodology incorporates "co-citation analysis of documents", but no co-citation result is reported anywhere; the four results subsections (pp. 120-125) present only the publication trajectory, the most-cited ranking, keyword co-occurrence and country co-authorship.
- [unacknowledged] The publication-trajectory subsection (p. 120) characterises 1987-2009 as "sparse and irregular output, with only isolated publications per year, each contributing less than 1% of the total dataset", but Table 3 records 4 papers (2.941176%) in 2006, 3 (2.205882%) in 2005 and 2 (1.470588%) in 2007.
- [unacknowledged] Saving is never analysed as an outcome: "saving" occurs only inside the paper's opening definition of financial behaviour, and neither the keyword network nor the most-cited table reports any saving-behaviour result (Sec. 1, p. 115; Figures 2-3, pp. 123-124).
- [unacknowledged] Table 4's column alignment is ambiguous and duplicated in the conversion, which repeats "O'Neill et al. (2005)" and inserts a 2010 year among otherwise descending rows, so the mapping of the citation counts 311 to 114 onto authors, titles and journals cannot be reconstructed (Table 4, pp. 121-122).
- [unacknowledged] Figures 1, 2 and 3 are reported without tabulated network statistics beyond occurrence counts, total link strength, document counts and citation counts; cluster membership, cluster sizes, silhouette values and the full country list are Not reported (Figures 1-3, pp. 120-124).
- [unacknowledged] The introduction is written as a narrative literature review citing specific empirical studies, while the 136-document bibliometric corpus is never cross-referenced with it, so no claim in the narrative synthesis is tied to a mapped record (Sec. 1, pp. 115-117; Table 3, pp. 120-121).
- [unacknowledged] No assessment of external validity is made: the paper performs no re-analysis on a second database or in another country, although the collaboration network it maps is dominated by two Anglo-Saxon systems (the country co-authorship subsection, pp. 124-125; Sec. 7, p. 125).
- [unacknowledged] No effect sizes, confidence intervals, significance tests or per-study sample sizes are reported for any of the behavioural findings the introduction synthesises, because those findings are inherited from cited work rather than computed here (Sec. 1, pp. 115-117).

## Definitions

- **Financial behavior** — "the attitudes, knowledge, skills, and psychological traits that shape how individuals and organizations make decisions about borrowing, spending, saving, and repaying debts" (Sec. 1, p. 115).
- **Effective debt management** — not merely a function of income or access to resources, but something "deeply shaped by behavioural determinants, including perceived self-efficacy, financial literacy, social pressures, attitudes toward money and indebtedness, as well as the ability to regulate emotions" (Sec. 1, p. 115).
- **Financial literacy** — "the competencies and understanding necessary to make well-informed personal financial decisions", treated as both a direct driver of debt management and an enabler of other positive financial behaviours (Sec. 1, p. 115).
- **Financial self-efficacy** — "an individual's confidence in their capacity to competently handle financial matters" (Sec. 1, p. 116).
- **Debt literacy** — the debt-specific knowledge distinguished from broad financial literacy in the evidence that households with both managed obligations better under stress (Sec. 1, p. 117).
- **Behavioural biases** — impulsivity, overconfidence, mental accounting errors, procrastination and availability bias, named as significant obstacles to effective debt management (Sec. 1, p. 116).
- **Availability bias** — "the tendency to focus on recent or salient information rather than long-term consequences", established to negatively influence credit card repayment behaviour (Sec. 1, p. 116).
- **Mental accounting** — earmarking funds for specific purposes, linked to lower indebtedness when used judiciously but harmful when it encourages compartmentalization without holistic oversight (Sec. 1, p. 116).
- **Financial socialization** — parental guidance during childhood, identified as a mechanism transmitting money attitudes across generations and shaping later repayment behaviour (Sec. 1, p. 117).
- **VOS mapping** — a technique that "locates items within a reduced-dimensional space in such a way that the inter-item distances correspond to their level of relatedness" (Sec. 5, pp. 119-120).
- **Association Strength (AS_ij)** — "proportional to the ratio between the empirically observed co-occurrence frequency of items i and j and the expected frequency of their co-occurrence under an assumption of statistical independence" (Sec. 5, p. 120).
- **Total link strength (TLS)** — the strength figure reported alongside each country's publication and citation counts, treated by the paper as a marker of international collaboration reach (Sec. 6.4, p. 124).

## Key Equations

- `AS_ij = C_ij / (w_i w_j)` — observed co-occurrence of items i and j over their expected co-occurrence under independence (Eq. 1, p. 120).
