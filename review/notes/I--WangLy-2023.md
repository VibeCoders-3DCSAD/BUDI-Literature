# I--WangLy-2023 — How Income Volatility Influences Saving Decisions: Evidence from the Lab

## Summary

Four incentivised online experiments (N = 712) test whether receiving a more volatile income changes how much people save. Participants completed a novel financial decision making task in which they performed 15 work tasks (rearranging six-character letter strings into alphabetical order) for hypothetical income, decided each round how much of their accumulated balance to spend on points, and then faced an unannounced financial emergency (home repairs costing £4,500) that wiped out their points if their savings were inadequate. Income volatility was manipulated between subjects across four coefficient-of-variation (CV) levels — 0.00 (No Volatility), 0.30 (Low), 0.37 (three Experiment 2 conditions), 0.60 (High), and 0.90 (Very High) — with total income held constant at £15,000 in every condition.

The headline result is that higher CV is associated with higher final savings. Pooling across all four experiments and treating CV as a continuous measure, the authors report very strong evidence that a higher CV predicts more saving (BF10 = 45.89), with a median posterior estimate of £2,134.69 per unit of CV, implying roughly £213 more saved per 0.1 increase in CV (95% HDI [£92, £328]). The abstract states this as "£213 more saved per 0.1 increase in CV".

The second headline result is a threshold. Saving behaviour did not differ from the stable-income baseline at CV = 0.30 or CV = 0.37, differed inconsistently at CV = 0.60 (higher savings in Experiment 1, but no difference in Experiment 3), and was highest at CV = 0.90. The authors conclude that a volatility threshold of roughly CV = 0.60 must be exceeded before saving behaviour moves.

A persistent anomaly runs through the paper: participants' self-reported perceived volatility ratings did not track their saving behaviour. Participants in the Low (CV = 0.30) and High (CV = 0.60) Volatility conditions of Experiment 1 rated their income as similarly volatile (M = 6.00 vs M = 6.40, BF01 = 3.15) despite saving different amounts. Experiment 2 ruled out minimum income, maximum income and range as alternative drivers. Experiment 3 attributed the discrepancy to the absolute framing of the rating scale and found only anecdotal within-participant sensitivity (BF10 = 2.91, mean difference 0.43). The paper also reports a null effect of income volatility on financial impatience, contrary to the two prior experimental studies that motivated it.

## Problem and Motivation

Income volatility is becoming more common: permanent full-time employment has declined in Australia, insecure part-time work has become prevalent, and platform or "gig" work has risen sharply (Sect. 1, p. 3). Fluctuating income has been linked to psychological depression, higher mortality risk, greater divorce risk and increased mortgage default (Sect. 1, p. 3). Most of this evidence concerns inter-year volatility; intra-year volatility is comparatively under-studied (Sect. 1, p. 3).

The existing intra-year evidence base is almost entirely observational, which limits causal inference (Sect. 1, p. 3). Only two experimental studies existed at the time of writing, and both found that volatility increased financial impatience: West et al. (2020) gave Kenyan participants income spikes via unconditional cash transfers and found more smaller-sooner choices; Peetz et al. (2021) gave participants volatile hypothetical income in a lab task and found a higher likelihood of choosing USD 15 today over USD 17 in two weeks (Sect. 1, pp. 3–4).

The paper focuses on saving rather than impatience for two reasons. First, income uncertainty is more severe for people in poorer financial circumstances, for whom accumulating savings and creating "financial slack" is especially critical for meeting unexpected expenses and avoiding debt (Sect. 1, p. 4). Second, the literature is genuinely split: the traditional precautionary-saving view (Drèze & Modigliani, 1972; Leland, 1968; Sandmo, 1970) predicts that uncertainty raises saving, while competing accounts predict the opposite through financial impatience, stress and scarcity, or a rational reallocation of attention to better-defined short-term outcomes (Sect. 1, pp. 4–5). The authors frame the contribution as threefold: expanding experimental work on income volatility and financial decisions, examining both the extensive and intensive margins of the volatility effect, and offering a reusable task (Sect. 2, pp. 5–6).

## Method

**Design.** Four incentivised online experiments using a novel financial decision making task; Experiments 1, 2 and 4 manipulate income volatility between subjects, Experiment 3 manipulates it within subject across two counterbalanced attempts. The paper describes the set as "four incentivised online experiments" (Abstract, p. 2) and as "four laboratory-based experiments" (Sect. 2, p. 5), and states no formal design label beyond that.

**Sample.** N = 712 participants in total across the four experiments (Experiment 1: 241; Experiment 2: 241; Experiment 3: 150; Experiment 4: 80); the unit of analysis is the individual participant. Experiment 1 comprised No Volatility (n = 81), Low Volatility (n = 80) and High Volatility (n = 80). Experiment 2 comprised Same Range (n = 80), Same Min (n = 81) and Same Max (n = 80). Experiment 3 comprised Low to High (n = 75) and High to Low (n = 75). Experiment 4 comprised a single Very High Volatility condition (n = 80). Sample sizes were set by a priori power analysis in G*Power targeting a small effect (𝛿 = .80; 𝛾 = .20; 𝛼 = .05), with the note that Experiments 1 and 2 each recruited one participant more than intended (Sect. 3.1.1, p. 6).

**Context.** geography: United Kingdom (participants recruited via Prolific Academic and required to be located in the UK); population: adults aged 18–65, fluent in English, with prior participants excluded from later experiments; setting: online incentivised experiments in which income and the financial emergency were hypothetical but participation and bonus payments were real, with bonus eligibility determined by being in the top 10% of scores within one's experimental condition.

The financial decision making task proceeded as follows. Each of 15 rounds required participants to rearrange a six-character string of letters (e.g., "hksykjl") into alphabetical order. On completion they received income and were asked how much they wanted to spend. Money could be spent to purchase points at £1.00 = 1 point; points determined eligibility for the bonus performance-based payment. Any unspent money remained as savings and carried over into subsequent rounds, where it could again be spent or saved (Sect. 3.1.2, p. 7).

Income sequences differed by condition but always totalled £15,000 across the 15 rounds. In the No Volatility condition participants received £1,000 per task (CV = 0). In the Low Volatility condition income ranged from £431 to £1,340 (CV = 0.30). In the High Volatility condition income ranged from £173 to £2,088 (CV = 0.60). The order in which the different incomes arrived was randomised for the Low and High Volatility conditions (Sect. 3.1.2, p. 7). Experiment 2 added three sequences all with CV = 0.37: Same Range (£173–£2,088, range £1,915, matching High Volatility's minimum, maximum and range), Same Min (£173–£1,580, range £1,407, matching High Volatility's minimum only), and Same Max (£681–£2,088, range £1,407, matching High Volatility's maximum only) (Sect. 4.1.2, pp. 13–14). Experiment 4 used a Very High Volatility sequence ranging from £44 to £3,457 with CV = 0.90 (Sect. 6.1.2, p. 22).

After the 15th round, participants encountered a financial emergency: home repairs costing £4,500 resulting from storm damage. If savings were adequate they "won" and kept their purchased points; if not, they lost all purchased points. Participants were told at the outset that an emergency would occur but not when or how much it would cost (Sect. 3.1.2, p. 7). In Experiment 3, the first emergency was the same £4,500 home repair and the second was a car breakdown costing £2,500 (Sect. 5.1.2, p. 18, footnote 9).

Just before the emergency cost was revealed — deliberately timed so responses would not be contaminated by the outcome — participants rated how volatile they perceived their income to have been (0 = "Not Volatile"; 10 = "Extremely volatile"), rated how difficult it was to decide how much to spend or save each round (0 = "Not difficult"; 10 = "Extremely difficult"), and completed a financial impatience measure using the fill-in-the-blank approach: the lowest immediate amount they would accept in place of £50 in two weeks, bounded between £0 and £50, with a lower amount indicating greater impatience (Sect. 3.1.2, p. 8).

The procedure began with instructions, then a practice stage of three rounds at £50 income per task plus a practice emergency (£50 parking ticket fine). Savings and points were then reset to zero and the 15 experimental rounds began. After the rounds, the perceived volatility, perceived difficulty and financial impatience measures were collected, the emergency cost was revealed, the outcome was reported, and demographics and financial situation questions were answered before debrief (Sect. 3.1.3, p. 8).

In Experiment 3 the procedure was adapted for two attempts. At the end of the first attempt, participants were told the emergency had occurred but the cost was not revealed until both attempts were complete. Participants were explicitly told the timing and cost of the emergency might differ between attempts. Scores were visually reset to zero between attempts, and the final score combined both attempts for bonus eligibility (Sect. 5.1.2, p. 18). This experiment was preregistered on AsPredicted (Sect. 5, p. 17, footnote 7).

All analyses were Bayesian with default priors, reporting BF10 or its inverse BF01, interpreted against Lee and Wagenmakers (2013). ANOVAs tested overall differences across conditions and t-tests tested pairwise comparisons unless stated otherwise (Sect. 3.2, p. 8). Data and code are available at https://osf.io/pqgha/ (Sect. 3.2, p. 9).

## Key Findings

- num: Pooled across all four experiments, higher CV was associated with more savings with very strong evidence (BF10 = 45.89), with a median posterior CV effect of £2,134.69 and a 0.1 CV increase corresponding to roughly £213 more saved (95% HDI [£92, £328]) (Sect. 6.2.2, p. 24).
- num: In Experiment 1, final savings differed between conditions with very strong evidence (BF10 = 38.71). High Volatility saved M = £8,571.64 against No Volatility M = £6,647.88 (BF10 = 33.67) and Low Volatility M = £6,579.54 (BF10 = 24.23). No and Low Volatility did not differ (BF01 = 5.84) (Sect. 3.2.2, p. 9).
- num: In Experiment 1, perceived volatility ratings differed between conditions with extreme evidence (BF10 = 6.72×10^13), driven by lower ratings in No Volatility (M = 3.17) versus Low (M = 6.00) and High (M = 6.40). Low and High did not differ (BF01 = 3.15) — a discrepancy against the saving result (Sect. 3.2.1, p. 9).
- num: In Experiment 1, perceived difficulty did not differ between conditions (BF01 = 6.44; overall mean 4.07 out of 10), and financial impatience did not differ between conditions (BF01 = 7.67; mean smaller-sooner amount £37.60 against a £50 delayed reward) (Sect. 3.2.3, p. 10).
- num: Experiment 2 found no differences among the Same Range, Same Min and Same Max conditions on any measure: perceived volatility BF01 = 3.91, final savings BF01 = 16.19, perceived difficulty BF01 = 6.82, financial impatience BF01 = 12.50 (Sect. 4.2.1, p. 14).
- num: Experiment 2 conditions all saved less than Experiment 1's High Volatility condition: Same Range M = £7,096.65 (BF10 = 3.03), Same Max M = £6,842.40 (BF10 = 6.69), Same Min M = £7,362.69 (BF10 = 1.16) (Sect. 4.2.2, p. 16).
- num: In Experiment 3, participants saved substantially more on their second attempt regardless of volatility level, with extreme evidence for a task attempt effect (BF10 = 5.01×10^6); the volatility level effect was contradicted (BF01 = 3.36) and the interaction was anecdotal against (BF01 = 2.11) (Sect. 5.2.2, p. 20).
- num: Experiment 3's High Volatility first attempt produced lower final savings (M = £6,834.69) than Experiment 1's High Volatility condition (M = £8,571.64) with moderate evidence of a difference (BF10 = 5.50), while the perceived volatility ratings were similar (BF01 = 5.44) (Sect. 5.2.2, pp. 20–21).
- num: Experiment 4's Very High Volatility condition (CV = 0.90) produced higher perceived volatility (M = 6.79 vs M = 3.17) with extreme evidence (BF10 = 1.03×10^13) and higher final savings than No Volatility (M = £8,342.80 vs £6,647.88) with strong evidence (BF10 = 10.97) (Sect. 6.2.1, p. 23).
- num: Across all four experiments pooled, perceived volatility ratings differed between levels with extreme evidence (BF10 = 4.62×10^25), but excluding No Volatility the remaining conditions did not differ (BF01 = 1.41); final savings by volatility level were inconclusive (BF01 = 1.01) (Sect. 6.2.2, pp. 23–24).
- The paper proposes a volatility threshold: below CV = 0.60 saving behaviour did not differ from the stable baseline, at CV = 0.60 results were inconsistent across experiments, and above CV = 0.60 savings were highest (Sect. 7.2, p. 25).
- The income volatility manipulation did not increase financial impatience, contrary to Peetz et al. (2021) and West et al. (2020). The authors suggest the effect may be small, that their hypothetical manipulation and hypothetical measure may both be weaker than the prior studies' designs, and that fill-in-the-blank measures produce shallower discounting than binary-choice tasks (Sect. 3.3, p. 12).
- The authors read the results as evidence of saving intention rather than realised behaviour, and warn that the task removed the short-term liquidity pressure that dominates real volatile-income households (Sect. 7.1, pp. 24–25).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The authors acknowledge that the task is a highly simplified version of the real spending–saving trade-off. Participants never had to worry about income being insufficient for immediate expenses because there were none in the task, so they could focus entirely on the long-term savings goal; in the real world, a drop in income may leave people unable to meet financial obligations (Sect. 7.1, p. 25).
- The authors acknowledge that the results may reflect saving intentions rather than realised saving behaviour — a gap between saving intentions and behaviours (Rabinovich & Webley, 2007) (Sect. 7.1, p. 24).
- The authors acknowledge that the effect of income volatility on saving may depend on financial situation: "The motivation to save more in response to a fluctuating income may be a luxury for those in strong financial positions who need not be concerned about it affecting their short-term liquidity" (Sect. 7.1, p. 25).
- The authors acknowledge the null financial impatience result and offer three possible explanations — hypothetical manipulation, hypothetical measure, and use of a fill-in-the-blank rather than binary-choice instrument — while stating that exploring these explanations was beyond the study's scope and that further investigation is needed (Sect. 3.3, p. 12).
- The authors acknowledge that future work should examine alternative volatility definitions such as the second-order coefficient of variation (Kvålseth, 2017), investigate whether other characteristics of income sequences explain perceived volatility, and distinguish volatility from predictability (e.g., seasonal work or annual bonuses) (Sect. 7.2, pp. 25–26).
- [unacknowledged] The saving results at CV = 0.60 are internally inconsistent and the paper does not resolve the contradiction: Experiment 1's High Volatility condition saved M = £8,571.64 with very strong evidence against the No and Low conditions (BF10 = 33.67 and 24.23), while Experiment 3's High Volatility first attempt saved M = £6,834.69 and differed from Experiment 1's High Volatility condition with moderate evidence (BF10 = 5.50). Both numbers are reported, at Sect. 3.2.2 p. 9 and Sect. 5.2.2 p. 21 respectively.
- [unacknowledged] The pooled analysis across all four experiments deliberately forgoes random assignment of participants to conditions. The authors defend this on the grounds that the experiments drew from the same participant pool, offered similar incentives, and were conducted within months of each other (Sect. 6.2, p. 23, footnote 11), but the pooled estimate of £213 per 0.1 CV is not protected by randomisation in the way the individual experiments are.
- [unacknowledged] Participants were UK-based Prolific Academic respondents aged 18–65 who are fluent in English and, in Experiments 1 to 3, majority degree-holding (Table A1, p. 35). The threshold estimate of CV ≈ 0.60 and the £213-per-0.1-CV slope are therefore specific to this online panel and are not shown to generalise to lower-income or non-UK populations, which the paper itself flags as the group for whom income uncertainty is most severe (Sect. 1, p. 4).
- [unacknowledged] All income and the financial emergency were hypothetical. Only participation payment and a top-10% bonus were real, and the bonus depended on points purchased, which puts the spending incentive in direct tension with the saving goal. The paper reports no manipulation check on how participants weighed the bonus against the emergency.
- [unacknowledged] The perceived volatility scale was an 11-point absolute judgment with a single verbal anchor at each end (0 = "Not Volatile"; 10 = "Extremely volatile") and defaulted to 5. Participants were required to move the slider to progress (Figure D6, p. 45). The paper attributes the perception–behaviour discrepancy to absolute framing but does not test an alternative explanation such as anchoring on the default value.
- [unacknowledged] Figure 4's caption states "Experiment 4" while the figure plots Experiment 3's two-attempt perceived volatility ratings against Experiment 1's Low and High Volatility conditions (Figure 4, p. 19), so the figure labelling does not match the experiment it describes.
- [unacknowledged] The paper's own reference to the income sequence dimensions table (Table 1, p. 12) shows that CV, minimum, maximum and range are perfectly confounded in Experiment 1. Experiment 2 was designed to break that confound but held CV constant at 0.37 across all three conditions, so it cannot separate CV from the dimensions it was meant to isolate at the CV = 0.60 level.

## Definitions

- **Income volatility** — fluctuation in income from pay to pay. The paper distinguishes inter-year volatility (between years) from intra-year volatility (within a year) and focuses on the latter (Sect. 1, p. 3).
- **Coefficient of variation (CV)** — standard deviation of an income sequence divided by its mean. Larger CV means more volatile income. Used because it is standardised and comparable across incomes differing in magnitude, frequency and timing (Sect. 3, p. 6).
- **Precautionary saving** — saving motivated by uncertainty about future earnings, such that more must be set aside to insure against a potential dip in future income (Sect. 1, p. 4).
- **Final savings** — the amount a participant had saved at the end of the task, after all 15 rounds and before the emergency cost was revealed (Sect. 3.2.2, p. 9).
- **Perceived volatility rating** — participant's self-reported rating of how volatile their income was during the task, on an 11-point scale from 0 = "Not Volatile" to 10 = "Extremely volatile" (Sect. 3.1.2, p. 8).
- **Perceived difficulty** — participant's rating of how difficult it was to decide how much to spend or save each round, on an 11-point scale from 0 = "Not difficult" to 10 = "Extremely difficult" (Sect. 3.1.2, p. 8).
- **Financial impatience** — measured via the fill-in-the-blank approach as the lowest immediate amount a participant would accept in place of £50 in two weeks; a lower smaller-sooner amount indicates greater impatience (Sect. 3.1.2, p. 8).
- **No / Low / High / Very High Volatility** — the paper's condition labels for CV = 0.00, 0.30, 0.60 and 0.90 respectively (Sect. 3.1.2, p. 7; Sect. 6.1.2, p. 22).
- **Medium Volatility** — the paper's pooled label for the three Experiment 2 conditions, all at CV = 0.37 (Sect. 6.2, p. 22).
- **Same Range / Same Min / Same Max** — Experiment 2 conditions that share the range, the minimum, or the maximum respectively with Experiment 1's High Volatility condition, all at CV = 0.37 (Sect. 4.1.2, pp. 13–14).
- **Inclusion factor** — computed by comparing the posterior odds of models that include and exclude a predictor of interest, used to quantify evidence for whether a predictor should be included (Sect. 5.2.2, p. 20, footnote 10).

## Remember This

- Four incentivised online experiments (N = 712) manipulate income volatility via the coefficient of variation of hypothetical pay sequences in a novel spending-versus-saving task, with a £4,500 unannounced emergency at the end of 15 rounds.
- Pooled across experiments, higher CV predicts more saving (BF10 = 45.89; median posterior £2,134.69 per unit CV; ~£213 more saved per 0.1 CV, 95% HDI [£92, £328]).
- Saving behaviour did not move below CV = 0.30–0.37, moved inconsistently at CV = 0.60, and was highest at CV = 0.90 — the paper's proposed volatility threshold sits near CV = 0.60.
- The results contradict themselves at CV = 0.60: Experiment 1 High Volatility saved M = £8,571.64 (BF10 = 33.67 vs No Volatility) while Experiment 3's High Volatility first attempt saved M = £6,834.69 and differed from Experiment 1's High Volatility with BF10 = 5.50. Both are reported.
- Perceived volatility ratings failed to track saving behaviour throughout: Low and High Volatility rated their income as similarly volatile (M = 6.00 vs M = 6.40, BF01 = 3.15) despite different saving.
- Experiment 2 ruled out minimum income, maximum income and range as drivers by holding CV at 0.37 while varying those dimensions.
- The income volatility manipulation produced no detectable effect on financial impatience (BF01 = 7.67), contrary to Peetz et al. (2021) and West et al. (2020).
- Experiment 3 found extreme evidence for a task attempt effect (BF10 = 5.01×10^6): second attempts saved more regardless of volatility, so only first attempts were pooled into the combined analysis.
