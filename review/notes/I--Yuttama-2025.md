# I--Yuttama-2025 — Behavioral Shifts in Digital Finance: How E-Payment Influences Consumer Spending and Financial Literacy

## Summary

A survey-based PLS-SEM study of 400 active e-payment users in Central Java, Indonesia, testing whether e-payment usage raises consumer spending and whether it improves cash management, with financial literacy entered as a moderator of both paths. The paper's central claim is a "dual" effect: e-payment lowers the psychological pain of paying and so increases spending frequency and impulsivity (path coefficient 0.731, p 0.000), while the automated records, categorised transaction histories and dashboards embedded in the same systems improve monitoring, budgeting and planning (path coefficient 0.493, p 0.000). Financial literacy is positioned as a boundary condition: it weakens the e-payment-to-spending path (interaction -0.082, p 0.000) and is reported in the text as strengthening the e-payment-to-cash-management path (interaction -0.065, p 0.005). The study is cross-sectional, self-reported, single-region and correlational, and the paper describes it throughout in causal language. Two internal inconsistencies are recorded without reconciliation below: the sign of the second interaction against the prose that describes it, and an age-frequency block in Table 2 that sums to 410 respondents against a stated N of 400.

## Problem and Motivation

The paper opens from a macro observation: global payment systems are shifting from cash to digital platforms, and governments and financial institutions are actively promoting digital payment adoption for financial inclusion and economic efficiency (Introduction, p. 358). Indonesia is presented as a case of rapid adoption, and Central Java specifically as a region where e-wallet and mobile-banking use has surged among young consumers and small businesses (p. 358). The concern that motivates the study is stated plainly: while the transition offers convenience, transaction security and financial tracking, "concerns have emerged regarding its impact on consumer spending habits and financial management practices" (p. 359).

The literature is described as divided. One stream links digital payments to increased consumer spending because they reduce the psychological barriers associated with cash transactions, "leading to higher impulsivity and lower spending awareness" (Hampson et al., 2021, cited p. 359). Another stream holds that e-payment systems offer enhanced financial tracking tools that allow users to monitor expenses and manage cash flow effectively (Liu & Zhang, 2021, cited p. 359). The paper states that "the extent to which e-payment encourages excessive spending or improves financial discipline remains inconclusive, particularly in emerging markets like Indonesia" (p. 359).

The stated gap is that prior work treated e-payment as a direct, uniform driver of spending or of cash management, whereas the paper argues the relationship is conditional on individual differences in financial literacy. Financial literacy is framed as "a moderating mechanism that determines how consumers interpret, evaluate, and respond to the convenience and automation of digital transactions" (p. 359). The paper's review section develops this as a "boundary-condition view": linear models cannot explain why e-payment produces both overspending and improved financial discipline in different user groups (p. 361). Three specific gaps in prior work are named — Usman et al. (2025) did not assess impact on financial planning or savings behaviour; Laukkanen (2016) did not examine how digital payment behaviour affects personal cash management; and Liu & Zhang (2021) lacked empirical analysis of consumer financial habits in developing economies (p. 360). The paper's contribution claim is that it is "the first study to comprehensively examine the dual role of e-payment usage in shaping consumer behavior and cash management, while also investigating financial literacy as a moderating factor" (p. 368).

## Method

**Design.** Quantitative, cross-sectional, survey-based study using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling (PLS-SEM) with moderation analysis, conducted under a positivist paradigm; the paper labels the design "a quantitative research approach" and "a survey-based research design" and states no further design label (Research Design, p. 363).
**Sample.** N = 400 respondents (151 male, 249 female), unit of analysis = the individual active e-payment user; a separate pre-test of 67 respondents preceded the full survey (Table 2, p. 365; Pre-Test and Instrument Validation, p. 364).
**Context.** geography: Central Java, Indonesia (with Semarang named in the market context); population: residents of Central Java aged at least 18 who actively use e-payment services including mobile banking, e-wallets or digital payment platforms, recruited by purposive sampling; setting: structured self-administered questionnaire using five-point Likert items, analysed in SmartPLS 4 with bootstrapping at 5,000 resamples.

Procedural detail the paper reports:

- Sample size was derived from Slovin's formula with a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05), yielding "the estimated sample size for this study was 400 respondents" (Population and Sampling, p. 363).
- Eligibility required respondents to be at least 18 years old, "ensuring that they had financial decision-making autonomy", and to be active users of e-payment services (p. 363).
- Four constructs were measured with five items each: e-payment usage (X), consumer behaviour (Y1), cash management (Y2) and financial literacy (Z), all on a 1–5 Likert scale (Table 1, p. 364; Table 3, p. 366).
- Item wording is printed in full in Table 1 and is sourced to four prior studies: e-payment items from Ly & Ly (2024); consumer-behaviour items from Hampson et al. (2021); cash-management items from Liu & Zhang (2021); financial-literacy items from Laukkanen (2016) (p. 364).
- E-payment usage was operationalised as transaction frequency, convenience, security and promotional benefits; consumer behaviour as shopping frequency, impulsivity, spending awareness and online shopping accessibility; cash management as expense tracking, transaction monitoring, budgeting and spending control; financial literacy as understanding of e-payment risks, financial planning habits and budgeting discipline (p. 364).
- A pre-test with 67 respondents assessed clarity, reliability and validity, after which minor revisions were made; a Focus Group Discussion with financial experts and researchers refined ambiguous items (p. 364).
- Validity and reliability procedures are stated: convergent validity via AVE with a 0.50 threshold; discriminant validity via the Fornell-Larcker criterion and the HTMT ratio; reliability via Cronbach's alpha and composite reliability with a 0.70 threshold (p. 364).
- The outer (measurement) model was assessed through factor loadings; the inner (structural) model through path coefficients, R² and effect size f²; hypotheses were supported if the t-statistic exceeded 1.96 and p was below 0.05 under bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples (Data Analysis Technique, p. 365).
- The paper states the study "examine[s] the causal relationships between e-payment usage, consumer behavior, and cash management" (p. 363), although the design is a single-wave survey with no manipulation or temporal ordering.

## Software

- SmartPLS 4 (PLS-SEM estimation and bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples; Data Analysis Technique, p. 365)

## Key Findings

- E-payment usage has a significant positive path to consumer behaviour: original sample coefficient 0.731, t = 16.153, p = 0.000 (Table 6, p. 367). The paper interprets this as increased reliance on digital payment methods encouraging higher spending (p. 366).
- E-payment usage has a significant positive path to cash management: original sample coefficient 0.493, t = 9.597, p = 0.000 (Table 6, p. 367), interpreted as electronic transactions improving financial monitoring and tracking (p. 367).
- The financial-literacy × e-payment interaction on consumer behaviour is -0.082, t = 3.880, p = 0.000 (Table 6, p. 367); the paper reads this as financial literacy weakening the positive relationship between e-payment usage and consumer behaviour (p. 367).
- The financial-literacy × e-payment interaction on cash management is -0.065, t = 2.826, p = 0.005 (Table 6, p. 367). The Findings text describes this as financial literacy strengthening the relationship between e-payment usage and cash management (p. 367), and the Discussion repeats that financial literacy "strengthens the positive effect of e-payment on cash management" (p. 369), while H4 predicts the opposite ("attenuates", p. 363). The negative sign and the prose do not agree; both are recorded here.
- The structural model explains 87.7% of the variance in consumer behaviour (adjusted 0.876) and 83.1% of the variance in cash management (adjusted 0.830); the paper calls both "very strong explanatory power" against a 0.75 threshold (p. 366).
- Measurement quality is reported as adequate: Cronbach's alpha from 0.872 to 0.905, rho_a from 0.896 to 0.923, rho_c from 0.905 to 0.929, and AVE from 0.656 to 0.722 (Table 4, p. 366).
- Descriptive construct means are moderately high and closely clustered: consumer behaviour 3.97 (SD 0.97), cash management 3.83 (SD 0.93), e-payment usage 3.68 (SD 0.94) and financial literacy 3.46 (SD 1.04) on a 1–5 scale (Table 3, p. 366).
- The respondent pool is majority female (249, 62.4%) and concentrated in the 25–34 age band (195, 48.7%), with 181 respondents (45.2%) holding a bachelor's degree and 214 (53.5%) reporting monthly income of IDR 3–6 million (Table 2, p. 365).
- The paper argues the effects of e-payment are not uniform: financially literate users "are more likely to view digital transactions as part of a broader financial strategy" whereas less literate users "may perceive e-payment as a tool for convenience and consumption" (p. 368).
- A drawback is acknowledged in the Discussion: over-reliance on digital tracking may reduce manual financial discipline and create "a false sense of financial control" (p. 369).

## Definitions

- **E-payment (X)** — electronic payment systems including mobile banking, e-wallets and digital payment platforms; measured through transaction frequency, convenience, security and promotional benefits (pp. 363–364).
- **Consumer behaviour (Y1)** — spending propensity, measured as shopping frequency, impulsivity, spending awareness and online shopping accessibility (p. 364); the paper also calls it "spending propensity" (p. 366).
- **Cash management (Y2)** — expense tracking, transaction monitoring, budgeting and spending control (p. 364).
- **Financial literacy (Z)** — understanding of e-payment risks, financial planning habits and budgeting discipline (p. 364); also theorised as "a self-regulatory mechanism that enables individuals to interpret financial information, evaluate risks, and make informed digital transactions" (p. 361).
- **Boundary condition** — the paper's framing of financial literacy as the construct that "determines the strength and direction of e-payment's behavioral effects" (p. 361).
- **Behavioural Economics Theory** — the framework explaining how "reduced transaction friction and lower payment salience in digital systems can distort spending judgment and increase purchase frequency" (p. 361), drawing on Thaler & Sunstein (2008).
- **Financial Literacy Theory** — the framework holding that financial knowledge improves self-control, decision-making and long-term planning (Lusardi & Mitchell, 2014, cited p. 362).
- **PLS-SEM** — Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling, chosen for its capacity to handle latent constructs and moderating effects (p. 365).
- **AVE / HTMT / Fornell-Larcker** — the convergent and discriminant validity criteria the Methods state were applied, with thresholds of 0.50 and 0.70 respectively (p. 364).
- **QRIS** — Quick Response Code Indonesian Standard, the QR payment scheme whose Central Java growth the paper uses as its opening market evidence (p. 359).

## Key Equations

- Slovin's formula for sample size, printed as `η = N / (1 + N e²)`, where η is the required sample size, N the total population and e the margin of error; applied at a 95% confidence level to yield 400 respondents (Population and Sampling, p. 363). The conversion of the PDF renders the denominator ambiguously.

No structural or measurement equations are printed; the PLS-SEM results are given only as tabulated coefficients.

## Limitations and Gaps

Acknowledged by the authors:

- The geographical focus on Central Java restricts generalisability "to regions with different financial cultures and technological infrastructures" (Conclusion, p. 370).
- Reliance on self-reported data "may also introduce social desirability bias, leading to underreporting of impulsive behavior" (p. 370).
- The study concentrates on financial literacy as the only moderator, "excluding other behavioral and contextual variables such as income, digital literacy, and psychological biases" (p. 370).
- Future research is called for using cross-regional and longitudinal designs, and examining AI-based financial advisors, cashback mechanisms and gamified saving systems (p. 370).
- The paper also acknowledges, in the Discussion, that over-reliance on digital tracking may reduce manual financial discipline and create a false sense of control (p. 369).

[unacknowledged] findings from reading the paper:

- The sign and the prose contradict each other on the second moderation path. Table 6 (p. 367) prints the financial-literacy × e-payment → cash-management coefficient as -0.065, while the Findings text (p. 367), the Discussion (p. 369) and the Conclusion (p. 369) all describe financial literacy as *strengthening* the positive e-payment effect on cash management. H4 (p. 363) predicts attenuation. Both the printed coefficient and the printed prose are retained in this note; no reconciliation is offered.
- Table 2's age block does not sum to the stated sample. The frequencies 72 + 195 + 129 + 14 = 410 and the percentages 18.0 + 48.7 + 32.1 + 3.5 = 102.3, against a stated N of 400 and an education/income block that does sum to 400 (Table 2, p. 365; p. 363).
- The printed gender percentages do not match the printed frequencies over the stated N: 151/400 = 37.75% and 249/400 = 62.25%, printed as 37.6 and 62.4 (Table 2, p. 365).
- Three paths are reported as "p < 0.000" (pp. 366–367) and Table 6 prints the value as 0.000 (p. 367). A probability cannot be below zero; the printed value is retained here.
- The Methods promise outputs that are never reported: factor loadings are described as assessed but no loadings appear; discriminant validity is said to be examined "using the Fornell-Larcker criterion and the Heterotrait-Monotrait (HTMT) ratio" (p. 364) but neither matrix is printed; and effect size f² is said to have been calculated (p. 365) but no f² value appears anywhere.
- The design cannot support the causal claims the paper makes. The paper states it examines "causal relationships" (p. 363) and that e-payment usage "encourages higher spending" (p. 366), but the data are a single cross-sectional questionnaire wave with no manipulation, no temporal ordering and no instrumental variable, so direction of causality is unidentified.
- The pre-test's 67 respondents are never characterised, and the paper does not state whether they are excluded from, or included in, the 400 analysed (p. 364).
- All constructs are measured with same-source, same-instrument, same-occasion Likert items, so common-method variance is a live alternative explanation for the very large R² values (0.877 and 0.831, p. 366). The paper reports no common-method test.
- The cash-management construct contains the item "I feel that using e-payment helps me control my spending" (Table 1, p. 364) while consumer behaviour contains the item "I am more impulsive in purchasing goods when using e-payment" (p. 364). Both are self-reports of the same underlying behaviour, so the negative moderation result on one and the positive path on the other are partly a function of item framing rather than of distinct outcomes.
- No confidence interval is reported for any path coefficient, no exact p value is given other than the two decimals printed, and no per-item loading, discriminant validity or effect-size evidence accompanies the structural claims.
- The paper reports no objective transaction data. Every behavioural quantity is a Likert-scale perception, so the study cannot speak to actual spending levels, actual expense tracking or actual budgeting behaviour.
- No sampling frame or response rate is reported; purposive sampling of self-selected active e-payment users means the 400 respondents are not a probability sample of Central Java, and the generalisation claim on p. 363 ("enabling statistical generalization of the findings to a broader population") is unsupported by the sampling procedure described.
- The paper cites Bank Indonesia statistics (pp. 358–359) but reports no primary market data of its own, so the macro context and the individual-level findings are never linked.

## Remember This

- The study is a single-wave PLS-SEM survey of 400 Central Java e-payment users; every construct is a five-point Likert self-report, and nothing objective is measured.
- The headline result is a dual effect: e-payment raises consumer spending (0.731, p 0.000) and simultaneously improves cash management (0.493, p 0.000).
- Financial literacy weakens the spending path (-0.082, p 0.000) but its interaction with cash management is printed as -0.065 (p 0.005) while the prose calls that path *strengthened*; the sign and the sentence disagree and both are kept.
- Table 2's age frequencies sum to 410 against a stated N of 400, and its age percentages sum to 102.3.
- The paper promises factor loadings, Fornell-Larcker, HTMT and f² in Methods and never prints any of them.
- Model fit is high on paper — R² of 0.877 and 0.831, SRMR 0.035 saturated / 0.036 estimated, NFI 0.849 — but all of it rests on same-source, same-occasion perceptual items.
- The practical recommendation the paper lands on is in-app nudges: spend alerts, weekly summaries, category caps and goal tracking.
