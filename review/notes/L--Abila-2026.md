## Summary

This study investigates the financial management practices and financial resilience of online freelancers in Laguna, Philippines, within the context of the Digital Platform Taxation (DPT) policy that imposed a 12% value-added tax on digital services starting June 1, 2025. Guided by Carroll's Buffer Stock Theory, the study examines how Buffer Stock Savings (BSS), Perceived Income Volatility (PIV), and Liquid Asset Accessibility (LAA) influence freelancers' capacity to withstand financial shocks. A mixed-methods exploratory pilot design was employed: a structured survey administered to a purposive-stratified sample of 30 online freelancers and semi-structured interviews conducted with a criterion-purposive subsample of 10 participants. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Pearson's correlation, and multiple linear regression; qualitative data were analyzed using deductive thematic and content analysis. Results indicate moderate relationships among BSS, PIV, LAA, and overall financial resilience, with Liquid Asset Accessibility emerging as the only statistically significant positive predictor of resilience. Freelancers demonstrated adaptive but largely reactive financial strategies focused on short-term survival rather than long-term security. The study proposes a six-component financial literacy program to strengthen freelancers' financial stability and planning capabilities, with implications for policymakers and digital platforms.

The paper's contribution is a localized empirical account of how a new tax regime interacts with the already precarious financial lives of gig-economy workers. It documents that freelancers in Laguna exhibit high awareness of digital platform taxation but uneven preparedness for large-scale financial shocks, and that immediate access to liquid funds matters more for their resilience than savings accumulation alone. The authors position the work as an exploratory pilot, explicitly acknowledging that the small sample limits generalization and that the cross-sectional design cannot establish causal effects.

## Problem and Motivation

The expansion of the digital economy has reshaped employment structures worldwide, particularly through the rise of online freelancing. Digital platforms such as Upwork, OnlineJobs.ph, Fiverr, and similar marketplaces have enabled individuals to offer services beyond geographic boundaries, creating flexible income opportunities. However, while freelancing offers autonomy and accessibility, it also exposes workers to income instability, lack of formal employment protection, and limited access to traditional financial safety nets. These conditions make financial management and resilience critical concerns for freelancers, especially in developing economies such as the Philippines where social protection systems are often employment-based.

Starting June 1, 2025, the Philippine government introduced Digital Platform Taxation policies, including the imposition of value-added tax on digital services and subscriptions used by freelancers. While these regulations aim to broaden the tax base and ensure fiscal sustainability, they have introduced additional financial pressure on freelancers whose earnings are already irregular. Platform fees, subscription costs, and compliance requirements further reduce net income, intensifying the need for effective financial management strategies. Despite the growing relevance of this issue, empirical research on how digital taxation affects freelancers' financial resilience remains limited, particularly at the local or provincial level.

Existing research on financial resilience has largely focused on salaried workers, households, or micro-entrepreneurs, emphasizing savings behavior, access to liquid assets, and income stability as key determinants of resilience. Buffer Stock Theory suggests that individuals facing income uncertainty tend to accumulate precautionary savings to smooth consumption during adverse shocks. Related studies also highlight perceived income volatility as a psychological and behavioral factor influencing financial decision-making. However, there is a noticeable gap in the literature regarding how these concepts apply to online freelancers operating under digital platform taxation regimes, especially within the Philippine context. This study was conducted to address this gap by examining the financial management practices and financial resilience of online freelancers in Laguna amid the implementation of digital platform taxation, and to propose a financial literacy program tailored to their realities.

## Method

**Design.** Mixed-methods research design with a quantitative-dominant approach and an embedded qualitative component; the authors position the study as an exploratory pilot study (Section 2.3, p. 122).
**Sample.** N = 30 online freelancers for the quantitative survey (unit of analysis: individual freelancer); N = 10 for the qualitative interviews (criterion-purposive subsample drawn from the initial 30).
**Context.** geography: Laguna, Philippines, a province in the CALABARZON region; population: online freelancers engaged in project-based or task-based online work using platforms such as LinkedIn, Facebook freelancer communities, OnlineJobs.ph, and other local networks, with an estimated population of 1,903 individuals; setting: survey administered online from late September to October 2025, after the June 1, 2025 implementation of Digital Platform Taxation; data analyzed using SPSS.

- Use a mixed-methods design with a quantitative-dominant strand (structured survey questionnaire) and an embedded qualitative component (open-ended survey questions and optional documentary evidence such as income logs, receipts, and budgeting records).
- Recruit participants through purposive-stratified sampling for the quantitative phase, with eligibility requiring at least one year of freelancing experience and active use of at least one digital freelancing platform; use proportional allocation across platforms based on estimated population shares.
- Select 10 participants for the qualitative phase through purposive-criterion sampling, targeting those who exhibited inconsistent responses between reported financial practices and outcomes.
- Measure financial management constructs (Buffer Stock Savings, Perceived Income Volatility, Liquid Asset Accessibility) and financial resilience using 5-point semantic differential scales; generate composite scores for statistical analysis.
- Measure perceptions of digital platform taxation, including awareness of the taxation system, perceived effects on costs and take-home pay, and the extent to which VAT influenced budgeting and financial security.
- Collect demographic information (age, gender, freelancing niche, years of experience, income bracket) to support subgroup analysis.
- Validate the instrument through expert review by a freelance accounting taxation specialist and an academic expert with an MBA and a PhD in Information Technology Management; conduct a pilot test involving 15–30 freelancers to assess internal consistency using Cronbach's Alpha with α ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable.
- Analyze quantitative data using descriptive statistics (mean, frequency, standard deviation), Pearson's correlation to examine relationships among financial management variables and financial resilience, and multiple linear regression to determine predictive relationships.
- Analyze qualitative data using deductive thematic and content analysis based on predetermined categories (BSS, PIV, LAA, FR), with emerging themes also identified; use triangulation through open-ended responses and documentary evidence to enhance credibility.
- Interpret results using predefined scoring classifications: 1.00–1.80 = Very Low; 1.81–2.60 = Low; 2.61–3.40 = Moderate; 3.41–4.20 = High; 4.21–5.00 = Very High.
- Treat composite scores as continuous variables for parametric testing when distributional assumptions were satisfied; otherwise apply nonparametric measures.

## Key Findings

- num: The overall mean score for financial management practices was 3.47 (SD = 1.36), interpreted as high (Table 4, p. 125).
- num: Digital Platform Taxation awareness recorded the highest mean score among financial management variables (M = 3.81, SD = 1.25), followed by Buffer Stock Savings (M = 3.49, SD = 1.44) and Liquid Asset Accessibility (M = 3.47, SD = 1.39), while Perceived Income Volatility received the lowest score (M = 3.10, SD = 1.38) (Table 4, p. 125).
- num: Financial resilience was moderate, with an overall mean score of 3.19 (SD = 1.30); the highest resilience item was self-confidence in financial recovery (M = 3.81, SD = 1.18), while the lowest were medical expense preparedness (M = 2.85, SD = 1.16) and unexpected income loss preparedness (M = 2.88, SD = 1.30) (Table 5, p. 126).
- num: Buffer Stock Savings (r = 0.598, p < 0.01) and Liquid Asset Accessibility (r = 0.517, p < 0.01) showed moderate positive correlations with financial resilience; Perceived Income Volatility showed a negative correlation (r = -0.478, p < 0.01) (Section 3.3, p. 126).
- num: Regression analysis showed that 73% of variance in financial resilience (R² = 0.73, p < 0.01) could be explained by financial practices and demographic factors (Section 3.3, p. 126).
- num: Liquid Asset Accessibility was the only statistically significant positive predictor of financial resilience (β = 0.58, p = 0.04) (Section 3.3, p. 126).
- num: Male freelancers scored 1.13 points higher in resilience (p = 0.03), while freelancers with 5–7 years of experience showed lower resilience (-1.62, p = 0.01) (Section 3.3, p. 126).
- num: The estimated population of online freelancers in Laguna was 1,903 individuals, derived from LinkedIn (1000), Facebook freelancer communities (562), OnlineJobs.ph (329), and other local networks (12) (Table 1, p. 121).
- num: The quantitative sample of 30 was proportionally allocated across platforms: LinkedIn 16, Facebook 9, OnlineJobs.ph and other local networks 5 (Table 2, p. 122).
- The study distinguishes between taxation awareness (knowledge of the existence and general impact of VAT) and taxation literacy (the ability to compute taxes, identify deductible expenses, and comply with filing requirements); awareness was generally high but literacy gaps were observed (Section 3.2, p. 125).
- Freelancers demonstrated adaptive financial management through micro-saving strategies, primarily using digital wallets for emergency funds and short-term liquidity needs, but savings behavior was largely conditional on client workload and income flow, reflecting reactive rather than structured long-term planning (Section 3.1, p. 123).
- Only a small subset of participants submitted documentary evidence, indicating potential non-response bias (Section 3.1, p. 124).
- Freelancers reported income pressure due to irregular project availability and platform-related deductions such as VAT and service fees; many compensated by reducing discretionary expenses, increasing work load, or relying on family support during financial shortages (Section 3.1, p. 124).
- The proposed financial literacy program comprises six components: strengthening buffer stock savings under tax constraints; managing income volatility in the digital economy; enhancing liquidity and emergency fund access; improving tax literacy and recordkeeping; building financial resilience, adaptive confidence, and awareness of insurable risk; and promoting equitable and experience-based financial education (Table 6, pp. 127–128).

## Definitions

- **Buffer Stock Savings (BSS)** — Precautionary savings accumulated to smooth consumption during adverse income shocks, derived from Carroll's Buffer Stock Theory.
- **Perceived Income Volatility (PIV)** — The psychological and behavioral experience of income instability, treated as a factor influencing financial decision-making.
- **Liquid Asset Accessibility (LAA)** — The ease with which freelancers can access funds during emergencies, often through digital wallets and e-wallets.
- **Financial Resilience** — The perceived capacity to withstand and recover from financial shocks, operationalized in this study through BSS, PIV, and LAA.
- **Digital Platform Taxation (DPT)** — The Philippine government's policy, effective June 1, 2025, imposing a 12% value-added tax on digital services and subscriptions used by freelancers.
- **Taxation awareness vs. taxation literacy** — Awareness refers to knowledge of the existence and general impact of VAT; literacy involves the ability to compute taxes, identify deductible expenses, and comply with filing requirements (Section 3.2, p. 125).
- **Exploratory pilot study** — The authors' positioning of the study given the relatively small sample size, with inferential statistics used to identify potential trends and preliminary indicators rather than definitive population-wide parameters (Section 2.3, p. 122).
- **Purposive-stratified sampling** — The sampling design used for the quantitative phase, with proportional allocation across platforms based on estimated population shares (Section 2.3, p. 121).
- **Purposive-criterion sampling** — The sampling design used for the qualitative phase, selecting 10 participants who exhibited inconsistent responses between reported financial practices and outcomes (Section 2.3, p. 122).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The authors acknowledge that the study is limited to online freelancers in Laguna, which may restrict generalizability to other regions or countries; the sample included only registered online freelancers using prominent platforms, excluding other segments of the gig economy and traditional employment (Introduction, p. 119).
- The authors acknowledge that as a cross-sectional study, it identified relationships between variables at one point in time and could not establish causal effects or long-term changes (Introduction, pp. 119–120).
- The authors acknowledge that varying levels of awareness of digital platform taxation among respondents may have influenced reported experiences; findings related to taxation reflect participants' perceptions rather than an objective assessment of tax regulations (Introduction, p. 120).
- The authors acknowledge that only a small subset of participants submitted documentary evidence, indicating potential non-response bias (Section 3.1, p. 124).
- The authors acknowledge that the study does not provide sufficient evidence to explain the observed gender difference in resilience, and that further research is needed to explore contextual factors (Section 3.3, p. 126).
- The authors acknowledge the relatively small sample size and position the study as an exploratory pilot, with inferential statistics used to identify potential trends rather than definitive population-wide parameters (Section 2.3, p. 122).
- [unacknowledged] The paper reports a regression R² of 0.73 and significant predictors but does not report the adjusted R², F-statistic, degrees of freedom, or the number of predictors entered, so the regression model cannot be fully evaluated (Section 3.3, p. 126).
- [unacknowledged] The paper states that the questionnaire was pilot-tested for internal consistency using Cronbach's Alpha with α ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable, but it does not report the actual computed alpha values for the instrument or its subscales (Section 2.4, p. 122).
- [unacknowledged] The paper describes collecting demographic information (age, gender, freelancing niche, years of experience, income bracket) to support subgroup analysis, but reports only gender and years of experience in the regression results; the descriptive demographics of the sample are not tabulated (Section 2.4, p. 122).
- [unacknowledged] The qualitative component is described as embedded and used to contextualize statistical data, but the paper does not report systematic qualitative findings (e.g., themes with participant counts or illustrative quotes from interviews) beyond the documentary table (Table 3, p. 124).
- [unacknowledged] The proposed financial literacy program (Table 6, pp. 127–128) is not evaluated or piloted; the paper states that its effectiveness "can be measured" through behavioral outcomes but does not report any measurement.
- [unacknowledged] The paper reports that the 12% VAT reduced take-home pay and increased costs, but does not quantify the average reduction in net income or the proportion of freelancers affected, relying instead on self-reported perceptions (Section 3.1, pp. 123–124).
- [unacknowledged] The paragraph on gender and career experience effects appears twice, verbatim, on the same page (Section 3.3, p. 126), suggesting an editorial error that complicates the reading of the regression results.
- [unacknowledged] The paper does not report response rates or the number of survey questionnaires distributed versus returned, limiting assessment of non-response bias beyond the documentary evidence (Section 2.5, p. 123).
- [unacknowledged] The study uses a 5-point semantic differential scale for financial management constructs, but the paper does not report the individual scale items, their wording, or the composite scoring procedure, limiting reproducibility (Section 2.4, p. 122).

## Remember This

- The study examines financial management practices and financial resilience of 30 online freelancers in Laguna, Philippines, amid the 12% Digital Platform Taxation implemented June 1, 2025.
- Buffer Stock Savings (M = 3.49), Perceived Income Volatility (M = 3.1), Liquid Asset Accessibility (M = 3.47), and Digital Platform Taxation awareness (M = 3.81) were the four financial management variables measured.
- Financial resilience was moderate (M = 3.19, SD = 1.30), with confidence in recovery (M = 3.81) exceeding preparedness for medical emergencies (M = 2.85).
- Liquid Asset Accessibility was the only significant positive predictor of resilience (β = 0.58, p = 0.04); male gender (1.13 points, p = 0.03) and 5–7 years experience (-1.62, p = 0.01) also influenced resilience.
- The study proposes a six-component financial literacy program but does not evaluate it.
- The estimated population of online freelancers in Laguna was 1,903, derived from LinkedIn, Facebook, OnlineJobs.ph, and other local networks.
