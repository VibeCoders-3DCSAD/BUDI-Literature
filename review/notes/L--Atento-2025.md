## Summary

This descriptive–correlational survey study assesses Filipino students' awareness and perceptions of monetary policy and examines how these relate to saving, investing, and spending behaviors. Data were collected from 200 respondents across senior high school, undergraduate, and graduate levels in selected Philippine institutions. Awareness is moderate overall, with graduate students and business/economics students exhibiting significantly higher understanding than senior high and non-business peers. Students generally perceive monetary policy as effective in controlling inflation and stabilizing the economy but express more cautious views about its role in employment and long-term growth. Awareness correlates positively with perceptions of effectiveness (r = 0.48), saving behavior (r = 0.31), and investment behavior (r = 0.24), but not with spending behavior (r = –0.07). Regression confirms awareness and perceptions predict saving (R² = .22) and investment (R² = .15) but not spending (R² = .04). The study is grounded in the Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) and Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB).

## Problem and Motivation

Despite the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP) consistently emphasizing its twin mandates of price stability and financial stability, the degree to which ordinary Filipinos—particularly students and young adults—understand and perceive monetary policy remains underexplored. Research suggests public awareness of economic policy is a critical determinant of its effectiveness, as expectations influence consumption, saving, and investment behaviors (Blinder et al., 2008). In societies where financial literacy remains uneven, a knowledge gap about monetary policy mechanisms may limit the policy's ability to achieve its intended outcomes. Studies have consistently shown that Filipino students often demonstrate low levels of financial literacy compared to their peers in other ASEAN countries (OECD, 2017; Ramirez, 2021). The youth population aged 15–30 accounts for nearly 30% of the Philippine population and will shape the country's workforce, entrepreneurial ventures, and consumption patterns in the decades to come.

## Method

**Design.** Descriptive–correlational survey design; the authors state this explicitly: "The study adopts a descriptive–correlational survey design" (Sec. 3.1, p. 9).
**Sample.** N = 200 valid responses (from 230 initially received after data cleaning) across three educational strata: senior high school (Grades 11–12), undergraduate (first to fourth year), and graduate (master's and doctoral) students. Unit of analysis is the individual student respondent.
**Context.** geography: Philippines (selected higher education institutions and senior high schools); population: Students enrolled during academic year 2024–2025, aged 16–18 (senior high), 19–22 (traditional college), 23–30 (young professionals/graduate), and 31+; setting: Mixed-mode survey administration (online Google Forms and paper-based) across selected HEIs and senior high schools.

- The study uses stratified random sampling to ensure proportional representation of educational levels and academic programs (Sec. 3.2.2, p. 9).
- Stratification categories include educational level (senior high, undergraduate, graduate), program/field of study (business/management, economics, IT/computer studies, engineering, other programs), and demographic characteristics (gender, age brackets) (Sec. 3.2.2, p. 9).
- Sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1, determining approximately 190–210 respondents would be sufficient to detect small-to-moderate effects at the desired statistical power (Sec. 3.2.3, p. 9).
- The research instrument was a structured survey questionnaire developed based on existing validated scales and conceptual frameworks (TRA/TPB), comprising four sections: demographics (5 items), awareness of monetary policy (10 items, 5-point Likert), perceptions of monetary policy (10 items, 5-point Likert), and financial behavior (6 items combining Likert-scale and categorical responses) (Sec. 3.3.1, p. 10; Sec. 3.5.3, p. 11).
- Content validity was established through review by three subject-matter experts: one academic specializing in economics, one financial literacy practitioner, and one educational psychologist (Sec. 3.3.2, p. 10).
- A pilot test with 30 students assessed clarity, reliability, and time required; Cronbach's alpha coefficients exceeded the 0.70 threshold for each subscale (Sec. 3.3.2, p. 10).
- Data analysis used Jamovi and SPSS; descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means, standard deviations) and inferential statistics (ANOVA, independent samples t-test, Pearson's correlation, multiple regression) (Sec. 3.6, p. 11).
- Post-hoc tests used Tukey's HSD to identify specific group differences (Sec. 3.6.2, p. 11).
- Multicollinearity was not a concern (VIFs < 2.0); residual plots supported linearity and homoscedasticity; residuals approximated normality for N = 200 (Sec. 3.6.2, p. 11).
- Internal consistencies were acceptable: awareness α = .82, perception α = .85, saving α = .78, investment α = .76, spending α = .74 (Sec. 3.6.3, p. 12).

## Key Findings

- Overall awareness of monetary policy is moderate, with students demonstrating basic familiarity with inflation and interest rates but limited understanding of more complex mechanisms such as open market operations or monetary targeting (Sec. 4.2, p. 13).
- Graduate students consistently report higher awareness scores (M = 3.75) compared to undergraduate (M = 3.25) and senior high school students (M = 2.80); all pairwise differences were significant (F = 47.83, p < .001) (Sec. 4.3, Table 6, p. 14).
- Business and economics students reported the highest awareness (M = 3.48), followed by IT/Computer Studies (M = 3.28), Engineering (M = 3.16), Education/Health Sciences (M = 3.10), and Others (M = 3.02); F = 11.22, p < .001 (Sec. 4.3, Table 7, p. 15).
- Older students, particularly those aged 23–30 (M = 3.48) and 31+ (M = 3.60), reported higher awareness than the youngest group aged 16–18 (M = 2.85); F = 14.62, p < .001 (Sec. 4.3, Table 4, p. 14).
- No significant difference in awareness between male (M = 3.26) and female respondents (M = 3.28); t(198) = 0.31, p = .756 (Sec. 4.3, Table 5, p. 14).
- Awareness correlates positively with perception (r = 0.48, p < .001), saving behavior (r = 0.31, p < .001), and investment behavior (r = 0.24, p = .001), but shows no significant relationship with spending behavior (r = –0.07, p = .310) (Sec. 4.4, pp. 15-16).
- Regression findings indicate awareness and perception jointly predict saving (R² = .22) and investment (R² = .15) but not spending (R² = .04) (Sec. 4.5, p. 16).
- Saving behavior: Awareness (β = 0.21, p = .002) and perception (β = 0.28, p < .001) are both significant predictors (Sec. 4.5, p. 16).
- Investment behavior: Awareness (β = 0.17, p = .015) and perception (β = 0.20, p = .004) are both significant predictors (Sec. 4.5, p. 16).
- Spending behavior: Neither awareness (β = −0.05, p = .381) nor perception (β = −0.06, p = .345) is significant (Sec. 4.5, p. 16).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The authors acknowledge that the cross-sectional design prevents causal inference; longitudinal research is needed to track changes in awareness over time and in response to policy or educational interventions (Sec. 3.8, p. 12).
- The authors acknowledge reliance on self-administered surveys introduces potential biases such as social desirability, recall errors, or misinterpretation of items (Sec. 3.8, p. 12).
- The authors acknowledge that although stratified random sampling was employed, the sample is restricted to selected HEIs and senior high schools, which may limit the generalizability of findings to all Filipino students (Sec. 3.8, p. 12).
- The authors acknowledge that other critical variables—such as trust in government institutions, media exposure, and cultural attitudes toward debt—were beyond the scope of the study but may significantly influence monetary policy literacy (Sec. 3.8, p. 12).
- The authors acknowledge that ongoing global uncertainties (e.g., post-COVID recovery, geopolitical shocks, climate-induced disruptions) may have influenced students' responses, complicating efforts to isolate the effect of educational background alone (Sec. 3.8, p. 12).
- [unacknowledged] The paper reports a moderate relationship between awareness and saving (r = 0.31) and investment (r = 0.24), but the regression models explain only 22% and 15% of variance respectively, suggesting substantial unmeasured influences.
- [unacknowledged] The study is limited to selected educational institutions in the Philippines and does not report the number of institutions, their geographic distribution, or whether the sample is nationally representative.
- [unacknowledged] The paper does not report effect sizes for ANOVA results (only F and p values), making it difficult to assess the practical significance of group differences.
- [unacknowledged] The study does not report confidence intervals for correlation or regression coefficients, limiting precision assessment.

## Definitions

- **Monetary policy** — The deliberate actions of a country's central bank to regulate the supply of money and credit in the economy to achieve macroeconomic objectives such as price stability, employment generation, sustainable growth, and financial system stability (Sec. 1.1.1, p. 2; citing Mishkin, 2019).
- **Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (BSP)** — The Philippines' central monetary authority; primary mandate is to maintain price stability while promoting a financial system conducive to balanced and sustainable growth (Sec. 1.1.1, p. 2).
- **Inflation targeting** — A framework adopted by the BSP in 2002 that seeks to anchor inflation expectations and provide transparency and accountability in monetary decision-making (Sec. 1.1.1, p. 2).
- **Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA)** — Framework positing that individual behavior is shaped by behavioral intentions, which are influenced by attitudes and subjective norms (Sec. 1.1.5, p. 3).
- **Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB)** — Extended form of TRA that adds perceived behavioral control to the model of behavioral intentions (Sec. 1.1.5, p. 3).
- **Financial literacy** — A broad set of competencies including budgeting, saving, investing, and understanding credit (Sec. 1.1.4, p. 3).
- **Awareness of monetary policy** — Measured through items assessing knowledge of monetary policy objectives (e.g., inflation targeting, interest rate adjustments, exchange rate stability), with scores computed as averages across items (Sec. 3.5.2, p. 11).
- **Perceptions of monetary policy effectiveness** — Items gauging respondents' views on whether monetary policy can manage inflation, stabilize currency, and support growth (Sec. 3.5.2, p. 11).
