# L--Bulan-2025 — The Interplay of Behavioral Intention on the Relationship between Self-Control and Saving Behavior among MSME Business Owners

## Summary

A local, quantitative, cross-sectional survey study of micro, small and medium enterprise (MSME) business owners in the municipality of Tagbina, Surigao del Sur, Philippines. The paper asks three questions in one framework: how self-control relates to saving behaviour, how behavioural intention relates to saving behaviour, and whether behavioural intention moderates the self-control-to-saving-behaviour relationship. Data come from a researcher-modified structured questionnaire administered to 285 respondents, with self-control measured on three dimensions (anticipatory control, down-regulation of temptation, behavioural inhibition), saving behaviour measured on three dimensions (saving consistency, saving goals, saving attitudes), and behavioural intention measured on five indicators (attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioural control, financial literacy, goal-setting). Analysis is descriptive (means and descriptive levels) plus bivariate Pearson correlation plus a three-step hierarchical regression with a self-control × behavioural-intention interaction term.

The paper reports that all three constructs sit at very high self-rated levels (self-control overall mean 4.23, saving behaviour overall 4.38, behavioural intention overall 4.35 on a five-point Likert scale), that every self-control and behavioural-intention indicator correlates significantly and positively with every saving-behaviour indicator (p = 0.000 throughout, r ranging from 0.390 to 0.671), and that the interaction term in the hierarchical regression is statistically significant (B = -.150, β = -.985, p < 0.05) with a change in R-square of .014. The authors interpret the negative interaction as behavioural intention negatively moderating the self-control-to-saving-behaviour link: the self-control slope is steeper at low behavioural intention and flatter at high behavioural intention, even though saving behaviour itself is higher at higher behavioural intention. The stated contribution is a unified three-variable test in a local MSME population where prior work examined only two-variable associations. The paper reports no system, no software artefact, no forecasting model and no optimisation: its contribution sits entirely inside the financial-planning and savings-behaviour literature, not the PFM-application or algorithm literature.

## Problem and Motivation

The Philippines is described as having a higher poverty percentage than other Asian nations, with entrepreneurship promoted as the route to job formation and wealth creation, and MSMEs described as generating 5,607,748 jobs or 65.10% of national employment in 2022 (Sect. 1 Introduction, p. 1). MSMEs nonetheless face strict loan requirements and high interest rates, so many owners rely on personal savings to fund start-ups, and savings serve as the emergency safety net (p. 1). Against that, the paper cites Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas statistics for the last quarter of 2023 showing household savings fell across all income groups, and reports that in 2024 Filipinos wanted to save more and pay off more debt because of rising costs (p. 2).

The stated research gap is that the interaction of self-control, behavioural intention and saving behaviour has not been examined inside a single study framework: "Prior studies focused on two-variable associations rather than their interaction," and there is little empirical study of MSME owners' saving behaviour despite their importance to the local economy (Sect. 1 Introduction, p. 2). The paper also notes that MSME owners face difficulties saving despite having surpluses, and that everyday temptations and urges challenge self-control (p. 2). The intended audience is policy makers and financial organisations, with the results positioned as input to financial education programmes for MSMEs in Tagbina (p. 2).

## Method

**Design.** Descriptive-correlational and regression methodologies, in the authors' own words: "This investigation employed descriptive-correlational and regression methodologies" (Sect. 2 Research Methods, p. 4); the moderation test uses a three-step hierarchical regression. The authors state no further formal design label and do not name a design such as cross-sectional survey, though the data are single-occasion self-report.
**Sample.** n = 285 respondents (unit of analysis: individual MSME business owner in the municipality of Tagbina, Surigao del Sur); the paper writes that "questionnaires were sent to 285 persons" and the abstract reports a "survey of 285 respondents" (Abstract, p. 1; Sect. 2, p. 4). No response rate and no number of questionnaires returned versus distributed is reported.
**Context.** geography: Municipality of Tagbina, Surigao del Sur, Philippines (single municipality); population: micro, small and medium enterprise business owners; setting: paper-and-structured-questionnaire survey administered once, five-point Likert scale (5 = Strongly Agree, 1 = Strongly Disagree), simple random sampling via the Stat Trek generator, informed consent obtained before survey completion and response privacy preserved.

Procedure as reported:

- Build a single structured questionnaire from three adapted standard instruments, with original phrases added by the researcher to fit the study context (Sect. 2, p. 4).
- Measure the independent variable, self-control, after Katzir et al. (2021) across three dimensions: anticipatory control (4 items), down-regulation of temptation (4 items), behavioural inhibition (5 items) (p. 4).
- Measure the dependent variable, saving behaviour, after Mpaata et al. (2021) across three dimensions: saving consistency (5 items), saving goals (5 items), saving attitude (4 items) (p. 4).
- Measure the moderating variable, behavioural intention, across five factors: attitude (5 items), subjective norms (5 items), perceived behavioural control (5 items), financial literacy (5 items), goal-setting (5 items), with the base indicator from Ru et al. (2018) and two further indicators added (p. 4).
- Select the target respondents by simple random sampling using the Stat Trek generator and distribute questionnaires to 285 persons in Tagbina (p. 4).
- Use descriptive statistics to report the perceived level of each construct and dimension, labelled Very High / High (Sect. 3.1–3.3, pp. 5–6).
- Use bivariate correlational analysis across the three constructs to produce the pairwise r-values, treating the bivariate interactions as the necessary input to a moderation analysis (Sect. 2, p. 5).
- Multiply self-control by behavioural intention to create the interaction term (Sect. 3.6, p. 8).
- Run a three-step hierarchical regression predicting saving behaviour: Step 1 self-control; Step 2 self-control + behavioural intention; Step 3 self-control + behavioural intention + the interaction term (Table 6, p. 9).
- Report the change in R-square at each step as the variance in saving behaviour explained at that phase (Sect. 3.6, pp. 8–9).
- Use the Step 3 unstandardised interaction coefficient together with descriptive statistics (Table 8) to drive a ModGraph of the main effects and the interaction (Table 7; Figure 2, p. 10).
- State hypotheses: the paper says "Three hypotheses have been framed based on the model developed and were tested using statistical analysis tools" but does not print the three hypotheses anywhere in the text (Sect. 2, p. 4).
- Conduct the research ethically with informed consent and response privacy (Sect. 2, p. 5).

## Software

- Stat Trek random-sampling generator (version not reported) — used to draw the simple random sample of respondents (Sect. 2 Research Methods, p. 4).
- ModGraph (version not reported) — named as the tool behind the moderation graph in Figure 2 (Sect. 3.6, p. 9).
- Statistical analysis software: Not reported. No package (SPSS, Stata, R, SmartPLS or similar) is named anywhere in the paper.
- Data-collection instrument: researcher-modified structured questionnaire on a five-point Likert scale, assembled from Katzir et al. (2021), Mpaata et al. (2021) and Ru et al. (2018) (Sect. 2, p. 4).
- No software system, application, mobile artefact, prototype, dataset release or code repository is described by this paper. It is a survey study, not a development study.

## Key Findings

- num: Self-control overall mean is 4.23, described in the narrative as "a fairly high mean" and in Table 1 as "Very High" (Table 1, p. 5).
- num: Saving behaviour overall mean is 4.38, the highest of the three construct-level means, described in the narrative as "extremely high" (Table 2, p. 5).
- num: Behavioural intention overall mean is 4.35, with attitude the highest single dimension at 4.59 and subjective norms the lowest at 4.18 (Table 3, p. 6).
- num: Every one of the nine self-control × saving-behaviour correlations in Table 4 is significant at p = 0.000 and the null hypothesis is rejected in all nine rows (Table 4, pp. 6–7).
- num: Every one of the fifteen behavioural-intention × saving-behaviour correlations in Table 5 is significant at p = 0.000 and the null hypothesis is rejected in all fifteen rows (Table 5, p. 8).
- num: The strongest correlation anywhere in the paper is perceived behavioural control × saving consistency at r = 0.671 (Table 5, p. 8).
- num: The weakest correlation anywhere in the paper is down-regulation of temptation × saving consistency at r = 0.390 (Table 4, p. 6).
- num: The hierarchical regression Step 1 R-square change is .483, described in the narrative as 48% of the variance in respondents' saving behaviour attributable to self-control alone (Table 6, p. 9; Sect. 3.6, p. 9).
- num: Step 2 R-square change is .216, described in the narrative as an extra 22% of variance, and Step 3 R-square change is .014, described as approximately 1.4% more variance beyond the two primary effects (Table 6, p. 9; Sect. 3.6, pp. 8–9).
- num: The interaction effect is negative (B = -.150, β = -.985, p < 0.05), which the authors read as behavioural intention negatively moderating the self-control-to-saving-behaviour relationship (Table 6, p. 9; Sect. 3.6, p. 9).
- num: The self-control slope is steeper for low behavioural intention than for high behavioural intention, which the paper describes as the basis for rejecting the null hypothesis (Sect. 3.6, p. 10).
- The behaviour of the self-control coefficients across steps is not discussed by the authors: the self-control B falls from .751 in Step 1 to .256 in Step 2 and then rises to .862 in Step 3 (Table 6, p. 9).
- The authors conclude that self-control and behavioural intention both contribute to predicting saving behaviour in MSME entrepreneurs, and that the self-control-to-saving link is strong at low behavioural intention and diminished at high behavioural intention (Sect. 4 Conclusion, p. 10).

## Limitations and Gaps

Acknowledged by the authors:

- Geographic and sampling restriction: "The study has specific limitations, as it concentrated solely on the municipality of Tagbina with a limited sample size" (Sect. 4 Conclusion, p. 10). The authors suggest further testing on respondents with diverse features to enhance the universality of the moderation test.
- Measurement subjectivity: "This study employs attitude assessments derived from respondents' subjective evaluations of their self-control and savings behavior" (Sect. 4 Conclusion, p. 10). The authors recommend objective measures of self-regulation through classified behavioural tests and objective assessment of saving behaviour through quantifying the amount saved and the frequency of savings.
- Omitted external factors: the authors state that further study "would have benefited from investigations of the external factors that influence savings behavior, like economic conditions, income, access to financial services, and government policies" (Sect. 4 Conclusion, pp. 10–11).

[unacknowledged] findings of this extraction:

No response rate or usable-return count is given.
- [unacknowledged] Twenty-four bivariate correlations are tested and reported in Tables 4 and 5 with no correction for multiple comparisons; no confidence intervals, no standard errors and no effect sizes other than r are printed for any correlation.
- [unacknowledged] The Step 2 constant in Table 6 is not statistically significant (B = -.108, Std. Error = .175, t = -.618, Sig. = .537), yet the narrative states that "In the second step of the regression analysis, the model exhibited significance (p < 0.05)" (Sect. 3.6, p. 8; Table 6, p. 9). The authors do not comment on the non-significant constant.
- [unacknowledged] The narrative says "The decrease in r-square quantifies the variance in saving behavior explained at each phase" (Sect. 3.6, p. 8) while the values it then cites are increases: .483 at Step 1, .216 at Step 2, .014 at Step 3 (Table 6, p. 9). The word "decrease" is not consistent with the direction of the tabulated changes.
- [unacknowledged] The paper treats a main effect and an interaction as a single conclusion. Section 3.6 states that "MSME business owners with greater behavioral intention exhibit a higher level of saving behavior" and, in the same section, that the self-control-to-saving slope is steeper at low behavioural intention (Sect. 3.6, pp. 9–10). The first is a main effect of behavioural intention, the second is the interaction; the paper's conclusion sentence merges them.
- [unacknowledged] The design is single-occasion, single-source, self-report, so no causal claim about self-control producing saving behaviour is supported, yet the abstract and narrative use causal language: "self-control positively impacts saving behavior" and "the impact of self-control on their saving behavior diminishes" (Abstract, p. 1; Sect. 3.6, p. 8).
- [unacknowledged] All Table 7 cell means lie between 3.9008 and 4.8320 on a five-point scale. This restricted range, together with possible common-method variance from a single questionnaire, may attenuate or inflate the reported correlations; neither possibility is discussed.
- [unacknowledged] The abstract reports only that behavioural intention "moderates the connection between self-control and saving behavior" without stating the direction of moderation (Abstract, p. 1). The direction (negative) appears only in the conclusion, so a reader of the abstract alone cannot tell whether the moderator strengthens or weakens the relationship.
- [unacknowledged] The text of Sect. 3.1 calls the 4.23 overall self-control mean "a fairly high mean" while Table 1 labels the same value "Very High"; the text of Sect. 3.2 calls the 4.38 overall saving-behaviour mean "extremely high" while Table 2 labels it "Very High" (Sect. 3.1, p. 5; Sect. 3.2, p. 5). The descriptive-level vocabulary is not used consistently between narrative and table.
- [unacknowledged] The reported coefficients contradict the paper's own literature framing in one place and confirm it in another: the paper reports a significant positive self-control-to-saving link while noting that Qaiser et al. (2020), Alshebami and Seraj (2021) and Christianto and Asandimitra (2023) "argued that self-control does not have a significant impact on saving behavior" (Sect. 3.4, p. 7). The authors attribute the divergence to foreign-country demographics but offer no test of that explanation.
- [unacknowledged] No power analysis or sample-size justification is given for 285 respondents, despite the paper relying on a three-step hierarchical regression with an interaction term, which typically requires more observations than a main-effects model.

## Definitions

- **Self-control** — the ability to postpone immediate gratification in favour of future outcomes; the capacity to resist short-term temptation and pursue long-term goals; the ability to manage behaviour and emotions and to prevent acting out of undesirable impulses (Sect. 1.1.1, p. 2, citing Groß, 2021 and Yang et al., 2022).
- **Anticipatory control** — one of the three measured self-control dimensions (4 items in the instrument) (Sect. 2, p. 4; Table 1, p. 5).
- **Down-regulation of temptation** — one of the three measured self-control dimensions (4 items) (Sect. 2, p. 4; Table 1, p. 5).
- **Behavioral inhibition** — one of the three measured self-control dimensions (5 items) (Sect. 2, p. 4; Table 1, p. 5).
- **Saving** — "the portion of income that is not used for immediate consumption, but rather set aside for future use" (Sect. 1.1.2, p. 3, quoting the Central Bank of the Republic of Turkey glossary).
- **Saving behavior** — the amalgamation of one's perceptions of future needs, the act of making a decision to save, and the actual act of saving; also an individual's action of decreasing expenses or postponing consumption (Sect. 1.1.2, p. 3, citing Tharanika et al., 2017).
- **Saving consistency** — one of the three measured saving-behaviour dimensions (5 items) (Sect. 2, p. 4; Table 2, p. 5).
- **Saving goals** — one of the three measured saving-behaviour dimensions (5 items) (Sect. 2, p. 4; Table 2, p. 5).
- **Saving attitudes** — one of the three measured saving-behaviour dimensions (4 items) (Sect. 2, p. 4; Table 2, p. 5).
- **Behavioral intention** — a person's planned actions or behaviours towards an object; in the Theory of Planned Behavior, the direct antecedent of conduct that reflects the level of effort an individual is prepared to invest in performing an action (Sect. 1.1.3, p. 3, citing Ajzen, 1991; Ajzen, 2002).
- **Behavioral Life-Cycle (BLC) hypothesis** — Shefrin and Thaler's (1988) account in which individuals face a dilemma between immediate gratification and long-term financial security when conserving money, and in which the desire to mitigate short-run "doer" consumption preferences motivates the use of financial constraints (Sect. 1.1.4, p. 3).
- **Planner–doer distinction** — within the BLC framework, some individuals "consider saving a good in and of itself" and rarely express doer behaviour, tending instead to think about long-run welfare (Sect. 1.1.4, p. 3, quoting Thaler & Shefrin, 1981).
- **Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB)** — Ajzen's (1991) theory in which attitude, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control govern the likelihood of engaging in a behaviour, with behavioural intention as the essential element (Sect. 1.1.4, p. 4).
- **Moderation (as used here)** — behavioural intention is modelled as the moderating variable, self-control as the independent variable, saving behaviour as the dependent variable, and the product of self-control and behavioural intention as the interaction term (Sect. 3.6, p. 8).
- **MSME** — micro, small and medium enterprise; the study population is MSME business owners in Tagbina, Surigao del Sur (Sect. 1 Introduction, p. 1).

## Remember This

- A local (Philippine) cross-sectional survey of 285 MSME business owners in Tagbina, Surigao del Sur, testing whether behavioural intention moderates the self-control-to-saving-behaviour link.
- All three constructs are self-rated very high on a five-point Likert scale: saving behaviour 4.38, behavioural intention 4.35, self-control 4.23.
- All 24 bivariate correlations between the self-control/behavioural-intention indicators and the saving-behaviour indicators are positive and significant at p = 0.000, ranging from r = 0.390 to r = 0.671.
- The hierarchical regression's Step 1 R-square change is .483 (48% attributed to self-control alone), Step 2 is .216 (22%), and Step 3 is .014 (approximately 1.4% for the interaction), with the interaction term significant at p < 0.05.
- The moderation is negative: the self-control slope is steeper at low behavioural intention (low-self-control × low-intention cell mean 3.9008) and flatter at high behavioural intention (low-self-control × high-intention cell mean 4.1983).
- The narrative in Sect. 3.5 reports 0.601 and 0.577 for the subjective-norms cells while Table 5 prints 0.498 and 0.550; both values are kept in the evidence table and the contradiction is recorded in Limitations.
- The paper states three hypotheses were framed and tested but never prints them, and it reports no reliability or validity statistics for the adapted questionnaire.
