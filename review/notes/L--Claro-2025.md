# The Regressors of Financial Well-Being Among LGU Employees in Davao del Norte

`L--Claro-2025` — Claro (2025), *ISRG Journal of Economics, Business and Management, 3(6)* \[doi: Not reported]

## Summary

A descriptive-correlational survey of 300 local government employees finds that access to financial services, service availability, affordability and usage predict financial well-being, while financial literacy does not, and concludes that knowledge alone leaves local public-sector well-being unimproved.

## Problem and Motivation

Filipino households face inadequate savings — only 30% hold sufficient savings — and the resulting emergency preparedness and debt-management problems are matched by a public sector whose employees carry the additional burden of income volatility from multiple income sources and allowances (Background, p. 172). Financial insecurity then pushes individuals toward high-interest loans and other risky credit behaviours (Background, p. 172). Although Filipino financial behaviour has been studied for financial literacy alone, little is known about local government employees' specific financial experiences despite the Cap 201 and 202 salary standardisation that made their incomes more predictable (Background, p. 172).

## Method

**Design.** quantitative descriptive-correlational research design, anchored on the Capability Approach, Consumer Financial Behaviour Theory and Self-Determination Theory (Theoretical Framework, p. 174; Methodology, p. 174).
**Sample.** n = 300 local government employees from local government units in Davao del Norte; 50.66% male and 49.33% female, spread across the 0-24 (12.33%), 25-34 (33.33%), 35-44 (37.00%), 45-54 (13.33%) and 55 and above (4.00%) age groups (Research Respondents, p. 175).
**Context.** geography: Davao del Norte, Philippines; population: LGU employees; setting: a structured survey of local public servants conducted in 2024, with the publisher ISRG (Background, pp. 171-172; Research Respondents, p. 175).

- Ground financial well-being in the Capability Approach, Consumer Financial Behaviour Theory and Self-Determination Theory, treating well-being as the capacity to meet daily obligations, plan for expenses and handle unexpected events (Theoretical Framework, p. 174; RRL, p. 173).
- Operationalise the dependent variable with three validated indicators: financial preparedness, beliefs of credit limits and risky indebtedness behaviour (RRL, p. 173).
- Specify five regressors — financial literacy, accessibility, availability, affordability and usage of financial services — and state that the study will determine the factors that significantly influence financial well-being (Statement of the Problem and RRL, p. 172).
- Use an adopted and validated questionnaire as the data-collection instrument (Methodology, p. 174).
- Conduct a descriptive-correlational analysis of the collected survey data, described as conducted by the researchers (Methodology, p. 174).
- Present the descriptive and inferential findings of the five regressors, then derive a conclusion from the most significant predictor (Conclusion, p. 175).

## Key Findings

- num: The study surveys 300 local government employees from Davao del Norte, 50.66% male and 49.33% female, with the largest age groups 35-44 (37.00%) and 25-34 (33.33%) (Research Respondents, p. 175).
- Financial well-being is measured through three indicators — financial preparedness, beliefs of credit limits and risky indebtedness behaviour — and respondents rated preparedness and credit-limit beliefs as high or very high, and risky indebtedness behaviour as moderate (Conclusion, p. 175).
- num: 30% of Filipinos hold sufficient savings, which the paper cites as the evidence of inadequate household financial resilience (Background, p. 172).
- Access, availability, affordability and usage of financial services significantly predict financial well-being (Conclusion, p. 175).
- Access is the strongest predictor of financial well-being, followed by usage, affordability and availability (Conclusion, p. 175).
- Financial literacy correlated weakly positively with financial well-being but was not a significant predictor, so knowing about money is not matched by doing the right thing with it (Conclusion, p. 175).
- Improved access to affordable financial services is treated as the route to financial inclusion, letting individuals manage finances more effectively, save securely and invest wisely (Background, p. 172).
- Respondents' high preparedness alongside moderate risky indebtedness is read as showing that good intentions coexist with over-borrowing, which the paper attributes to a need for targeted financial education (Conclusion, p. 175).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The single-province, single-sector setting generalises only to Davao del Norte LGU employees, so the findings do not extend to other provinces or to private-sector workers.
- [unacknowledged] The study is cross-sectional and correlational, so it cannot establish that financial-service access, availability, affordability or usage cause financial well-being.
- [unacknowledged] The conversion supplied for this paper omits the entire Results and Discussion body, including all descriptive tables, coefficients, p-values and model-fit statistics, so none of the reported predictor strengths can be checked numerically.
- [unacknowledged] The instrument, its reliability and validity statistics, the sampling technique and the treatment of missing responses are not reported, so the quality of the measurements cannot be assessed.
- [unacknowledged] Financial literacy is measured as an individual's own perception of financial knowledge rather than as an objective knowledge test, which the authors do not raise despite the same distinction being central to the wider literature.
- [unacknowledged] The dependent variable rests entirely on self-reported preparedness, credit-limit beliefs and indebtedness behaviour, and no financial records, balances or borrowing data support the ratings.
- [unacknowledged] The paper reports no income, employment status or debt-exposure data, so the income-position effects and the ordinary-expense borrowing patterns asserted in Chapter 2 have no empirical basis here.

## Definitions

- **Financial well-being** — the state of individuals and families, confident of and able to meet their present and future obligations, and can choose their satisfaction and well-being (Background, p. 171).
- **Financial literacy** — individuals' or households' ability to make informed decisions and effectively manage their financial resources, comprising knowledge, understanding and skills combined with the motivation and confidence to apply them (Background, p. 172).
- **Financial inclusion** — ensuring that people have effective access to a wide range of financial services that meet their needs and are delivered transparently at affordable cost with adequate consumer protection (Background, p. 172).
- **Financial accessibility** — the ease with which individuals can obtain and use financial services, encompassing physical proximity, online availability and the absence of unnecessary barriers to entry (RRL, p. 174).
- **Financial availability** — the existence of financial products and services tailored to meet the needs of individuals, ranging from basic accounts to more sophisticated financial instruments (RRL, p. 174).
- **Financial affordability** — the cost of financial services relative to the financial resources of individuals, determining whether they can sustain the services without compromising other essential needs (RRL, p. 174).
- **Financial preparedness** — the ability to manage sudden and unavoidable life expenses such as job loss, serious illness or disability, acting as a safeguard against economic shocks that can otherwise cause hardship and poverty (RRL, p. 173).
- **Beliefs of credit limits** — consumers' subjective understanding of their own borrowing capacity, which they commonly conflate with their purchasing power (RRL, p. 173).
- **Risky indebtedness behaviour** — actions and attitudes that expose individuals to unsustainable debt, including overestimating debt-management capability, engaging in debt without adequate planning and facing financial distress as a consequence (RRL, p. 173).
- **Local Government Units (LGUs)** — the lowest administrative tier of Philippine government, whose employees' incomes follow salary standardisation while allowances remain variable (Background, p. 172).
