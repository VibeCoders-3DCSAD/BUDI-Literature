# L--Cruz-2026 — Extraction Note

## Summary

This paper reports a descriptive-correlational survey of young professionals in the National Capital Region (NCR) of the Philippines, testing whether years of investing and monthly income are correlated with financial well-being. The authors surveyed 389 respondents aged 25-35 (the abstract states 385) using the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's Financial Well-Being Scale (18 items, 4-point Likert, Cronbach's alpha 0.964), and analysed the data with Pearson correlation and multiple regression. The overall financial well-being mean is 3.25, interpreted as "Excellent." Years of investing correlates with financial well-being at r = .364 (p = .000), income at r = .309 (p = .000), education at r = .288 (p = .000), and financial behaviour at r = .309 (p = .000). In multiple regression, Model 4 (years investing, income, and financial behaviour) explains 62.3% of the variance in financial well-being (R² = .623), with financial behaviour the strongest predictor (Beta = .706). Both hypotheses are supported. The authors conclude that targeted investment education and financial planning initiatives are warranted.

## Problem and Motivation

The paper identifies a gap in the literature: while studies on financial well-being continue to progress, "there are no studies that specifically elaborate the role of income levels and year of investment activities in overall financial health in the context of young professionals in NCR" (Sect. 1, p. 903). The study aims to answer three questions: (1) the level of financial well-being among young professionals in NCR; (2) the relationship between financial well-being and years of investing; and (3) the relationship between financial well-being and income. The authors argue that understanding these relationships can inform individuals' financial decisions, promote financial security, improve personal financial management skills, and guide policy interventions. They also suggest the findings can offer insights for similar groups in other emerging economies facing comparable challenges, urbanization, job markets, and accessibility to financial products (Sect. 1, p. 903).

## Method

**Design.** Descriptive-correlational research design (Abstract, p. 902); the authors also describe the study as "based on the survey designed to examine the causal relationship" (Sect. 5, p. 906), though the analysis is correlational.

**Sample.** n = 389 young professionals (the abstract states 385; the methods and all tables state 389); the unit of analysis is the individual respondent.

**Context.** geography: National Capital Region (NCR), Philippines; population: young professionals aged 25-35 who are working and residing in NCR and investing; setting: online survey (Google Forms distributed via Facebook and Instagram), conducted from February 9 to March 11, 2024.

Additional details:

- The instrument was adopted from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's "Measuring financial well-being: A guide to using the CFPB Financial Well-Being Scale," validated by academic experts, and pilot-tested with a Cronbach Alpha of 0.964 (Sect. 5, p. 906).
- The first part of the instrument screened for eligibility: 25-35 years old, working and residing in NCR, and investing (Sect. 5, p. 906).
- The second part collected demographics: age, gender, civil status, highest educational attainment, monthly income, number of years of investing, and work location in NCR (Sect. 5, p. 906).
- The third part consisted of 18 questions on financial well-being, answered on a 4-point Likert scale from "Strongly Agree" to "Strongly Disagree," interpreted using Pimentel's (2019) intervals (3.51-4.00 highest, 1.00-1.50 lowest) (Sect. 5, pp. 906-907).
- Sample size was derived using Cochran's formula, arriving at 385, but 389 responses were collected (Sect. 5, p. 907).
- Data were analysed using Pearson's coefficient correlation and multiple regression (Sect. 5, p. 907).
- The researchers state they safeguarded confidentiality and anonymity of participants (Sect. 5, p. 908).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The abstract reports 385 young professionals while the methods section, Table 1, Table 2, and all analyses report 389; the two numbers are never reconciled (Abstract, p. 902 vs. Sect. 5, p. 907; Table 1, p. 907).
- [unacknowledged] Table 1 reports the male frequency as 289 and the male percentage as 51.93%, but 51.93% of 389 is approximately 202; moreover, 187 female + 289 male = 476, not 389. The printed cell is internally inconsistent with its own percentage and with the reported total.
- [unacknowledged] The narrative accompanying Table 2 states that "253 of the respondents had a monthly income of 20,001 to 50,000 PHP with 45.24%," but Table 2 prints 176 for that category. 176/389 = 45.24%, while 253/389 = 65.04% (Sect. 6, p. 908; Table 2, p. 908).
- [unacknowledged] Table 1's age percentages sum to 99.49% (65.04 + 34.45), not 100.00% (Table 1, p. 907).
- [unacknowledged] The study uses a purposive sample of 389 online respondents, so it cannot support population-level inference for the 2,451,596 NCR residents aged 25-35 from which the sample was drawn (Sect. 5, p. 907).
- [unacknowledged] The methods section claims the survey was designed to examine the "causal relationship" (Sect. 5, p. 906), but a correlational design with a single cross-sectional survey cannot establish causation.
- [unacknowledged] No confidence intervals are reported for any correlation or regression coefficient; only p-values are given.
- [unacknowledged] The paper does not report the survey's response rate, the number of invitations sent, or any test for non-response bias.
- The authors acknowledge that future research should consider triangulation methods, larger sample sizes, and face-to-face interviews or focus groups (Sect. 7.2, p. 913).
- The authors acknowledge that the study is limited to young professionals in NCR and that generalizability to other countries, industries, and markets remains to be tested (Sect. 7.1, p. 913).
- The authors acknowledge that some studies use the demographic profile, which comprises the income level, in testing for the influence on financial well-being (Sect. 6.3.2, p. 912).

## Key Findings

- Years of investing has a significant low positive correlation with financial well-being (r = .364, p = .000; Table 4, p. 909).
- Income has a significant low positive correlation with financial well-being (r = .309, p = .000; Table 4, p. 909).
- Education (r = .288, p = .000) and financial behaviour (r = .309, p = .000) also correlate significantly with financial well-being (Table 4, p. 910).
- The overall financial well-being mean is 3.25, interpreted as "Excellent" (Table 3, p. 909).
- The highest-rated item is FWB9 ("My finances are the driving force in my life") at 3.40; the lowest is FWB6 ("My financial advisor helps me make informed decisions about my money") at 3.06 (Table 3, pp. 908-909).
- In multiple regression, Model 4 (years investing, income, financial behaviour) explains 62.3% of the variance (R² = .623), with financial behaviour the strongest predictor (Beta = .706, t = 21.747, p = .000; Table 5, p. 910; Table 7, p. 911).
- Both H1 and H2 are supported (Sect. 6.2, p. 911).
- The authors recommend targeted investment education programs, promotion of financial well-being, and policy interventions (Sect. 7.2, pp. 913-914).

## Definitions

- **Financial well-being** — having enough money to live comfortably; determined by control over finances, ability to endure financial stress, meeting financial goals, and financial freedom (Sect. 2, p. 903, citing Lambert et al., 2023 and CFPB, n.d.).
- **Objective financial well-being** — quantifiable factors such as credit scores, financial capability, and ability to handle unexpected expenses (Sect. 2, p. 903, citing Erner et al., 2016).
- **Subjective financial well-being** — personal perceptions and feelings of financial security and control (Sect. 2, p. 903, citing Erner et al., 2016).
- **Years of investing** — the number of years a respondent has been investing in asset classes (Sect. 5, p. 906; Table 2, p. 908).
- **Financial behavior** — a control variable used in the correlation and regression analyses; expected to influence investors' financial decisions (Sect. 6.1, p. 909).
- **CFPB Financial Well-Being Scale** — the 18-item, 4-point Likert instrument adopted for the study (Sect. 5, p. 906).
- **Financial burnout** — a state where individuals feel pushed to the limit in managing their finances, often due to limited cash flow (Sect. 2.1, p. 904, citing Konish, 2020).

## Remember This

- The paper is a descriptive-correlational survey of young professionals in NCR, Philippines, not an app or algorithm study.
- Sample size is reported inconsistently: 385 in the abstract, 389 in the methods and tables.
- Financial well-being correlates significantly but weakly with years of investing (r = .364) and income (r = .309).
- Financial behaviour is the strongest predictor in the full regression model (Beta = .706, R² = .623).
- Table 1's male frequency (289) is inconsistent with its own percentage (51.93%) and the total (389).
- Table 2's narrative says 253 respondents earn 20,001-50,000 PHP, but the table says 176.
- The authors recommend targeted investment education, budgeting and saving tools, and debt management strategies.
