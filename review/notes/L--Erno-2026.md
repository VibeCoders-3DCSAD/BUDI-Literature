## Summary

A quantitative descriptive survey of 300 household financial decision-makers in the Municipality of Tago, Surigao del Sur, Philippines, measuring financial capability across four domains (budgeting, saving, debt management, investment practices) and measuring financial resilience as an outcome. Financial capability is reported as structurally imbalanced: debt management is the only strong domain (overall mean 3.86, Agree), while budgeting (2.35), saving (2.44) and investment practices (2.37) all fall in the Disagree band, producing an overall capability mean of 2.76 (Moderately Agree). Financial resilience is low (overall mean 1.98, Disagree), with the weakest single indicator being the ability to manage sudden or multiple expenses through savings and planning (1.60, Strongly Disagree). The authors read the pattern as defensive and risk-averse financial behaviour — strong repayment discipline driven by concern over indebtedness, but weak forward-looking planning, limited institutionalised saving, minimal investment engagement and low coping confidence. The study is anchored in social cognitive theory and behavioural economics and interprets the gaps through risk perception, decision fatigue, present bias and emotional management, although no instrument measuring those psychological constructs is reported in the methodology.

## Problem and Motivation

The paper argues that sustainable saving behaviour is a central construct for household financial stability in uncertain economic environments, and that financial capability is more than technical competence: "Beyond technical competence, households' perceptions of risk, future uncertainty, and financial stress shape their motivation to save and allocate resources prudently" (Introduction). It cites Zafar (2026) for the claim that regular saving among low- and middle-income households contributes to resilience against income shocks, and Katnic et al. (2024) for the claim that community-level financial empowerment reduces structural vulnerability.

The stated gap is that "limited empirical evidence integrates sustainable saving behavior and financial capability within a unified framework focused specifically on community households' financial resilience" (Introduction). The paper argues that localised empirical assessment is needed to capture contextual determinants — social norms, perceived risks and coping strategies shaped by community environments — and that Yeboah (2025) established the necessity of linking financial capability metrics with resilience indicators for development planning and behavioural policy design. The study positions itself as supplying empirical evidence from community households on how behavioural and psychological drivers embedded in saving practices and financial capability collectively shape financial resilience.

## Method

**Design.** Quantitative descriptive research design; the authors state the label explicitly and justify it as appropriate for portraying characteristics of a population accurately and systematically without manipulating variables (METHODOLOGY 3.1). No causal or inferential component is claimed.
**Sample.** n = 300 respondents; unit of analysis is the adult household member who serves as primary financial decision-maker within the household (METHODOLOGY 3.2).
**Context.** geography: Municipality of Tago, Surigao del Sur, Philippines — a predominantly rural municipality with varying income stability, partial access to formal financial institutions and reliance on informal financial practices (METHODOLOGY 3.2, Limitations); population: adult primary household financial decision-makers selected across several barangays to capture variation in income patterns, livelihood sources and access to financial services; setting: community-based face-to-face survey administration within selected barangays, with localised (Cebuano) item wording.

Procedure as printed:

- Sampling is described as a "community-based sampling approach" that considered accessibility and willingness of participants while ensuring coverage across different areas of the municipality; no sampling frame, randomisation mechanism or response rate is reported (METHODOLOGY 3.3).
- The instrument is a structured survey questionnaire with Likert-scale items assessing four financial capability domains — budgeting, saving practices, debt management and investment behaviour — plus additional items on financial resilience covering preparedness for shocks, adaptability during hardship and recovery capacity; items were adapted from established financial capability and resilience frameworks and localised in language (METHODOLOGY 3.4).
- Administration was direct and in-person; participants were informed of purpose and gave voluntary consent; the researcher clarified items before completion to reduce item non-response among participants with varying literacy levels (METHODOLOGY 3.5).
- Sustainable saving behaviour was operationalised through regular saving habits, goal-oriented saving, emergency savings and use of secure saving mechanisms; financial resilience through shock absorption, adaptability, recovery speed, income diversification and access to support systems (METHODOLOGY 3.6).
- Ethics: voluntary participation, anonymity and confidentiality assured, no personally identifiable information recorded, neutral item wording given the sensitivity of financial information (METHODOLOGY 3.7).

Analytic approach: descriptive only. Domain means and an overall mean are reported for each of the four capability domains and for resilience; no reliability coefficient, factor analysis, significance test, correlation or regression is reported anywhere in the paper.

## Key Findings

- num: Financial capability overall mean is 2.76 (Moderately Agree), described as "partial capability development" with "significant asymmetry" (Table 1; Results).
- num: Debt management is the strongest domain at 3.86 (Agree), with the single highest item being capacity to repay and avoidance of high-interest loans at 4.30 (Strongly Agree) (Table 1).
- num: Budgeting overall is 2.35 (Disagree), although family involvement in budget preparation scores 3.90 (Agree) and depositing money into a bank or e-wallet scores 1.60 (Strongly Disagree) (Table 1).
- num: Saving overall is 2.44 (Disagree), with teaching saving values within the family at 4.16 (Agree) against 1.60 (Strongly Disagree) for keeping savings in secure and reliable financial institutions (Table 1).
- num: Investment practices overall is 2.37 (Disagree) and is described as "the weakest domain of financial capability" (Table 1; Results).
- num: Financial resilience overall mean is 1.98 (Disagree), with every one of the six resilience indicators falling in the Disagree or Strongly Disagree band (Table 2).
- num: The lowest resilience indicator is managing sudden, unexpected or multiple expenses without severe financial disruption at 1.60 (Strongly Disagree) (Table 2).
- The authors interpret the configuration as "defensive and risk-averse": strong debt discipline protects households from immediate collapse but does not build assets, and the absence of structured budgeting, protected saving and investment planning constrains adaptive capacity (Results; Conclusion).
- The paper attributes the gap between saving values and saving practice to behavioural barriers — present bias, decision fatigue in managing scarce resources, and emotional stress associated with financial uncertainty (Conclusion).
- The paper reports that households score low on seeking financial advice both when investing (2.20, Disagree) and when experiencing loan difficulties (2.30, Disagree), which it reads as informational and support-system gaps possibly linked to stigma or low trust in advisory services (Table 1; Results).
- Financial resilience is presented as contingent on balanced integration across planning, saving, borrowing and investing rather than on any single strong domain (Conclusion).

## Limitations and Gaps

Acknowledged by the authors (Limitations):

- Geographic scope is confined to a single predominantly rural municipality, so local economic conditions, access to financial institutions and community norms may shape behaviours and risk perceptions differently from urban or more financially integrated regions, and results may not be fully generalisable (Limitations).
- The quantitative descriptive design "did not establish causal relationships among behavioral practices, psychological factors, and resilience outcomes," so the study cannot determine how risk perception, decision fatigue or emotional stress influence financial decisions over time (Limitations).
- Reliance on self-reported data from household decision-makers introduces potential response bias; participants may overstate responsible behaviours or underreport financial stress due to recall limitations or social desirability (Limitations).
- The study "focused primarily on behavioral and capability indicators and did not directly measure psychological constructs such as financial anxiety, coping confidence, or perceived control, which may mediate resilience outcomes" (Limitations).
- External economic factors — inflation, employment instability, market access and exposure to environmental risks — were not incorporated (Limitations).
- The cross-sectional design captured behaviour at a single point in time, limiting observation of adaptation to shocks, seasonal income variability or policy change (Limitations).

Observed but not flagged by the authors:

- [unacknowledged] Objective 2 asks the study to "analyze the behavioral and psychological determinants (such as risk perception, financial planning orientation, self-control, and coping with financial stress) that influence sustainable saving behavior and financial capability," but no such determinants are measured anywhere in the instrument or reported in Table 1 or Table 2; the psychological material is interpretive commentary on the two descriptive tables and on cited literature, not a reported finding.
- [unacknowledged] The title and abstract use driver/shape language ("drivers," "shape the financial resilience") that the descriptive design, absent any significance test, correlation or regression, cannot support; no inferential statistic appears in the paper.
- [unacknowledged] No reliability or validity evidence is reported for the survey instrument — no Cronbach's alpha, no pilot, no factor structure — despite the instrument being the sole source of all reported numbers.
- [unacknowledged] The sampling procedure is described only as "community-based" with selection based on "accessibility and willingness of participants," which is a convenience logic; no sampling frame, barangay-level allocation, refusal count or response rate is given, so the representativeness of the 300 respondents within Tago is untestable.
- [unacknowledged] The Likert scale anchors are never printed; the numeric range consistent with the reported values (1.00–4.30) implies a 5-point scale, but the paper does not state the scale, the verbal anchors for each point, or the cut-offs used to assign "Agree" versus "Moderately Agree," so the adjectival ratings cannot be reproduced.
- [unacknowledged] The paper reports an "Over-all Mean" of 2.76 for financial capability across four domains whose overalls are 2.35, 2.44, 3.86 and 2.37; the reported figure cannot be reproduced from the printed domain means (the unweighted average of those four values is approximately 2.755, which is close but is not stated as the derivation), and no weighting rule is given.
- [unacknowledged] Barangay-level or subgroup breakdowns are absent; the 300 respondents are pooled, so no claim about variation across barangays, income classes or livelihood sources can be checked despite the sampling rationale being explicitly to capture such variation.
- [unacknowledged] Several cited works carry obvious printing or transcription defects — for example "(Pignatiello et al., 220)" in the psychological-factors section and "Similarly, and Wilson (2023)" in the design section — indicating at least one proofreading failure, and the reference list mixes an author-date entry with a bare "79." running number, so citation-level traceability is uneven.

## Definitions

- **Financial capability** — "the combination of awareness, skills, attitudes, and behaviors necessary for sound financial outcomes," extending beyond literacy and requiring consistent behavioural application in real-life contexts (Literature, Financial Capability Foundations, citing Fenton et al., 2022).
- **Sustainable saving behavior** — "consistent, long-term financial discipline aligned with future-oriented goals and adaptive coping strategies" (Literature, Sustainable Saving Behavior Patterns). Also described as "habitual allocation of income toward precautionary and developmental purposes" (Ouyang et al., 2025).
- **Financial resilience** — the capacity of households "to withstand, adapt to, and recover from economic disruptions while maintaining essential consumption" (Literature, Household Financial Resilience), and "the ability to absorb shocks while sustaining basic needs" (Liu et al., 2025).
- **Budgeting** — treated as a financial control mechanism that "aligns income flows with expenditure priorities" and reduces decision fatigue by creating routine financial scripts (Results, Budgeting as the Behavioral and Cognitive Foundation).
- **Debt management (defensive sustainability)** — prudent borrowing and repayment discipline; measured by necessity of borrowing, capacity to repay, avoidance of high-interest loans, on-time repayment, lender comparison and debt monitoring (Table 1; Results).
- **Investment practices** — described as "an advanced stage of financial capability formation requiring both knowledge and psychological readiness" (Results, citing Rahmiyami and Somodiharjo, 2025).
- **Decision fatigue** — "repeated budgeting and spending decisions can deplete cognitive resources, leading to impulsive purchases and reduced saving consistency" (Literature, Psychological Factors in Financial Decision-Making).
- **Present bias** — "leads households to prioritize immediate consumption over future security, while commitment-based products help mitigate impulsive spending tendencies" (Literature, Sustainable Saving Behavior Patterns).
- **Risk perception** — affects saving behaviour "by determining how households evaluate future uncertainties and potential income disruptions" (Literature, Psychological Factors in Financial Decision-Making).
- **Emergency savings** — precautionary funds that "improve resilience during economic shocks by providing both financial protection and psychological reassurance" (Results, citing Bagood et al., 2025).

## Remember This

- Single-municipality Philippine household survey (Tago, Surigao del Sur), n = 300 primary financial decision-makers, descriptive only, no inferential statistics.
- Capability is asymmetric: debt management 3.86 (Agree) against budgeting 2.35, saving 2.44 and investment 2.37 (all Disagree); overall capability 2.76 (Moderately Agree).
- Resilience is uniformly low: overall 1.98 (Disagree), with all six indicators between 1.60 and 2.35.
- The highest single item in the whole instrument is capacity to repay and avoidance of high-interest loans (4.30, Strongly Agree); the lowest is managing sudden or multiple expenses (1.60, Strongly Disagree).
- Objective 2's psychological determinants (risk perception, self-control, coping with financial stress) are discussed but never measured in the reported instrument.
- The paper's own conclusion is that resilience requires balanced integration of planning, saving, borrowing and investing, not one strong domain.
