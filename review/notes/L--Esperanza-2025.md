## Summary

A quantitative-descriptive correlational survey of 100 wage earners in Cebu City, Philippines, testing whether three perceived dimensions of digital lending — accessibility, usage, and quality — predict three debt-management strategies the authors label STOP (budget restraint), PAY (repayment behaviour), and CAUTION (informed borrowing). Data were collected through a structured 5-point Likert questionnaire, checked for reliability (Cronbach's alpha 0.80–0.86) and content validity (IOC = 0.92), and analysed in SPSS version 25 using descriptive statistics and three multiple linear regressions, one per debt-management strategy.

The descriptive results are favourable but cautious: accessibility is rated highest (weighted mean 3.83), quality next (3.57), and usage neutral (3.17), for an overall 3.52. Among the debt strategies, CAUTION scores highest (4.20), STOP next (3.96), PAY lowest (3.80). Wage earners report significant financial strain (weighted mean 3.46), led by the need to take a sideline job (3.74) and by having no personal savings (3.51).

The regression results are thin. Perceived quality is a significant positive predictor of PAY (B = 0.364, p = 0.021) and CAUTION (B = 0.379, p = 0.010). Usage is a significant negative predictor of STOP (B = -0.259, p = 0.007). Accessibility is not significant in any of the three models. The STOP model is not significant overall (F(3, 96) = 1.409, p = 0.245) and the PAY model is not significant overall either (F(3, 96) = 1.984, p = 0.121); only the CAUTION model reaches significance (F(3, 96) = 3.314, p = 0.023). Explained variance is 5.2%, 6.1% and 9.4% respectively. The paper concludes that digital lending alone is insufficient for responsible borrowing and proposes a Financial Inclusion Program built on digital debt literacy, transparency standards, and consumer protection.

## Problem and Motivation

The paper opens from the rapid spread of digital lending in emerging economies, which it says has "greatly transformed personal finance, providing more accessible options compared to traditional credit institutions, especially for underserved groups" (p. 111). For wage earners in developing countries, digital lending platforms have become essential channels for formal credit, overcoming limited documentation, banking exclusion, and geographic isolation (p. 111–112). Cebu City is chosen as the site because of high digital adoption, a strong labour market, and growing dependence of urban wage earners on digital finance (p. 112).

The motivation is that this expansion carries risk. The paper states that "increased accessibility to credit—when unaccompanied by sufficient financial capability—resulted in impulsive borrowing behavior, recurring indebtedness, and overreliance on short-term loans" (p. 112), citing Bhuvaneswari and Vinitha (2023) and Wang and Overby (2022). It adds that algorithmic lending models, while efficient, "often failed to disclose loan conditions in a user-friendly manner, thereby exacerbating financial distress among vulnerable consumers" (p. 112). The Financial Capability Framework is invoked to argue that access must be paired with decision-making capacity, risk evaluation, and borrower empowerment (p. 112).

Two theoretical anchors are used: the Technology Acceptance Model, for the proposition that perceived ease of use and usefulness drive adoption, especially among users with higher digital literacy (p. 112), and the Financial Capability Framework, for the proposition that access without capability is not genuinely inclusive (p. 112). The three independent variables — accessibility, usage, and perceived quality — were selected for conceptual alignment with both.

The stated gap is behavioural and structural: existing fintech models, in the authors' framing, do not transcend basic inclusion metrics. The study therefore introduces a Financial Inclusion Program anchored in STOP, PAY, and CAUTION as behavioural mediators between platform interaction and financial outcomes (p. 112), and frames its novelty as the integration of public–private collaboration, behavioural economics, and digital simulation tools tailored to the urban Filipino wage earner (p. 112).

## Method

**Design.** The paper states its own label: "This study employed a quantitative-descriptive correlational design to assess the relationship between digital lending and the debt management practices of wage earners in Cebu City" (Design, p. 115).
**Sample.** n = 100 wage earners; the unit of analysis is the individual wage earner residing and working in Cebu City.
**Context.** geography: Cebu City, an urbanized financial hub in the central Philippines; population: formally employed wage earners aged 21 to 60 with at least one prior experience using a digital lending platform, of whom 58% were female, 43% aged 26–35, and 39% earning PHP 10,001–20,000 monthly; setting: a cross-sectional structured survey of urban wage earners, analysed in SPSS version 25.

- Administer a structured questionnaire in four sections: demographic profile, perceptions of digital lending (accessibility, usage, quality), and responses to the STOP, PAY, and CAUTION debt-management constructs (Research Instrument, p. 115).
- Measure every variable with multiple indicators on a 5-point Likert scale from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5) (p. 115).
- Pilot the instrument on 20 respondents before full deployment and report Cronbach's alpha of 0.82 for Accessibility, 0.80 for Usage, 0.85 for Quality, 0.83 for STOP, 0.84 for PAY, and 0.86 for CAUTION (p. 115).
- Establish content validity through three experts in financial literacy, quantitative research, and fintech adoption, with an overall Index of Item-Objective Congruence of 0.92 against a cutoff of 0.80 (pp. 115–116).
- Support construct validity with inter-item correlation analysis, exploratory factor analysis of item loadings, and the Fornell-Larcker criterion, the authors noting this was done despite not adopting structural equation modelling (p. 116).
- Draw the sample by simple random sampling with a pre-screening questionnaire verifying three eligibility criteria: formal employment at the time of data collection, at least one prior experience using a digital lending platform, and age 21 to 60 (Respondents, p. 115).
- Justify n = 100 by reference to Cohen (1992) recommendations for correlational studies requiring moderate effect size detection and sufficient statistical power in regression analysis (p. 115).
- Analyse with descriptive statistics (frequencies, means, standard deviations) and multiple linear regression, interpreting results at a 0.05 significance level (Data Analysis, p. 116).
- Run three separate regressions, one for each dependent variable (STOP, PAY, CAUTION) against the same three predictors (accessibility, usage, quality).
- Secure ethical clearance from Cebu Technological University, obtain informed consent, anonymize responses with respondent codes, and comply with the Philippine Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Ethical Considerations, p. 116).
- Build the Financial Inclusion Program from the findings, structured around four objectives with strategies, persons involved, budgets, funding sources, time frames, and expected outcomes (Financial Inclusion Program Generated, p. 123).

## Key Findings

- num: Perceived quality of digital lending is a significant positive predictor of PAY (B = 0.364, p = 0.021) and of CAUTION (B = 0.379, p = 0.010), but not of STOP (B = 0.191, p = 0.183) (Tables 11–13, pp. 121–122).
- num: Usage of digital lending is a significant negative predictor of STOP (B = -0.259, p = 0.007), and not significant for PAY (B = -0.06, p = 0.553) or CAUTION (B = -0.071, p = 0.455).
- num: Accessibility is not a significant predictor in any of the three models: STOP B = 0.123 (p = 0.302), PAY B = -0.031 (p = 0.809), CAUTION B = 0.149 (p = 0.215).
- num: Only the CAUTION model is significant overall (F(3, 96) = 3.314, p = 0.023); STOP (p = 0.245) and PAY (p = 0.121) are not.
- num: Explained variance is R² = 0.052 for STOP, R² = 0.061 for PAY, and R² = 0.094 for CAUTION.
- num: Accessibility is rated highest among the digital lending dimensions (weighted mean 3.83, SD = 0.6672), followed by quality (3.57, SD = 0.5885) and usage (3.17, SD = 0.8846); overall 3.52 (SD = 0.7134) (Table 5, p. 118).
- num: Among debt-management methods, CAUTION scores highest (4.20, SD = 0.6465), then STOP (3.96, SD = 0.629), then PAY (3.8, SD = 0.6694); overall 3.99 (SD = 0.6483) (Table 9, p. 120).
- num: The most pressing reported challenge is getting a sideline job to earn extra income (M = 3.74), followed by working more hours (3.56), worry about regular expenses (3.53), and having no personal savings at all (3.51) (Table 10, p. 121).
- num: Emergency saving is the weakest STOP item (M = 3.01, SD = 1.1733) while future planning items are the strongest (M = 4.30) (Table 6, p. 119).
- num: Instrument reliability is high across constructs (alpha 0.80–0.86) and content validity is IOC = 0.92 against a cutoff of 0.80 (pp. 115–116).
- The authors interpret the usage–STOP result as a behavioural paradox in which "greater access might reduce financial restraint" (Abstract, p. 111) and conclude that "frequent reliance on digital credit may weaken budgeting discipline and increase debt exposure" (Conclusion, p. 123).
- The authors conclude that "digital lending alone is insufficient to ensure responsible borrowing" and that platform trustworthiness, transparency, and ethical conduct shape financial outcomes (p. 123).
- The paper reports that structural income inadequacies were more critical than behavioural gaps in its challenge data (p. 121).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The authors acknowledge that the cross-sectional design captures behaviours and perceptions at a single point in time, "which limits the ability to establish causality or track changes in financial behavior over time" (p. 124).
- The authors acknowledge that the sample was limited to 100 wage earners in an urban setting, potentially affecting how well the results apply to rural areas or other demographic groups with different access to digital financial services (p. 124).
- The authors acknowledge that reliance on self-reported data may introduce response bias, especially on questions about financial literacy, borrowing behaviour, and debt levels (p. 124).
- The authors acknowledge that the quantitative approach does not explore the deeper psychological or contextual factors that influence debt decisions, and they call for longitudinal or mixed-method designs and wider geographic coverage (p. 124).
- [unacknowledged] Two of the three regression models do not reach overall statistical significance — STOP at F(3, 96) = 1.409, p = 0.245 and PAY at F(3, 96) = 1.984, p = 0.121 (Tables 11–12, pp. 121–122) — yet the paper discusses their individual coefficients as established findings and H4 asserts that digital lending dimensions "significantly influence the STOP, PAY, and CAUTION methods of debt management" (p. 114).
- [unacknowledged] H5 states that perceived quality is "the strongest predictor of responsible debt management behavior" (p. 115), but in the STOP model the standardised beta for usage (-0.364) is larger in magnitude than that for quality (0.179), and quality is not significant for STOP at all (Table 11, p. 121).
- [unacknowledged] The stated eligibility criterion is age 21 to 60 (p. 115), but Table 1 reports an 18–25 age category containing 28 respondents (28.00%), so the reported sample contradicts the stated screening rule (Table 1, p. 116).
- [unacknowledged] The abstract states that "greater access might reduce financial restraint" (p. 111), but the significant negative predictor of STOP is usage, not accessibility; accessibility is non-significant for STOP (B = 0.123, p = 0.302) (Table 11, p. 121).
- [unacknowledged] Explained variance is 5.2%, 6.1% and 9.4% (Tables 11–13), so the great majority of variance in all three debt-management outcomes is unaccounted for by the three digital lending dimensions; the paper notes this as "low explanatory power" but still advances programmatic recommendations from the coefficients.
- [unacknowledged] No confidence intervals, effect sizes, or a priori power calculation are reported for any regression estimate, despite the sample-size justification citing Cohen (1992) (pp. 115, 121–122).
- [unacknowledged] The paper reports no sampling frame, no response rate, and no description of how the simple random sample of 100 was drawn from the Cebu City wage-earner population (Respondents, p. 115).
- [unacknowledged] No correction for multiple comparisons is applied across the nine predictor tests in Tables 11–13, three of which are reported as significant at p < 0.05 (pp. 121–122).
- [unacknowledged] The pilot test of 20 respondents is used to establish reliability, but no pilot-level statistics other than the alpha coefficients are reported (p. 115).
- [unacknowledged] The Financial Inclusion Program budget is given as "75,000" for two objectives and "Included in the total program cost" for a third, with no currency and no total stated (p. 123).
- [unacknowledged] Hypotheses H1, H2 and H3 are stated as tests of "a significant relationship" between each digital lending dimension and debt management, but no correlation analysis is reported for them; only the three regression models are presented (pp. 113, 121–122).
- [unacknowledged] The study is dated to Cebu City only and the authors do not test whether the instrument or the STOP/PAY/CAUTION constructs behave the same way outside an urban Philippine fintech context (p. 124).

## Definitions

- **Digital lending** — Credit extended through digital platforms; the paper describes these as offering "rapid processing, remote access, and minimal procedural friction" (p. 113).
- **STOP** — Budget restraint: budgeting and debt avoidance, measured by six items including avoiding borrowing to balance a budget and following a budget (p. 112; Table 6, p. 119).
- **PAY** — Repayment behaviour: timely repayment and commitment to obligations, measured by six items including repaying borrowed money as soon as possible (p. 112; Table 7, p. 119).
- **CAUTION** — Informed borrowing: critical evaluation of borrowing terms and risk-aware decision-making, measured by five items including researching interest rates and repayment plans (p. 112; Table 8, p. 120).
- **Technology Acceptance Model (TAM)** — The adoption theory the study is guided by, holding that perceived ease of use and usefulness are key factors influencing technology adoption (p. 112).
- **Financial Capability Framework** — The framework holding that financial access must be complemented by decision-making capacity, risk evaluation, and borrower empowerment to be genuinely inclusive (p. 112).
- **Financial Inclusion Program** — The study's proposed intervention, "a strategic initiative designed to enhance the financial resilience of wage earners through responsible digital lending practices, debt management education, and consumer protection" (p. 123).
- **Cronbach's alpha** — The internal-consistency statistic used to establish instrument reliability, reported as 0.82, 0.80, 0.85, 0.83, 0.84 and 0.86 across the six constructs (p. 115).
- **Index of Item-Objective Congruence (IOC)** — The content-validity index used, reported at 0.92 against an acceptable cutoff of 0.80 (pp. 115–116).
- **Fornell-Larcker criterion** — The convergent and discriminant validity check the authors report applying to their multi-item scales (p. 116).
- **BNPL (Buy Now, Pay Later)** — Instalment credit schemes discussed in the literature review as eroding repayment discipline and normalising indebtedness (p. 114).
- **Weighted mean categories** — For Table 10, "significant" refers to responses in the 3.41–4.20 range and "moderate" to 2.61–3.40 (p. 121).

## Remember This

- A survey of 100 wage earners in Cebu City, Philippines, testing accessibility, usage, and quality of digital lending against the STOP, PAY, and CAUTION debt-management strategies.
- Perceived quality predicts PAY (B = 0.364, p = 0.021) and CAUTION (B = 0.379, p = 0.010); usage negatively predicts STOP (B = -0.259, p = 0.007); accessibility predicts nothing.
- Only the CAUTION model is significant overall (p = 0.023); STOP (p = 0.245) and PAY (p = 0.121) are not, despite being discussed as findings.
- Explained variance is 5.2%, 6.1% and 9.4% — most variance in debt-management behaviour is unaccounted for.
- The paper's headline framing of "greater access might reduce financial restraint" does not match what was measured: the significant negative predictor was usage, not accessibility.
- The proposed Financial Inclusion Program targets strategic partnerships, financial literacy, financial empowerment, and consumer protection.
- The stated age screen is 21–60 but Table 1 reports a 28% share of respondents aged 18–25.
