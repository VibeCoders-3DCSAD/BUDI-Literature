# Causes of Salary Loan Dependency: Basis for Strengthening Financial Literacy Program

`L--Francisco-2026` — Francisco (2026), *International Journal of Multidisciplinary Educational Research and Innovation*

## Summary

Among 341 surveyed DepEd Masbate non-teaching personnel, salary loans recur for ordinary needs and existing debts, and respondents attribute the recurrence to financial illiteracy in budgeting, saving and debt management, to income insufficiency, and to easy workplace access to lenders.

## Problem and Motivation

Philippine public employees draw salary-deducted loans that repeat rather than resolve a shortfall, and the paper reports ATM-card pawning by DepEd personnel in Masbate despite a standing prohibition on accepting payroll cards as collateral (Introduction, pp. 706-707). The gap claimed is the absence of localized empirical evidence on salary loan dependency among non-teaching personnel of a provincial DepEd division, since existing work covers teachers, private-sector employees or national programmes (Local Literature, pp. 712-713).

## Method

**Design.** Descriptive quantitative survey design; Sec. "Research Design" states the study describes existing conditions and patterns rather than determining cause-and-effect relationships (p. 714).
**Sample.** n = 341 non-teaching personnel of the DepEd Masbate Province Division Office and its covered schools, drawn from a population of N = 1,651 regular non-teaching personnel, all of whom must have availed of at least one salary loan (Research Respondents, pp. 714-715).
**Context.** geography: Masbate Province, Philippines; population: permanent non-teaching DepEd personnel holding at least one salary loan; setting: DepEd Masbate Province Division Office and the schools it covers, administered by the Division Human Resource Management Office and school heads.

- Adopt a descriptive quantitative survey design chosen to describe loan-usage trends and quantify agreement with statements on financial literacy, obligations, spending behaviour and lending access (Research Design, p. 714).
- Take the population as all regular non-teaching personnel of the division office and covered schools; exclude contractual, job order and inactive staff, and require at least one prior salary loan (Research Respondents, p. 714).
- Compute the infinite-population requirement with Cochran's formula at Z = 1.96, p = q = 0.5 and e = 0.05, giving n0 = 384.16, then apply the finite population correction to N = 1,651 for a required 311.8, "at least 312", and raise the target to 341 for power and non-response (Research Respondents, p. 715).
- Verify the larger sample by reverse-engineering Cochran's formula at n = 341 to obtain a margin of error of approximately 4.73% (Justification for the Final Sample Size, p. 716).
- Screen the master list against the three inclusion criteria, number each eligible employee, and draw the sample with a computer-based random number generator, replacing refusals from the remaining list (Research Respondents, pp. 715-716).
- Group the drawn names by assignment location, coordinate distribution and retrieval with school heads and administrative officers, and hand out division-office copies on site (Research Respondents, p. 716).
- Build a structured questionnaire in sections — demographic profile; financial management practices and salary-loan experience; and knowledge of budgeting, saving, interest, debt management and financial planning — most items closed-ended on a Likert-type scale (Research Instrumentation, pp. 716-717).
- Have experts in finance, education and research methodology content-validate the questionnaire for clarity, relevance and alignment, and revise item wording (Research Instrumentation, p. 717).
- Secure permission from the division office, administer the questionnaire in print or online form, obtain informed consent, screen returned forms for completeness, and retain only properly accomplished questionnaires (Data Analysis, p. 717).
- Analyse categorical variables with frequency and percentage, Likert items with the mean and the standard deviation, demographic-to-loan associations with the chi-square test of independence at a significance level of 0.05, and financial literacy to loan-dependency indicators with Spearman rank correlation; state that all results will be presented in tabular form (Data Analysis, pp. 718-719).

## Software

- Microsoft Excel `RAND` function or an online randomizer tool (version not reported) — random selection of the 341 respondents

## Key Findings

- num: The sampling frame population is N = 1,651 regular non-teaching personnel, for which Cochran's formula at Z = 1.96, p = q = 0.5 and e = 0.05 gives n0 = 384.16 and, after the finite population correction, a required 311.8, stated as "at least 312" (Research Respondents, p. 715).
- num: The final sample is 341 respondents, raised above that requirement for greater power, precision and non-response, and its reverse-engineered margin of error is approximately 4.73% (Research Respondents, p. 715; Justification for the Final Sample Size, p. 716).
- num: The chi-square test of independence is set at a significance level of 0.05 (Data Analysis, p. 718).
- num: The recommended payroll savings scheme is a voluntary deduction of ₱200–₱500 per pay period (Recommendation 3, p. 723).
- Most respondents are in the 36-45 age group, married, earning ₱30,000 or below per month, and supporting several dependents, a profile the paper reads as mid-career household strain (Sec. 3.1, pp. 719-720).
- Respondents report multiple salary loan applications within the past three years, with many borrowing ₱50,000 or more and a notable number ₱100,000 or above, which the paper reads as a routine strategy and possible debt cycle rather than a one-time emergency (Sec. 3.2, p. 720).
- Reported loan purposes are daily living expenses, repayment of existing debts, and medical and education needs, and salary-deduction repayment is presented as a structural reinforcer that lowers take-home pay and prompts further borrowing, with delayed or missed payments reported (Sec. 3.2, p. 720).
- The leading stated cause is inadequate financial literacy in budgeting, saving and debt management, with respondents admitting difficulty creating a budget, managing priorities and preparing for emergencies (Sec. 3.3, p. 721).
- Income insufficiency is a second stated cause, with many strongly agreeing their salary does not meet household needs, and spending behaviour plus workplace access reinforce the pattern through convenient payroll-deduction lending and promotion among co-workers (Sec. 3.3, p. 721).
- Respondents strongly supported continuous literacy seminars, financial counselling, literacy in orientation and training, payroll savings schemes and stronger cooperatives, yet the paper reports no frequency, percentage, mean, standard deviation, chi-square or Spearman value for any of these claims (Sec. 3.4, pp. 721-722; Results, pp. 719-722).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The paper reports no statistical results at all: no frequency, percentage, weighted mean, standard deviation, chi-square value or Spearman coefficient appears anywhere, so none of its four research questions is answered with data and its own promise that "all statistical results were presented in tabular form" is unmet (Data Analysis, p. 719; Results, pp. 719-722).
- [unacknowledged] The title and the conclusions assert what "causes" dependency, but the design section states the study aims to describe conditions rather than determine cause-and-effect, and no instrument measures or tests any causal path (Research Design, p. 714; Conclusion, p. 722).
- [unacknowledged] The sampling frame requires at least one prior salary loan, yet the abstract sets out to examine prevalence and the reported population of 1,651 is the whole workforce: the size of the eligible borrower subset is never reported, so prevalence of loan use cannot be estimated from the sample as drawn (Abstract, p. 705; Research Respondents, p. 714).
- [unacknowledged] Questionnaires distributed and returned are never counted, so the 341 respondents cannot be reconciled against a response rate and the non-response that motivated raising the target cannot be checked (Research Respondents, pp. 715-716).
- [unacknowledged] Instrument quality rests on expert content validation alone: no pilot test, no item count, no reliability statistic and no statement of how many Likert points are used, so the promised weighted means cannot be reconstructed (Research Instrumentation, pp. 716-717).
- [unacknowledged] Several works cited in the body have no reference-list entry — Ajzen (1991), Sherraden (2013), Lusardi & Tufano (2015), Kim and Garman (2004), CFPB (2017), Madrona (2017), Carolino (2020), Pomroy (2021), CSC (2023), and the OECD/INFE items dated 2018 and 2020 — so the Theory of Planned Behavior, the CFPB financial-well-being claims and the Masbate ATM-pawning report cannot be traced to a retrievable source (pp. 706-707, 721, References pp. 724-728).
- [unacknowledged] Entries in the reference list carry identifiers that belong to different works: the Philippine Journal of Human Resource Studies entry and the Philippine Journal of Public Policy entry share one DOI, and entries for the Philippine Journal of Business Research, the Philippine Journal of Education, World Development, the Philippine Journal of Educational Administration and the Philippine Journal of Financial Studies link to unrelated journals or repositories (References, pp. 724-728).
- [unacknowledged] The paper has no limitations or ethical-review section; ethics appear only as an abstract sentence on voluntary participation, confidentiality and consent, with permission sought from the division office and no approving body named (Abstract, p. 705; Data Analysis, p. 717).
- [unacknowledged] It generalizes from one division office and its covered schools, all non-teaching, all borrowers, with no comparison to teachers, contractual staff or other divisions (Research Respondents, pp. 714-715).

## Definitions

- **Financial literacy** — the knowledge, skills and confidence necessary to manage financial resources effectively, particularly for budgeting, saving, investing and debt management (Review of Related Literature, p. 708).
- **Financial literacy, operational definition** — the ability to apply knowledge and skills in budgeting, saving and debt management, which the paper credits with promoting responsible borrowing and long-term financial stability (Introduction, p. 707).
- **Financial capability** — the practical application of financial knowledge in real-life financial situations (Review of Related Literature, p. 708).
- **Financial well-being** — a multidimensional concept reflecting both objective financial condition and a subjective assessment of security, satisfaction and perceived control over one's financial life (Review of Related Literature, p. 708).
- **Theory of Planned Behavior** — the framework holding that borrowing behaviour is shaped by attitudes toward loans, workplace norms and perceived control over financial decisions (Introduction, p. 707).
- **Theory of Resource Allocation** — the framework holding that employees allocate limited income across competing household needs, raising reliance on loans when demand exceeds resources (Introduction, p. 707).
- **Salary loan dependency** — used throughout as recurrent salary-loan availment for ordinary needs and existing debts; the paper never supplies a formal definition or measurement threshold (Research Questions, p. 708; Sec. 3.2, p. 720).

## Key Equations

- `n0 = Z² · p · q / e²` — Cochran's sample size for an infinite population at Z = 1.96, p = q = 0.5, e = 0.05.
- `n = n0 / (1 + (n0 − 1)/N)` — finite population correction applied to N = 1,651.
- `Mean = Σx / N` — average response across Likert items and respondents.
- `s = √[Σ(X − X̄)² / (N − 1)]` — sample standard deviation of response scores.
- `χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E` — chi-square independence statistic; significant when p < 0.05.

## Remember This

- Descriptive survey of 341 DepEd Masbate non-teaching personnel; all are prior salary-loan borrowers.
- Every result is narrative: no frequency, mean, standard deviation or test value is printed.
- Named causes: budgeting, saving and debt-management gaps; income insufficiency; normalized workplace lending.
- Sampling numbers are the paper's only quantitative output, ending at a 4.73% margin of error.
- Conclusions assert causes although the design explicitly disclaims causal inference.
