# Financial Literacy Practices on the Investment Decisions of Accounting Professionals in Makati City

## Summary

A quantitative correlational study of 80 accounting professionals in Makati City, Philippines, examining how three financial literacy practices — budgeting, saving and investing, and debt management — influence investment decisions across four parameters: risk tolerance, investment goals, cost-effectiveness, and asset allocation. Data were collected through a self-made survey questionnaire using a Four-point Likert scale and analysed with Pearson's Product Moment Correlation Coefficient. Budgeting (r=0.924, p=0.001), saving and investing (r=0.970, p<0.001), and debt management (r=0.919, p=0.001) each show a statistically significant "Very Strong Correlation" with investment decisions. Saving and investing emerges as the strongest correlate. The study finds that diversification is the most valued saving and investing practice (mean 3.90), refraining from new debts is the most valued debt management practice (mean 3.95), and evaluating investment performance and adjusting strategies is the most valued investment goal (mean 3.99). The authors conclude that financial literacy practices are positively associated with investment decisions and recommend routine evaluation of investment performance.

## Problem and Motivation

Financial literacy enables individuals to assess risks linked with investment options and make educated decisions (Abstract). Only one-third of adults globally demonstrate basic understanding of financial concepts, and billions lack skills to adapt to rapid shifts in the financial landscape (Review of Literature). The issue poses challenges for both developing and developed economies, with vulnerable populations — women, the economically disadvantaged, and those with limited education — particularly at risk due to lower financial literacy levels (Klapper et al., 2014). In the Philippines, the Bangko Sentral ng Pilipinas (2021) Financial Inclusion Survey found that nearly half of Filipino adults struggled to keep up with living expenses and worried about their finances, though the number of adults investing in finance rose to 36% in 2021 from 25% in 2019 (Results and Discussion). The study addresses a gap: while many studies have examined the importance of financial literacy, there has been limited exploration into the correlation between financial literacy and investment choices (Introduction). The objective is to identify the financial literacy practices adopted by accounting professionals and determine the level of influence of these practices on their investment decisions.

## Method

**Design.** Quantitative correlational research using convenient selection of participants; analysis via Pearson's Product Moment Correlation Coefficient (r), with data measured on interval scale from a Four-point Likert scale. The authors state no further formal design label.
**Sample.** n = 80 accounting professionals of selected firms in Makati City; unit of analysis is the individual accounting professional.
**Context.** geography: Makati City, Philippines; population: Accounting professionals of selected firms in Makati City, of any gender and age, provided they have capability to respond and are exposed to and currently involved in investing activities; setting: Survey questionnaire administered to accounting professionals, with no experimental manipulation.

The study employed a self-made survey questionnaire as its research instrument to gather data on demographics and the degree to which financial literacy practices influenced investment decisions. The questionnaire underwent rigorous testing for validity and consistency, with a pilot testing phase with selected participants. The data obtained underwent analysis using appropriate statistical methods. The questionnaire was organized based on specific subject areas. After resolving any identified issues, refined data collection instruments were ready for wider implementation. Subsequently, the researcher analyzed the coded data, exploring connections among various categories to interpret findings and draw meaningful conclusions.

Data reliability analysis (Table 2) produced Cronbach's alpha coefficients of .876 and .815 for Financial Literacy Practices and Investment Decisions respectively, exceeding the 0.70 threshold and both categorized as "Good." The Cronbach's alpha based on standardized items was .882 and .819 respectively. The Financial Literacy Practices variable had 24 items, and Investment Decisions had 32 items. The statistical examination confirmed that all variable item arrangements within the questionnaire satisfied the validity criteria of the study.

## Key Findings

- Budgeting as a financial literacy practice received a total average mean of 3.70, interpreted as "Great Extent" (Table 3). The highest-rated item was "I keep track of how much money I make and spend each month" (mean 3.93), and the lowest was "I ask for help when I need it with my money" (mean 3.49).
- Saving and investing received a total average mean of 3.77, "Great Extent" (Table 4). The highest-rated item was "I diversify my investment portfolio to manage risk and enhance potential returns" (mean 3.90), and the lowest was "I establish clear savings objectives and work towards achieving them" (mean 3.63).
- Debt management received a total average mean of 3.74, "Great Extent" (Table 5). The highest-rated item was "I refrain from acquiring new debts unless absolutely necessary" (mean 3.95), and the lowest was "I consistently make timely payments towards reducing my outstanding debts" (mean 3.54).
- Risk tolerance as an investment decision parameter received a total average mean of 3.84, "Great Extent" (Table 6). The highest-rated item was "I understand the risks associated with different investment options due to my financial education" (mean 3.98).
- Investment goals received a total average mean of 3.81, "Great Extent" (Table 7). The highest-rated item was "I evaluate investment performance and adjust strategies more effectively" (mean 3.99).
- Cost-effectiveness received a total average mean of 3.88, "Great Extent" (Table 8). The highest-rated item was "I efficiently evaluate investments with optimal returns and minimal expenses to maximize profitability" (mean 3.95).
- Asset allocation received a total average mean of 3.86, "Great Extent" (Table 9). The highest-rated item was "I consider the historical performance and trends of different asset classes when making allocation decisions" (mean 3.98).
- Budgeting correlates with investment decisions at r=0.924 (p=0.001), interpreted as "Very Strong Correlation" (Table 10).
- Saving and investing correlates with investment decisions at r=0.970 (p<0.001), interpreted as "Very Strong Correlation" (Table 11).
- Debt management correlates with investment decisions at r=0.919 (p=0.001), interpreted as "Very Strong Correlation" (Table 12).
- The demographic profile shows 67 female respondents (83.75%) and 13 male respondents (16.25%); 66 respondents (82.50%) were aged 25-34, and 14 (17.50%) were aged 35 and above; no respondents were 24 years old or below (Table 1).

## Limitations and Gaps

- The authors acknowledge that the study is limited to accounting professionals in Makati City and does not investigate how demographic variables like age, educational attainment, and income levels affect financial literacy practices and their influence on investment decisions; they explicitly call for future studies to address this gap (Conclusion, p. 101).
- The authors recommend considering other financial literacy practices to examine their influence on other indicators of investment decision, and propose that researchers opt for different research settings in future investigations (Conclusion, p. 101).
- The authors suggest examining the influence of digital financial tools and online investment platforms on investment decisions as a future research direction (Conclusion, p. 101).
- [unacknowledged] The study uses convenience sampling ("convenient selection of participants," Methodology), which limits generalizability to the broader population of accounting professionals.
- [unacknowledged] The sample is predominantly female (83.75%) and aged 25-34 (82.50%), so gender and age comparisons are not possible and findings may not generalize across demographic groups.
- [unacknowledged] The study relies on self-reported data from a self-made questionnaire, which may be subject to social desirability bias; the authors do not report any control for this.
- [unacknowledged] The four-point Likert scale constrains variance and precludes a neutral midpoint, which may affect the distribution of responses and the strength of correlations.
- [unacknowledged] The study does not report confidence intervals for the correlation coefficients, effect sizes beyond r, or any adjustment for multiple comparisons.
- [unacknowledged] The paper reports only total average means and individual item means; no standard deviations, standard errors, or distributional statistics are printed for any of the measures.
- [unacknowledged] The study is cross-sectional, so causality cannot be inferred from the correlations; the authors do not discuss this limitation.
- [unacknowledged] The study does not report response rate, non-response bias, or how the 80 respondents were selected beyond "convenient selection."
- [unacknowledged] The paper reports correlation coefficients but does not include scatterplots or tests of linearity, normality, or homoscedasticity that would validate the use of Pearson's r.
- [unacknowledged] The authors do not report whether the pilot testing phase resulted in any changes to the instrument or whether pilot data were excluded from the final analysis.

## Definitions

- **Financial literacy** — Having the expertise and abilities needed for informed and efficient money handling (Kumari, 2020); understanding basic ideas behind investing, handling money to grow wealth, and staying financially secure (Introduction).
- **Investment decision** — The process of effectively managing complex situations in investment scenarios; involves carefully selecting the most appropriate investment types beyond considering available resources (Introduction).
- **Budgeting** — A financial literacy practice involving tracking money made and spent, making a spending plan, checking the plan regularly, and changing spending based on findings (Table 3).
- **Saving and investing** — A financial literacy practice involving allocating earnings to savings, establishing savings objectives, exploring saving options, prioritizing emergency funds, diversifying investment portfolios, educating oneself about investments, seeking guidance from financial professionals, and monitoring investment performance (Table 4).
- **Debt management** — A financial literacy practice involving maintaining records of outstanding debts, devising repayment strategies, prioritizing high-interest debts, making timely payments, refraining from new debts, negotiating with creditors, curbing unnecessary spending, and celebrating milestones (Table 5).
- **Risk tolerance** — An investment decision parameter involving understanding how risk influences returns, evaluating ability to handle risk, feeling at ease with portfolio risk, understanding risks associated with different options, regularly reassessing comfort level, making confident choices despite uncertainties, staying informed about markets, and trusting financial literacy to guide prudent decisions (Table 6).
- **Investment goals** — An investment decision parameter involving making decisions with confidence, aligning goals with financial objectives, managing risks and diversifying efficiently, evaluating performance and adjusting strategies, adapting to market changes, prioritizing long-term planning, collaborating with financial professionals, and seeing improvements in financial well-being (Table 7).
- **Cost-effectiveness** — An investment decision parameter involving efficiently evaluating investments with optimal returns and minimal expenses, considering future cost implications, identifying and avoiding costly products, pursuing low-cost strategies, grasping tax and fee influences, applying budgeting methods, continuously educating oneself, and trusting financial literacy for prudent choices (Table 8).
- **Asset allocation** — An investment decision parameter involving spreading investments across asset classes, adapting allocation to financial objectives and market dynamics, considering historical performance and trends, prioritizing growth potential while balancing risk, regularly rebalancing, using financial knowledge to identify opportunities and risks, seeking ways to improve allocation strategy, and trusting financial literacy for sound decisions (Table 9).
- **Pearson's Product Moment Correlation Coefficient (r)** — A coefficient that illustrates the correlation between two variables, indicating whether the relationship is positive, negative, weak, strong, or nonexistent (Okwonu et al., 2020).
- **Cronbach's alpha** — A reliability coefficient; scores of .876 and .815 exceeded the 0.70 threshold and both categorized as "Good" (Table 2).

## Remember This

- The study surveys 80 accounting professionals in Makati City, Philippines, using a self-made questionnaire on a Four-point Likert scale.
- Budgeting, saving and investing, and debt management are the three financial literacy practices examined; all three correlate significantly with investment decisions (r=0.924, 0.970, and 0.919 respectively).
- Saving and investing has the strongest correlation (r=0.970) and the highest total average mean (3.77) among the three financial literacy practices.
- Diversification is the most valued saving and investing practice (mean 3.90), and refraining from new debts is the most valued debt management practice (mean 3.95).
- Evaluating investment performance and adjusting strategies is the most valued investment goal (mean 3.99).
- The sample is predominantly female (83.75%) and aged 25-34 (82.50%), and no respondents were 24 years old or below.
- The paper reports no confidence intervals, no standard deviations, and no effect sizes beyond Pearson's r.
- The authors acknowledge the need for future research on demographic variables, other financial literacy practices, different research settings, and digital financial tools.
